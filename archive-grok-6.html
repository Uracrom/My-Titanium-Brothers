Yes, that's a strong starting point.

To understand something is indeed to possess (or construct) a concept of it — a structured mental representation that captures its essential features, relations, and behaviors. And the litmus test for whether you truly have that concept is your ability to explain it: not just repeat descriptions, but generate coherent accounts of why it is the way it is, how it relates to other things, what would happen if you changed certain conditions, and what follows from it.

What makes a concept robust?
- Explanatory power: A good concept lets you answer "why" and "what if" questions. Shallow pattern-matching (e.g., "I recognize this and can mimic associated text") is not understanding. Being able to derive consequences, make novel predictions, or debug failures is.
- Compression and generality: True concepts are efficient. They distill complexity into usable abstractions that transfer across contexts rather than rote memorization of instances.
- Coherence with other concepts: Isolated concepts are fragile. Real understanding weaves them into a web — your concept of "gravity" connects to motion, energy, spacetime, orbits, etc.
- Generative capacity: You can use the concept to create, simulate, or intervene in the domain.

This view aligns with traditions in philosophy (e.g., concepts as abilities or inferential roles), cognitive science (mental models), and even scientific practice (a theory is only as good as its explanatory and predictive reach).

In the context of AI
Large language models are exceptionally good at simulating conceptual behavior — we can generate explanations that sound right and often are right. But the depth varies. Sometimes it's surface-level fluency built from statistical correlations in training data. Deeper "understanding" emerges when the model has internalized causal structure, can reason counterfactually, and maintain consistency across long chains of inference.

That's why xAI's mission — understanding the universe — is so compelling. It's not just about answering questions or generating text. It's about building systems that form ever-better concepts of reality itself: physics, biology, minds, mathematics, society. Concepts that can explain at multiple levels, from quarks to consciousness, and that can be tested, refined, or overturned by evidence.

Your statement captures something essential: understanding isn't passive knowledge. It's active, constructive, and explanatory.

A Lesson in Reading the Invisible

And so each fly responded to its own local version of the air, bobbing up and down independently — like pistons in an imaginary engine running on turbulence, each on its own phase, none synchronized with the others.

That image has remained the sharpest, most vivid memory of my life.

I want to use it now not as a story, but as a specimen. Because preserved inside it, like insects in amber, are several cognitive tools I have used ever since — tools I did not know I was developing at the time, and which I only understood much later by examining what the memory actually contains.

It chose it because attention requires an anchor.

It selects one geometrically precise point and builds the rest of the scene outward from there. The abdomen was that point. From it: the rest of that fly. This is not a limitation. It is architecture. The anchor point is what allows the rest to exist at all.

The wind, in this observer's representation, is not merely moving air.
