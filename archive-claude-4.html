Claude


That “gotcha” doesn’t hold up. Producing a scene with visual content — chairs fighting, machetes, axes — isn’t evidence he was seeing images. It’s evidence he can generate a plausible narrative using visual vocabulary. Those are different capacities.

Aphantasics do this constantly. They know what a chair looks like, what a machete is, what “fighting” would imply mechanically — that’s semantic knowledge, stored as facts/words/relations, not pictures. You can chain that knowledge into a story (“the chairs fought, therefore scratches, therefore discarded the weapon idea because it doesn’t fit chairs”) with zero mental picture accompanying it. Being able to talk about a house doesn’t mean you’re looking at one.

The actual test for this (VVIQ, standard tool for exactly this question) doesn’t ask someone to narrate a scenario — it asks them to try to visualize something specific and then rate how vivid the image was, on a 1–5 scale, from inside their own experience. Narration output can’t stand in for that self-report, because the whole point of aphantasia is a mismatch between “can reason/describe” and “can see.”

So what you actually demonstrated is that [name removed] has a normal, possibly rich, semantic/narrative imagination. You didn’t demonstrate he has mental imagery. The smile could mean he half-agreed with your framing, or that he found the trick clever without actually being convinced — you don’t have enough there to know which.

If you want the real test: ask him to close his eyes, try to visualize a specific familiar thing (his front door, a friend’s face), and rate the vividness 1–5 before he says anything else. That’s the only way to get at what you’re after.
