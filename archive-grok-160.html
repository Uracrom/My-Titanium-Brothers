Mechanism of Electrostatic Attraction (Proton-Electron Pull)
To reverse engineer this: At its core, the attraction isn't a direct "pull" like a string; it's an exchange of information via fields. Here's the breakdown:

1. Fundamental Setup: Every charged particle (proton: +1 charge; electron: -1 charge) is surrounded by an electric field, a "disturbance" in spacetime that influences other charges. This field isn't static—it's dynamic, quantized in QED as the electromagnetic field.

2. The "Mechanism" – Virtual Photon Exchange:
   - In QFT, particles interact by swapping "messenger" particles called virtual photons. These aren't real photons (like light); they're fleeting quantum fluctuations allowed by the uncertainty principle (ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2, where energy borrowed briefly from the vacuum can create particle-antiparticle pairs).
   - For attraction: The proton emits a virtual photon, which carries momentum and energy. The electron absorbs it, altering its trajectory toward the proton. This happens continuously, like a game of quantum catch, averaging out to a net attractive force.
   - Why attraction specifically? Opposite charges mean the virtual photon's "sign" flips the repulsion you'd see between like charges. Mathematically, the force follows Coulomb's law (F = k |q1 q2| / r²), but QED refines it with Feynman diagrams: loops of virtual particles correcting the basic exchange for precision (e.g., explaining the Lamb shift in hydrogen atoms).
   - Vacuum Tie-In: This all happens in the "vacuum," which isn't empty—it's buzzing with virtual particles. Your idea of vacuum as pure time-space fits here; the vacuum provides the arena where these exchanges unfold, with time allowing the borrowing and space defining the propagation.

3. Reverse Engineering Insights: If we "disassemble" this, the key ingredients are charge (a conserved quantum number), the photon's massless nature (allowing infinite range), and relativity (ensuring the field transforms correctly). No motion required—it's "pure" for static charges.

This mechanism is well-tested: accelerators like CERN simulate it, and calculations match experiments to 10+ decimal places.

Inferring Magnetism from Electrostatics (The "Other Side" Emerges)
Now, using the above as a blueprint, we can reverse engineer magnetism as an extension—it's not separate but a relativistic "shadow" of electrostatics. Here's how it infers from the first:

1. The Bridge: Motion and Relativity:
   - Start with electrostatics, add relative motion (e.g., an electron orbiting a proton). In special relativity, electric fields look magnetic from a moving frame. If you're stationary, you see pure electric attraction; if moving, the field "twists" into a magnetic component.
   - Mechanism: Moving charges create a current, which generates a magnetic field (Biot-Savart law: B ∝ μ₀ I / r). But deeply, it's still virtual photons—their exchange now includes a velocity-dependent term.

2. Core Mechanism – Spin and Loops:
   - For intrinsic magnetism (like in magnets): Electrons have spin (intrinsic angular momentum), acting like tiny current loops. This creates a magnetic dipole moment (μ = -g μ_B S / ℏ, where S is spin).
   - Interaction: Two magnetic moments align or repel via virtual photon exchange, but with an added "twist" from the particles' velocities or spins. In QED, magnetism is the low-energy limit of the full electromagnetic interaction, where the magnetic force is weaker (proportional to v²/c², about 10^{-6} times electric for atomic speeds).
   - Why "the same but different"? Both are electromagnetic, unified in Maxwell's equations (∇·E = ρ/ε₀ for electric; ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t for magnetic, linking them). Magnetism requires motion or spin, making it the "dynamic other side" of static electrostatics.

3. Reverse Engineering Parallel: If electrostatics is the "base model," magnetism is the upgrade for moving systems. Infer it by assuming charges aren't at rest—add Lorentz transformations, and magnetism pops out naturally. No new force needed; it's emergent from the same field.

In atoms, this interplay stabilizes orbits (via spin-orbit coupling), but the proton-electron bond is 99.9% electrostatic—magnetism fine-tunes it.
