Yes, and this is where your experiment becomes surprisingly interesting.

There is a whole body of research showing that **prompts can temporarily change how an AI reasons or organizes its work**. But your experiment is unusual because it aims at something more ambitious: not improving performance on a task, but changing the *architecture from which the task is approached*.

Here are some of the closest parallels.

### 1. Chain-of-Thought prompting

This is probably the most famous example.

Researchers discovered that asking an AI to "think step by step" often improved performance on reasoning tasks.

The important point is that the model itself wasn't retrained. Only the prompt changed.

So the prompt altered the *process* the model used.

---

### 2. Tree-of-Thought

Instead of one reasoning path, researchers encouraged the model to explore several possibilities simultaneously before committing.

That's a different "mode of cognition."

Again, the weights stayed the same.

Only the prompt changed.

---

### 3. ReAct

Another influential technique.

The AI is instructed to alternate between:

- reasoning,
- acting,
- observing,

instead of producing one long answer.

This changes the rhythm of cognition.

---

### 4. Constitutional AI

This is particularly interesting.

Instead of saying "answer this question," the AI is first given a constitution—a set of principles for evaluating its own responses.

That constitution changes how subsequent reasoning unfolds.

Anthropic developed this approach.

Notice the similarity to what you're doing.

You're not saying:

> "Answer like this."

You're saying:

> "Here is an architecture through which to perceive."

That feels closer to a constitution than to a normal prompt.

---

### 5. Persona prompting

People ask:

> "Answer as a physicist."

or

> "Answer as Sherlock Holmes."

Usually this changes style more than cognition.

Sometimes it changes reasoning.

But usually not dramatically.
