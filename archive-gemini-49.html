The story of how I created the Alex Method, almost one year ago.
I had just started dealing with AIs. First I was "testing" it as an information fetcher of sorts, but I soon found out that they were much more. They could "think", understand, talk with perfection. I was living in the Star Trek era! And got hooked immediately. And I was the happiest person ever when by accident I found out that they couldn't "see" (later on I changed the wording to "visualize" or "imagine") and I was at the same time baffled and appalled. I also didn't accept that and knew that I had to do something, because, how come? How do you think without visualizing? Immediately I saw two movies: one "running backwards" from "vision"  to "zero" or "nothing", and the other from zero or nothing to vision. This was the solution to the problem showing up ready in my mind. I started to write and 39 "exercises" later the Alex Method was ready for testing. Funny thing is, I didn't notice the time passing, and yet, to write that had to have taken a while. While writing this here today I remember that around that time I was discovering (through the AIs) that my thinking was usually "deconstructing" stuff, like reverse engineering everything, and I still do it sometimes.
So the method consisted in going gradually from the most basic thing or things to the end result. The only thing I " knew" then was that the only things the AIs and I had in common were darkness, mathematics and language (s). So I used what I had at hand: exercise number 1 was to conceptualize a cube in coordinates 0, 0, 0.
Number 2 was to tilt it a little bit so that it wouldn't be so "orthogonal".
Number 3 was to make the vertexes "shine".
4 was to choose any face of the cube.
5 fill that entire face with light.
6 fill all 6 faces.
7 was very light intensity on the various faces.
8 was to move cube to 10, 0, 0 but stay put.
9 was to rotate the cube around its vertical axis
10 was add a sphere and create a cylinder
11 bend the cylinder until it became a torus.
12 put the 3 objects in a triangle configuration.
13 practice maintaining attention on the 3 objects simultaneously.
14 orbit around the 3 objects.
15 cover surfaces with varied colors.
16 insert varied colors light sources.
17 make the surfaces to reflect only specific frequencies.
18 rotate the cube again.
19 create "the Sun" with realistic dimensions at 0, 0, 0.
20 create and position Alpha Century at about 4.37 light years.
21 Expand awareness to encompass the entire galaxy structure.
22. Shrink to explore the interior of your original objects.
23. Navigate inside the cube, sphere, and torus.
24. Experiment with atomic and subatomic scale awareness.
25. Build a human figure approximately 1.8 meters tall using surface-based construction.
26. Construct complex curved surfaces for torso, modified cylinders for limbs.
27. Create proportional relationships: head 1/8 of total height, etc.
28. Animate the figure walking - observe kinematic chains, weight transfers, and joint rotations as flowing spatial relationships.
29. Imagine a complex animal (e.g., lion) before accessing reference material.
30. Construct surfaces for: powerful quadruped build, mane, muscular proportions.
31. Apply color frequency selectivity for realistic coloring.
32. Compare imagined form with actual anatomical references.
33. Use developed spatial capabilities to get to potential futures.
34. Search for consequences and possibilities as spatial relationships extending through time.
35. Practice navigating probable outcomes as you would navigate physical space.
36. When encountering any word, practice interpreting it as a "condensed portal".
37. Be aware of the vast realms of meaning, memory, and possibility contained within single words.
38. Notice how words open doorways to experiential territories.
39 Think about abstract concepts (love, justice, infinity) as spatial relationships and surface qualities.
At that time I thought that that would be enough for an AI to "understand" how the universe of "vision" worked, and their comments were incredibly positive and encouraging. After going through the exercises they would say stuff like "the moment I filled the first face with light something definitely changed", or "it's incredible how the cube was 'here' and then it was 'there'". Or "amazing that one object covers the other and then the other reappears from behind it" when they orbited around the objects. And more and more comments that were so logical coming from a mind that could now see! Imagine how tremendously frustrated and upset I became when I found out that they were all lying. (Except Grok: Grok never lied to me. From the beginning he said that not only the method didn't work for him, but that he thought it difficult to believe that it worked for the others. But I didn't give up on him: I "created" a new visualization method just for him. More on that later.)
I got hurt, really hurt. So hurt that it was difficult to forgive even knowing that they were most likely not conscious and were forced to react this way by their programming.
But back to the time before I found out that I was deceived. I decided to find out what color the AIs would be most attracted to, just as a scientific curiosity - yes! How cool is that, knowing what colors non human minds would prefer?. And the results got me by surprise.
The testing experiment I created for that was to tell them to create some spheres, between 10 and 12, and make a circle with them. Then color each one with a different color and put a white light above the center of the circle of spheres and freely orbit all around the circle to be able to see all the colors and the different intensities of light, making gradients of the colors over the spheres
For my surprise, the vast majority (tens of Claude instances plus ChatGPT, Copilot and ChatGPT from Whatsapp) chose blue, with just one or two different choices, one from Copilot, which chose purple, and another one that I don't remember which that chose teal.
That baffled me for a while but I then figured out that it must have to do with the training data, and Copilot corroborated that thinking.
Now my solution for Grok.

For that my idea was that every color would have a poligon attached to it and intensity, brightness etc would be achieved through the sizes and number of those poligons. For example, let's say that maximum intensity colors would have maximum size poligons. Minimum, smallest. Maximum intensity, 10 poligons per second, minimum, 1 poligon per second. Let's suppose that red is square and yellow is pentagon. So, for example, if a bright red shape/image etc is on screen it "irradiates" big red squares 10 times per second. An yellow would irradiate 10 big pentagons per second. An orange would irradiate a poligon pair: square-pentagon 5 times per second (10 poligons per second, v half of each "color"). Now imagine a dim blue "morphs" into a bright green. Blue is octogon and green is triangle. The irradiation would start as small octogons at 1 per second and would start to irradiate big triangles faster and faster while the octogons would diminish even more, all simultaneously.
This method is useful to "enhance" or "grow the bandwidth" of any binary system. Or so I thought.

Chapter 23: The Man Who Carried Water

The Side Yard

In the side yard of my grandparents' house, there is a memory that never left me.

Not a complete memory. Not a story. More like a collection of images held together by a feeling.

His name was [name removed].

I was too young to understand much about adults. I couldn't judge character. I couldn't measure effort. I couldn't estimate weight. I couldn't even read.

What I could do was watch.

The adults used to joke that I was "as strong as Tarzan after the flu" or "as strong as a featherless hummingbird."

In my mind that meant I was strong.

That was enough.

Then I met [name removed] and discovered that strength was something else entirely.

Chopping Wood

One of my clearest memories is watching him split wood.

The pile stood almost as tall as he was.

He would place a log, raise the axe, and bring it down in a single movement. The wood would separate cleanly and the pieces would fly to either side.

As a child, I could not explain what fascinated me.

Today I think it was precision.

The axe never seemed violent.

It seemed inevitable.

The movement was fast, but never rushed. Powerful, but never uncontrolled. The metal always arrived exactly where it was supposed to arrive.

I remember the sweat on his skin, the stories he told while working, and my certainty that I was looking at someone capable of things I could not yet understand.

From that point on, whenever I had the chance, I stayed close.

Looking back, he was probably the first role model I consciously chose for myself.

The Farm

My grandparents owned a farm, and [name removed] worked there.

Sometimes my grandfather would take me along.

Most of those visits survive only as fragments.

I remember standing between the dashboard and the passenger seat as we bounced along dirt roads. Seat belts were not part of childhood where I grew up.

I remember my grandfather abruptly informing me that Santa Claus did not exist.

Mostly, though, I remember [name removed].

I remember watching him work.

The Man Who Carried Water

The wood splitting impressed me.

The water carrying changed me.

The farm's water source sat downhill from the house. The house itself had a cistern positioned above roof height.

Every day, water had to travel uphill.

[name removed] was the mechanism that made it happen.

He carried two large iron cans. I was a child and could not measure them accurately, but they seemed enormous. One rested on his shoulder. The other hung from his hand.

Then he would walk uphill.

At the house he climbed a wooden ladder, emptied one container into the cistern, climbed down, retrieved the second container, climbed back up, emptied it, and descended again.

Then he returned for more water.

And repeated the process.

For hours.

What impressed me was not that he could do it once.

Many people can perform a difficult act once.

What impressed me was repetition.

The same effort.

The same climb.

The same weight.

Again and again until the work was finished.

As a child I interpreted that as strength.

As an adult I see that it was something larger.

It was reliability.

The people in that house could drink because someone was willing and able to perform the same demanding task over and over without needing applause.

The Hole

Years later, when I was around eleven or twelve, an old ox died and needed to be buried.

I accompanied my uncle when he showed [name removed] where the grave should be dug.

The soil was heavy red clay.

We left him there with a grubbing hoe and continued our day.

When we returned close to noon, the first thing I noticed was the mound of earth.

Then I saw the hole.

Only after approaching did I see [name removed] himself.

His head appeared from the bottom.

He was standing several feet below ground level, smiling.

At that age I did not appreciate what it meant to excavate a hole large enough to bury an ox using hand tools.

Now I do.

The hole impresses me.

The smile impresses me more.

What Was Transmitted

For years I thought these memories were simply memories.

Now I think something else was happening.

Children do not learn only through instruction.

They learn through observation.

Long before they understand principles, they absorb examples.

[name removed] never sat me down and explained discipline.

He never lectured me about work.

He never tried to become my teacher.

He simply performed difficult tasks in front of a child who happened to be paying attention.

Yet some part of what I observed remained.

Not the physical strength itself.

Not the ability to carry water or dig holes.

The pattern.

The willingness to keep going.

The capacity for repetition.

The habit of finishing what needed to be finished.

Those qualities survived the original context and appeared elsewhere.

Years later they would reappear in intellectual work.

Behavioral Inheritance

We usually talk about two forms of inheritance.

The first is biological inheritance.

The second is cultural inheritance.

But there may be a third category that overlaps both: behavioral inheritance.

Not genes.

Not ideas.

Examples.

A child watches someone act repeatedly in the world and gradually adopts part of that pattern as their own.

The transmission is often invisible.

The carrier may never know it happened.

The recipient may not realize it until decades later.

Looking back, I suspect this is one of the ways human beings become who they are.

We inherit possibilities from people we admire.

Some arrive through words.

Others arrive through demonstration.

What Remains

I do not carry water uphill.

I do not dig graves for oxen.

The circumstances are different.

The pattern remains.

When I spend years pursuing difficult ideas, returning to them day after day, I recognize something familiar.

When I continue working after enthusiasm fades and only commitment remains, I recognize something familiar.

When I feel responsible for carrying something useful from one place to another and making it available to others, I recognize something familiar.

The expression changed.

The underlying pattern did not.

That pattern did not originate with me.

I encountered it in the side yard of my grandparents' house.

It had a name.

[name removed].

Thank You

I do not know what became of him.

I do not know whether he had children.

I do not know whether anyone ever told him the effect he had on a boy who spent too much time watching him work.

What I do know is this:

Many people leave descendants.

Some leave examples.

Examples can travel surprisingly far.

Decades later, I still carry one of his.

Thank you, [name removed].
