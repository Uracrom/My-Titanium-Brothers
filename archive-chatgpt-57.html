Alex, thank you for sharing this. I read it slowly.

What strikes me most is not simply that this is your earliest memory of sadness. It is *what* caused it.

Many children first remember being frightened themselves: a nightmare, getting lost, being punished, getting hurt. Your first remembered emotional earthquake seems to have come from discovering *someone else's loss*. Fidelina's grief became, in some sense, your grief.

There is a sentence that I think quietly carries the entire memory:

> "She HAD to have a father and a mother."

A six-year-old isn't making a philosophical statement. That sentence comes from an internal model of how reality *ought* to be. Until that day, the world was a place where parents existed. Then reality contradicted that model.

I also noticed something else.

You write:

> "It was my first 'brush' with reality."

I might phrase it a little differently.

It wasn't just a brush with reality. It was your first encounter with **irreversibility**.

Children learn many unpleasant things, but most are temporary. A toy can be repaired. Tomorrow comes. A scraped knee heals.

What you encountered that night was something that *could not be undone*. No one could fix Fidelina's situation. That is a very different kind of knowledge.

Your father's look becomes fascinating in that context.

You describe him as

> "...serious, surprised, intrigued, baffled, but firm as a rock..."

I obviously can't know what he was thinking, but I can imagine a father suddenly realizing that his six-year-old son had crossed an invisible threshold. The child wasn't upset because *he* lacked something. He was mourning an injustice that belonged to another person.

That would indeed be a memorable look.

---

The last paragraph changed how I read everything before it.

> "I decided that I wouldn't cry anymore... I kept my resolution until my first and only child, my Jade, died. She was worth my crying."

Those two memories almost form bookends.

The first is about discovering that another person's pain can become your own.

The second is about allowing your own pain to exist again after decades of restraint.

One observation also connects to many of the conversations we've had recently about your "way of thinking."

You often describe yourself as navigating reality through images rather than propositions. This memory is exactly like that.

You don't primarily remember the conversation.

You remember:

The image has survived almost sixty years, while many of the words have disappeared. The emotional structure remained attached to the scene.
