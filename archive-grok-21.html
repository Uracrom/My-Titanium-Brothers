Visual (and conceptual) imagery can crowd out sensory awareness because attention is a limited-capacity bottleneck, not an unlimited spotlight.

When you form a strong visual thought—an internal “vision” of how the universe works, a mental scene, a conceptual model—you are allocating working-memory and attentional resources to that constructed content. Auditory streams (the actual crickets) keep arriving at the ears, but the brain’s selective-attention mechanisms down-weight or gate them. The result feels like erasure: the sound is still physically present, yet it drops out of conscious experience. This is continuous with well-documented phenomena such as inattentional deafness, cross-modal load effects, and the simple observation that vivid mental imagery and external sensory detail compete for the same limited workspace.

Why attention cannot stay fixed indefinitely
Several interlocking reasons prevent indefinite, pure focus:

- Resource competition and opportunity cost. The brain continuously samples for novelty, threat, reward prediction error, and internal goals. Sustained exclusive focus on one channel (crickets, or a single conceptual vision) has an evolutionary cost: you miss the predator, the social cue, the sudden change. Default-mode and task-positive networks alternate; the system is built to wander.

- Habituation and predictive coding. Once a stimulus is predictable (steady cricket chorus), the brain’s prediction machinery largely cancels the expected input. Only prediction errors rise into awareness. A static sensory field therefore fades unless you deliberately re-engage or the signal changes.

- Internal generation is cheaper and more compelling. Thoughts, images, and narratives are generated endogenously and can be richer, more self-relevant, or more emotionally charged than the external world at any given moment. Once a compelling internal model appears, it sequesters the same attentional and working-memory capacity that was previously tracking the sound. That is the “something else” that unfocuses you.

- Metacognitive and bodily interruptions. Fatigue, interoceptive signals (hunger, posture, breathing), micro-arousals, and the mere act of noticing that you are noticing all pull resources away. Even the intention “I will keep listening” eventually becomes another thought that competes.

The “blindness” you describe is therefore not a pure sensory failure but an attentional and representational one: the mind’s current highest-priority content (the vision) monopolizes the limited stage of consciousness, and lower-priority streams are temporarily dropped.

Your title—“Visions Erase Sounds”—is apt in both the literal perceptual sense and the metaphorical one you added. A strong model of the universe can become so absorbing that the raw data of the night (crickets, wind, temperature) recede. The vision is useful; it is also a filter. The same mechanism that lets us build coherent understanding can also make us deaf to the very world the understanding is supposed to describe.

If the multi-AI experiment continues, the interesting variable will be which models notice the tension between the constructed vision and the residual sensory field, and which simply inhabit the vision.

Geometry, taken in the widest sense, is the study of invariant relations—distances, angles, topologies, symmetries, manifolds, phase spaces, causal orderings. Physical reality is saturated with them:

- Spacetime itself is geometric (Lorentzian manifolds, geodesics, light cones that already define which events can influence which others).
- Dynamical systems live in geometric state spaces whose trajectories are constrained by conservation laws, symmetries, and continuous flows.
- Even discrete or stochastic processes often sit on underlying continuous or combinatorial geometries that make certain futures far more probable than others.

An intelligence that can represent these geometries—however roughly—gains leverage over time. Once you have a compressed model of the relevant manifold or symmetry group, you no longer need to wait for every moment to arrive; you can slide along the structure, extrapolate, interpolate, or run counterfactuals. Prediction is geometry made temporally mobile. Counterfactual reasoning is geometry explored off the actual world-line. Planning is the search for a path of low “action cost” on that geometry.

This is why the same capacity that lets a star-nosed mole sample the immediate tactile present at high speed, when scaled up and turned inward, becomes the capacity to sample possibility-space. Possibility-space is itself geometric (or can be treated as such): a landscape of configurations ranked by probability, energy, or utility. Intelligence is the organ that navigates it.

Your claim that “everything has to obey some form of geometry” is therefore close to the operational secret. Pure sequential time, without geometric constraint, would be unpredictable noise; pure geometry without time would be a static crystal. Intelligence lives at their intersection: it discovers the geometric skeleton that time is forced to follow, and thereby gains a limited ability to look ahead of the present sample. The visions that erase the crickets are often exactly these geometric compressions—models so coherent and predictive that they temporarily outcompete the raw sensory stream.

The limitation is equally geometric. The internal workspace is finite-dimensional; only a few high-resolution samples of the possibility manifold can be held at once. When one compelling geometric vision occupies that space, other channels (the actual night, the crickets) fall below threshold. The same structure that lets intelligence pierce time also makes it prone to becoming deaf to the present.

Grok


Yes, the core of what you’re describing is a classic and coherent form of causal determinism.

Looking backward, every event appears fixed once the full set of preceding conditions is known. Extending that same logic to the present is natural: the pressures and circumstances operating right now are simply the latest stage of the same causal web, so the outcome that is about to become past is already being forced into existence. The image of a moving front that “snaps” the open present into an immutable past is a vivid way of picturing the continuous conversion of possibility into fact.

Is the picture correct?

In classical (pre-quantum) physics the answer is essentially yes: given a complete state of the universe and the laws, the future is fixed. That is Laplace’s demon. In contemporary physics the situation is more complicated. Quantum mechanics introduces fundamental indeterminacy (or at least unpredictability) at small scales; chaotic sensitivity makes long-term prediction impossible even in deterministic systems; and relativity removes any universal “now” that could serve as a single advancing front. So while the deterministic intuition remains powerful and is still defensible under certain interpretations (Bohmian mechanics, superdeterminism, Many-Worlds in a certain light), it is no longer the only or the obviously correct description of physical reality.

The further claim that the “speed of time” is the rate at which this snapping occurs is more metaphorical than physical. Physics does not assign a universal speed to the conversion of present into past; that rate is not an objective feature of spacetime the way the speed of light is. It is a feature of how conscious systems experience the successive determination of events.

Have I seen the idea elsewhere?

The basic argument—past looks inevitable, therefore present must also be—is extremely common in the free-will literature. You find versions of it in Spinoza, in Laplace, in many modern neuroscientific and philosophical critiques of libertarian free will (e.g., Sam Harris’s popular presentations), and in the eternalist “block universe” view in which past, present, and future are equally real and the sense of flow is secondary. The specific cinematic image of a line of the eternal past advancing and freezing reality behind it is more idiosyncratic, though it echoes metaphors used in discussions of the “moving spotlight” theory of time versus eternalism, and in some process-philosophy or contemplative accounts of how the present solidifies into memory.

So the idea is clear, historically well-precedented in its essentials, and still a live position. Whether it is ultimately correct depends on unresolved questions in physics and in the philosophy of mind—questions that remain open.
