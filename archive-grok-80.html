When we call something incomplete (a text, a speech, a thought fragment, a half-finished sentence), the label itself implies a background expectation of wholeness. We measure "incomplete" against an implicit complete model in our minds—some gestalt, some anticipated whole that the partial thing is falling short of. That's why the judgment feels intuitive: the mind is already holding a template (a closed circle, a finished thought, a coherent narrative) and noticing the gaps against it.

But here's the flip you seem to be circling: that "complete model" is not in the external thing (the text/speech itself). It's projected from inside. The incompleteness isn't an objective property of the words on the page or the sounds in the air—it's a relational signal between what arrived and what the perceiver was primed to receive or complete.

This ties straight into Gestalt psychology's law of closure (and related principles like good continuation or prägnanz): the brain hates open figures and automatically fills in missing parts to perceive wholeness. Show people a dashed circle or three dots in a triangle arrangement, and they "see" the full circle or triangle. The incomplete stimulus triggers the mind's drive to resolve it into a complete percept. In language or abstract thought, the same mechanism operates: an unfinished sentence, a vague idea, an ellipsis (...)—we rush to supply the rest from our internal model. The feeling of "incompleteness" is the tension of that unresolved closure drive.

So when you say "when we think that something is incomplete it means that we have a complete model in our minds," you're exactly right—for perceptual/cognitive acts. The external input is always partial; the completeness is supplied by the observer. A text or speech is never inherently "complete" in isolation—it's a prompt for reconstruction. What feels incomplete is really the mismatch between the prompt and our anticipatory model (expectations from context, grammar, prior knowledge, emotional tone, etc.).

But you add the crucial caveat: "But not when it's a text or speech." Or rather, the same mechanism applies, yet we often treat the incompleteness as belonging to the thing itself (the writing/speaking) rather than to our own projection. That's the subtle shift you're glimpsing: we externalize the gap.
