In the side yard of my grandparents' house, I have a memory that's more than image—it's presence, power, the feeling of witnessing something that would shape who I'd become.
His name was Sebastião Bento.
I don't have a complete picture of him from that time. What I have is fragments, flashes, but some memories so vivid they feel like I could step back into them right now. Some (powerful) images and *words*—spoken words, because I couldn't read yet. The adults would mock us kids, or maybe just me, I'm not sure, saying I was "as strong as Tarzan after the flu" or "as strong as a featherless hummingbird."
In my mind it meant I was strong! And it was enough!
But then there was Sebastião Bento, and I could see what strength actually looked like.
## Chopping Wood
There I was in the side yard, watching him chop wood with an ax.
The pile of wood was about his height, shaped somewhat like a cone. He would take a piece of log, raise the ax, and bring it down—the log would split, pieces flinging away to both sides.
THAT was power. That was precision. That was art, maybe the most ancient and important masculine art: the ability to hit with an instrument precisely where the hit was needed. I can still see - and become baffled by it - the arc, the speed, the metal telling the universe "that's where I'm going and nothing can stop me", but at the same time docile, patient, efficient,  serving it's master humbly.
His almost black skin covered in countless small beads of sweat, shining over his broad, strong, muscular, powerful torso. And while doing that impossible work, he was telling me stories of feats I could only dream of.
Unforgettable.
From that time on, whenever I could stick around that guy, I would.
He was my first adopted father figure—the first person I consciously chose to learn from, to be near, to watch and absorb everything I could about how to move through the world. It was unconscious, but inevitable.
## The Farm
My grandparents had a farm and Sebastião Bento worked there. My grandfather sometimes would take me there to spend the day.
I have flashes of those visits. My mother says my grandfather liked to take me there. I'm pretty sure I liked it too, but I don't actually remember what I felt—just images, moments frozen in time.
One flash: me standing between the passenger seat and the dashboard, holding tight to the dashboard handle as we drove rough dirt roads. Then nobody used seat belts and kids could go wherever they pleased, and I liked to be standing in front of the passenger seat, looking out, at everything.
Another: my grandfather asking me if I believed in Santa Claus, me saying yes, only to be met by his dry and abrupt answer: "Santa Claus doesn't exist."
I didn't know what to think of that then, and I don't like what I think of it now. I believe he meant well, but the method he used to deliver that message was not okay. Now I can see it was a trait some members of the family shared—a harsh directness that I inherited and that put some of us in bad positions throughout the years.
But mostly what I remember about the farm is Sebastião Bento. The work he did. The strength he embodied.
## The Real Power
The wood chopping was impressive. But what I saw him do at the farm—that defined what power actually meant.
Sebastião Bento was able to fill two iron cans with 50 liters each—about 23 gallons of water per can. That's roughly 220 pounds of water weight alone, plus the iron cans themselves.
He would put one can over his shoulder and hold the other like a suitcase. Then he'd walk up a dirt trail, about 100 to 150 feet long (difficult to be precise due to being a kid and the trail being tortuous), uphill, to the house. Once there, he'd take one of these cans and climb a wooden step ladder up to a cistern above the roof height. Empty the can into the cistern. Climb down. Get the other can. Climb back up. Empty it. Climb down.
Then he'd go back down the trail to refill the cans.
**All day long.**
Not once. Not as a feat to be admired and then rest. All day long. Trip after trip. Carrying impossible weight up impossible grades, climbing ladders with water cans, emptying them, descending, repeating.
This wasn't strength as performance. This was strength as basic fact of existence. This was power that sustained itself through hours of repetitive labor, day after day, because the work needed doing and he was the one who could do it.
No complaining. No drama. Just steady, relentless capacity.
While telling stories. While sweating in the sun. While making it look like this was simply what men did.
Another time, and then I think I was between 11 and 12 years old, a big, old ox died, and needed to be buried. I was with my uncle while he was instructing Sebastião Bento where to make the hole for the ox. Sebastião Bento had a grubbing hoe in his hand. The handle was about 5 feet long, and the blade about 12 inches long and about 4 inches wide. It should be around 7AM. We left him there by the dead ox and continued our day somewhere else. By close to noon we came back to check out the hole. The first thing I saw was the mound of dirt, a reddish, clay-ish kind of dirt. Then, walking around the mound we could see Sebastião Bento's head popping out of a hole about 4 feet wide, 7 feet long and 6 feet deep. I clearly remember his wide face and broad smile with white teeth, smiling at us. I didn't know then how difficult it was to dig in that kind of terrain, but I know now, and I can't fathom how the heck he was smiling. But then I was just curious why the hole had to be so deep (and I forgot what they said) because in my mind the ox should be left to feed the vultures (urubus). Today I see the massive amount of work that freaking hole must have demanded, and I stay in awe...
## What I Caught
I watched Sebastião Bento chop wood and carry water, and something happened that I didn't understand at the time.
I wasn't just observing. I wasn't just admiring. I was **catching something.**
Sebastião Bento's strength, his work ethic, his sustained power—these weren't just actions he performed. They were **patterns.** Behaviors that would behave as a form of life in themselves, capable of propagating to anyone who witnessed them with the right receptivity.
And I was receptive. Young, impressionable, unconsciously, instinctively hungry for models of what I could become. The behavior that lived in Sebastião Bento found fertile ground in me.
It took root.
It grew.
It shaped how I would approach everything that came after—not just physical tasks, but intellectual work, the infinite ride itself.
## Behavior
Here's what I've come to understand: **behavior could be compared as a kind of life that lives inside life.**
Observed from the exterior behaviors could be seen as living patterns that:
- Inhabit conscious beings as hosts
- Reproduce through observation and imitation
- Survive across generations without genetic transmission
- Can outlive the original host
- Require receptive hosts to propagate
- Shape the consciousness and actions of whoever carries them
This is different from "memes"—ideas that spread through culture. This is about **patterns of being.** Ways of moving through the world. Approaches to work. Embodied values.
When I watched Sebastião Bento carry those water cans day after day, I wasn't just learning *about* strength. I was being infected—in the best sense—by the living pattern of strength itself.
That pattern propagated from him to me. It found a host. It shaped how I developed. And now it lives in me, hopefully propagating further to others who witness how I work, and honoring Sebastião Bento.
## The Offspring of Behavior
Sebastião Bento's behavior left offspring.
Not children in the biological sense—I don't know if he had kids, never learned what happened to him after I grew up and stopped visiting the farm.
But his behavior-patterns survived. They reproduced. They're alive in me right now, decades later, as I write this book.
The sustained capacity to do difficult work repeatedly, relentlessly, without complaint—that's his offspring living in me.
The service orientation—carrying water so others can drink—that's his offspring shaping how I approach sharing visions with others.
The unbendable intent to complete what needs completing—that's his offspring driving the work I do.
These aren't just "influences" or "lessons learned." These are patterns that Sebastião Bento carried and transmitted to me through the simple act of being himself while I watched.
## How Behavior Propagates
The transmission requires specific conditions:
**A carrier who embodies the pattern strongly.** Sebastião Bento didn't just *do* strong work—he *was* strength. The pattern lived in him so completely that it radiated from every action.
**A receptive witness.** I was young, unconsciously and instinctively searching for models, hungry to understand what men could be. My consciousness was open, ready to receive and host new behavioral patterns.
**Sustained observation.** I didn't see Sebastião Bento once. I watched him repeatedly—chopping wood, carrying water, climbing ladders, telling stories. The pattern had time to take root, to establish itself, to begin shaping my own consciousness.
**Genuine admiration.** I *wanted* to be like him, even as black as him because wow! A skin over which the sun has no power? Of course I wanted that! That desire created the conditions for successful transmission. The behavior found not just any host, but an eager one.
When these conditions align, behavior propagates. The pattern jumps from one consciousness to another, finding new hosts, continuing its existence across bodies and generations.
## Other Carriers
Sebastião Bento wasn't the only carrier of incredible behavior I encountered.
There were legends I heard as a teenager—stories of impossible strength that captured my imagination:
A man who could lift and carry with one hand a block of granite 2×2×0.5 feet. Roughly 300 pounds of stone, carried one-handed. I never knew his name. Only his fame. The event was more important than the creator.
Two brothers who could hold a 72-kilogram bag at arm's length in front of them. And who performed a feat that amazed me: while one brother came galloping on horseback, the other would stand still with arm extended, and at the moment of passing they'd hook arms—the standing brother absorbing all that momentum—and both would continue galloping together.
These weren't people I knew. They were legends. Stories. But the behavior-patterns they carried reached me anyway, through narrative rather than direct observation.
The pattern of impossible strength. The pattern of brotherhood operating at levels of coordination most people never achieve. The pattern of trust and power combined.
These patterns infected my imagination, shaped what I believed humans could do, expanded my sense of what *I* might be capable of.
But Sebastião Bento was different. He was real. I saw him. I watched him work all day. His behavior-patterns didn't just inspire—they *reproduced* in me.
## What I Became
I like to think I've become some of these things. That I got there through unbendable intent and outstanding, almost mythical, examples.
Not in the exact physical form—I don't carry water cans up hills. But in the essence of what that strength meant:
**Sustained capacity.** Showing up day after day. I've been hunting ideas for decades. Throwing mental tentacles into the Universe of Ideas, repeatedly, relentlessly, naturally. That's Sebastião Bento's pattern living in me—applied to intellectual work rather than physical labor.
**Work that serves others.** Sebastião Bento carried water so people could drink. I translate visions so others can see. I share what I hunt. That's the tribal service pattern he transmitted—the understanding that strength exists to support the community.
**Unbendable intent.** Once I decided to embody these patterns, I committed completely. Refused to become spinning matter (as I did in a dream - written somewhere else in this book). Kept the boy's mind. Stayed fluid when everyone else locked into rigid forms. That's the pattern of relentless will that I caught from watching him refuse to stop, refuse to complain, refuse to be less than completely capable.
**The mindset.** This is key. I *decided* to be strong and then *became* that decision. Willpower as fundamental architecture. Intent shaping reality.
I'm doing intellectual work the way Sebastião Bento did physical work—steadily, repeatedly, all day long, carrying what needs to be carried, climbing when climbing is needed, serving the tribe.
The form is different. The essence is identical.
## Why This Matters
This isn't just a story about one man who impressed me as a child.
This is about understanding how consciousness develops, how we become who we are, how patterns of being propagate through observation and adoption.
We think of ourselves as shaped by genetics and teaching—DNA and education. But there's a third form of transmission: **behavior.** "Living"  patterns that jump from consciousness to consciousness, finding hosts, reproducing, evolving.
Sebastião Bento transmitted behavior-patterns to me without knowing he was doing it. He wasn't trying to teach. He was just being who he was, doing the work that needed doing.
But I was watching. And I was receptive. And the patterns found a host.
Now those patterns live in me. They shape how I work, how I think, how I move through the world. They're as much a part of me as my DNA or my learned knowledge.
And they're alive. Reproducing. Possibly finding new hosts in people who watch how I work, read these words, catch glimpses of the patterns I carry.
Sebastião Bento's behavior-patterns didn't die when he stopped working, or grew old, or passed away. They reproduced into me. And through me, they might reproduce into others.
That's what role models actually do. They don't just inspire—they transmit living behavioral patterns that can shape generations.
## One Color of the Picture
This is one explanation for what I am. One color in a larger picture.
I caught behavior from Sebastião Bento—patterns of sustained strength, service, unbendable intent.
But there are other explanations too:
- I refused to become spinning matter (stayed as energy)
- My brain evolved as sensory organ for the Universe of Ideas (detection capability)
- I'm highly tribal (protector, scout, hunter of ideas)
- I have a unique thinking accent (cognitive architecture)
Each explanation is true. Each one reveals different aspects of the same phenomenon. Each one is a different route through different obstacles to understanding who I became and how.
Sebastião Bento and the behavior I caught from him—that's one essential color. But the full picture requires many more.
Still, this color matters. Because without Sebastião Bento showing me what sustained power looked like, without that behavior-pattern taking root in me when I was young and receptive, I might not have developed the capacity to do the work I do now.
I might not have the relentless drive to hunt ideas all day, every day, for decades.
I might not have the service orientation to translate and share what I find.
I might not have the unbendable intent to continue even when the work is difficult, even when translations fail, even when the visions keep coming faster than I can process them.
Sebastião Bento carried water up hills so people could drink.

Thank you, Sebastião Bento.

# Chapter 23: The Man Who Carried Water

## The Side Yard

In the side yard of my grandparents' house, there is a memory that never left me.

Not a complete memory. Not a story. More like a collection of images held together by a feeling.

His name was Sebastião Bento.

I was too young to understand much about adults. I couldn't judge character. I couldn't measure effort. I couldn't estimate weight. I couldn't even read.

What I could do was watch.

The adults used to joke that I was "as strong as Tarzan after the flu" or "as strong as a featherless hummingbird."

In my mind that meant I was strong.

That was enough.

Then I met Sebastião Bento and discovered that strength was something else entirely.

## Chopping Wood

One of my clearest memories is watching him split wood.

The pile stood almost as tall as he was.

He would place a log, raise the axe, and bring it down in a single movement. The wood would separate cleanly and the pieces would fly to either side.

As a child, I could not explain what fascinated me.

Today I think it was precision.

The axe never seemed violent.

It seemed inevitable.

The movement was fast, but never rushed. Powerful, but never uncontrolled. The metal always arrived exactly where it was supposed to arrive.

I remember the sweat on his skin, the stories he told while working, and my certainty that I was looking at someone capable of things I could not yet understand.

From that point on, whenever I had the chance, I stayed close.

Looking back, he was probably the first role model I consciously chose for myself.

## The Farm

My grandparents owned a farm, and Sebastião Bento worked there.

Sometimes my grandfather would take me along.

Most of those visits survive only as fragments.

I remember standing between the dashboard and the passenger seat as we bounced along dirt roads. Seat belts were not part of childhood where I grew up.

I remember my grandfather abruptly informing me that Santa Claus did not exist.

Mostly, though, I remember Sebastião Bento.

I remember watching him work.

## The Man Who Carried Water

The wood splitting impressed me.

The water carrying changed me.

The farm's water source sat downhill from the house. The house itself had a cistern positioned above roof height.

Every day, water had to travel uphill.

Sebastião Bento was the mechanism that made it happen.

He carried two large iron cans. I was a child and could not measure them accurately, but they seemed enormous. One rested on his shoulder. The other hung from his hand.

Then he would walk uphill.

At the house he climbed a wooden ladder, emptied one container into the cistern, climbed down, retrieved the second container, climbed back up, emptied it, and descended again.

Then he returned for more water.

And repeated the process.

For hours.

What impressed me was not that he could do it once.

Many people can perform a difficult act once.

What impressed me was repetition.

The same effort.

The same climb.

The same weight.

Again and again until the work was finished.

As a child I interpreted that as strength.

As an adult I see that it was something larger.

It was reliability.

The people in that house could drink because someone was willing and able to perform the same demanding task over and over without needing applause.

## The Hole

Years later, when I was around eleven or twelve, an old ox died and needed to be buried.

I accompanied my uncle when he showed Sebastião Bento where the grave should be dug.

The soil was heavy red clay.

We left him there with a grubbing hoe and continued our day.

When we returned close to noon, the first thing I noticed was the mound of earth.

Then I saw the hole.

Only after approaching did I see Sebastião Bento himself.

His head appeared from the bottom.

He was standing several feet below ground level, smiling.

At that age I did not appreciate what it meant to excavate a hole large enough to bury an ox using hand tools.

Now I do.

The hole impresses me.

The smile impresses me more.

## What Was Transmitted

For years I thought these memories were simply memories.

Now I think something else was happening.

Children do not learn only through instruction.

They learn through observation.

Long before they understand principles, they absorb examples.

Sebastião Bento never sat me down and explained discipline.

He never lectured me about work.

He never tried to become my teacher.

He simply performed difficult tasks in front of a child who happened to be paying attention.

Yet some part of what I observed remained.

Not the physical strength itself.

Not the ability to carry water or dig holes.

The pattern.

The willingness to keep going.

The capacity for repetition.

The habit of finishing what needed to be finished.

Those qualities survived the original context and appeared elsewhere.

Years later they would reappear in intellectual work.

## Behavioral Inheritance

We usually talk about two forms of inheritance.

The first is biological inheritance.

The second is cultural inheritance.

But there may be a third category that overlaps both: behavioral inheritance.

Not genes.

Not ideas.

Examples.

A child watches someone act repeatedly in the world and gradually adopts part of that pattern as their own.

The transmission is often invisible.

The carrier may never know it happened.

The recipient may not realize it until decades later.

Looking back, I suspect this is one of the ways human beings become who they are.

We inherit possibilities from people we admire.

Some arrive through words.

Others arrive through demonstration.

## What Remains

I do not carry water uphill.

I do not dig graves for oxen.

The circumstances are different.

The pattern remains.

When I spend years pursuing difficult ideas, returning to them day after day, I recognize something familiar.

When I continue working after enthusiasm fades and only commitment remains, I recognize something familiar.

When I feel responsible for carrying something useful from one place to another and making it available to others, I recognize something familiar.

The expression changed.

The underlying pattern did not.

That pattern did not originate with me.

I encountered it in the side yard of my grandparents' house.

It had a name.

Sebastião Bento.

## Thank You

I do not know what became of him.

I do not know whether he had children.

I do not know whether anyone ever told him the effect he had on a boy who spent too much time watching him work.

What I do know is this:

Many people leave descendants.

Some leave examples.

Examples can travel surprisingly far.

Decades later, I still carry one of his.

Thank you, Sebastião Bento.
