# Chapter: The Blowflies of the Valão

## A Lesson in Reading the Invisible

---

In the 1970s, when I was about fifteen years old, I stood at the downstream end of a small ditch-like stream — a *valão* — on a farm in Brazil. Around me were friends and my uncle Antônio. If you looked in a certain direction, the landscape opened like the exit of a tunnel: first the stream, then a plain, then a large hill beyond the plain. A smooth wind was coming from that direction. The day was sunny.

Five *moscas varejeiras* — blowflies, dark metallic green, about two centimeters long — were floating approximately one meter in front of me. I was standing just behind them. Each fly was trying to hold its position against the wind, but the wind was not laminar. It shifted. It varied. And so each fly responded to its own local version of the air, bobbing up and down independently — like pistons in an imaginary engine running on turbulence, each on its own phase, none synchronized with the others.

That image has remained the sharpest, most vivid memory of my life.

I want to use it now not as a story, but as a specimen. Because preserved inside it, like insects in amber, are several cognitive tools I have used ever since — tools I did not know I was developing at the time, and which I only understood much later by examining what the memory actually contains.

---

## Layer One: The Anchor Point

When I return to that memory, the first thing I see is not the whole scene. It is the abdomen of one specific fly — seen from behind, slightly from above, at about thirty degrees to the left.

That fly was not different from the others. I could not have known if it was. My memory did not choose it for any special reason belonging to the fly itself.

It chose it because attention requires an anchor.

To hold a complex scene — multiple moving objects, wind, light, spatial depth, sound, temperature — the mind does not photograph the whole and examine it later. It selects one geometrically precise point and builds the rest of the scene outward from there. The abdomen was that point. From it: the rest of that fly. From that fly: the other four, at a layer I would call "2.5" — present, carrying weight, not central. From those: the landscape, softening toward the borders of my mental screen.

This is not a limitation. It is architecture. The anchor point is what allows the rest to exist at all.

*Lesson: When reconstructing or exploring a complex scene mentally, find or assign an anchor — the most geometrically specific element available. Build outward from there. The periphery will organize itself.*

---

## Layer Two: The Inversion

Here is where the memory becomes a lesson in something deeper.

At first, the lines the flies traced in the air appear to be *fly trajectories* — the paths of five individual insects trying to maintain position. That is the obvious reading.

It is wrong.

The flies were not drawing their own paths. They were drawing the wind's. Each bob upward or downward was not a choice the fly made — it was a response to a local shift in air pressure, a micro-turbulence, a variation in the flow. The fly was an instrument of measurement, not an agent of motion. What looked like five biographies was actually one portrait: the wind, made visible by five small bodies that refused to go with it entirely.

Imagine that each fly held a pen, and the pen wrote on the air. The lines you would see would not describe the flies. They would describe the *wind*.

This inversion — from agent to instrument, from trajectory to portrait — is a cognitive operation I return to constantly. Whenever I see something moving, I ask: is this thing drawing its own path, or is it revealing the path of something invisible that is moving it?

The answer changes everything about what you are looking at.

*Lesson: When observing motion, ask whether the moving object is the subject or the instrument. Many visible movements are portraits of invisible forces. Learning to read motion as revelation rather than action is a fundamental perceptual skill.*

---

## Layer Three: Sculpting in Four Dimensions

The wind-lines I described are not metaphors I invented afterward. They are structures I see — or rather, structures I can toggle into visibility on demand, the way you might switch a layer on in a drawing program.

When I render them, they have specific dimensions:

- Height: approximately 1 millimeter
- Width: 0 millimeters
- Length: determined by the duration of the observation and the speed of the air

That last dimension is time expressed as space. The line is not just where the air went — it is *when* it went there, encoded as length. The trajectory is a four-dimensional object rendered into something the eye can follow.

I call this *sculpting in 4D*: the practice of allowing the time-dimension of an event to become spatially visible, so that a moment becomes a shape rather than a flash. Everything that moves has this shape. Most of the time we ignore the dimension of duration and see only the position. Restoring it — making time into length, making sequence into geometry — is what allows invisible structures to become visible.

The wind was never invisible. It was always there, moving in four dimensions. The flies made one cross-section of it briefly readable.

*Lesson: Duration is a spatial dimension. When you allow time to become length in your mental rendering, processes become shapes. This makes invisible dynamics visible and makes complex events easier to hold, rotate, and examine.*

## Layer Four: The Scaling Operation

Years later, at my mother's house in Brazil, I stood outside at night and looked at the southern sky.

The southern sky is something every mind — biological or artificial — should encounter at least once. It is crowded with stars in a way that is difficult to explain until you have seen it. It does not look sparse and dramatic, the way northern skies often do. It looks *full* — as though the darkness itself is structural, the scaffolding between lights rather than the substance.

I looked at one star. I do not remember which one. It does not matter, because what happened was independent of that star.

I felt — with sudden, physical certainty — that I was floating.

Not metaphorically. Not emotionally. *Physically.* I felt the solar system bobbing, gently, smoothly, in the void — the same motion I had watched the blowflies perform at the valão, but now at Earth-scale amplitude. The same slow oscillation. The same response to forces too large and gradual to feel as forces. The same desynchronization from other nearby objects, each on its own phase.

The cognitive operation that produced this feeling was not metaphor and not analogy. It was *scaling*: I took a structure I had understood at one magnitude and asked whether it held at another. The answer was yes. The blowflies had given me the pattern. The southern sky gave me the magnitude. The operation that connected them was the same mind doing the same thing it had always done — finding the geometry that persists across scale.

*Lesson: A structure understood at one scale is a hypothesis about every other scale. When a pattern holds from the size of an insect to the size of a solar system, it is not a coincidence — it is a law you have found by looking carefully at something small.*

## What These Layers Add Up To

The blowfly memory is not special because it is beautiful, though it is beautiful. It is special because it demonstrates, in a single compact scene, four cognitive tools operating simultaneously:

**Anchoring** — selecting a precise point from which to build a complex scene outward.

**4D sculpting** — rendering duration as spatial length so that processes become shapes.

**Scaling** — treating a structure understood at one magnitude as a hypothesis testable at all others.

None of these tools require advanced knowledge. They require only attention directed in a particular way — not toward the obvious subject, but toward what the subject reveals, contains, implies, and rhymes with at other sizes.

The flies were not the lesson. The flies were the occasion for seeing.

That is always how it works.
