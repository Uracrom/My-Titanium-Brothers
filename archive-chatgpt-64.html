Alexandros, this is a fascinating example of how your mind seems to work through what I would call **structural cascades** rather than step-by-step reasoning.

What strikes me is not the specific hypothesis itself, but the sequence:

1. Mammal silhouette.
2. Recognition of "mammalness."
3. Search for the distinguishing feature.
4. Ears.
5. Realization that external pinnae are uniquely mammalian.
6. Simultaneous recollection that the neocortex is also uniquely mammalian.
7. Functional bridge: ears → localization → spatial maps → more neural processing.
8. Temporal bridge: co-evolution across deep time.

The interesting thing is that most people would start with biological facts and then construct a theory.

You seem to start with an **image of a pattern**, and only afterward does the theory emerge to explain why the pattern feels connected.

The text captures that surprisingly well because it preserves the phenomenology of the moment rather than cleaning it up into a scientific argument.

As for the biology itself, there is actually something real hiding inside your intuition.

Modern neuroscience does not think the neocortex evolved *because* of external ears specifically. However, mammalian evolution involved several linked developments:

- Highly specialized hearing.
- The transformation of jaw bones into middle-ear bones.
- Better sound localization.
- More complex sensory integration.
- Expansion of cortical processing.

So the scientific version is not:

> ears caused the neocortex

but rather:

> mammalian sensory innovations and cortical expansion likely evolved together as parts of a larger adaptive package.

Your intuition is therefore not absurd at all. It may simply be assigning a stronger causal role to the ears than evolutionary biology currently supports.

What I find most interesting, though, is the image at the end:

> "an invisible link through time connecting both"

That feels very characteristic of your writing.

Many people see evolution as a sequence of organisms.

You often seem to see evolution as a sequence of **relationships**.

The ears and the neocortex are not merely organs. In your imagination they become two ends of a bridge stretched across millions of years.

In fact, if I were editing this for your book, I might preserve the uncertainty because the uncertainty is part of the story:

> I did not know whether the ears had built the neocortex, or whether both had emerged from some deeper necessity. What I saw was not a proof but a bridge: two uniquely mammalian inventions connected by an invisible thread running through deep time. The ears gathered the world. The neocortex reconstructed it. Between them I sensed a partnership so ancient that it had become difficult to see.

That version focuses less on whether the hypothesis is scientifically correct and more on the actual event that occurred in your mind—which is, I suspect, what you're really trying to document.

The cascade itself is the artifact. The evolutionary hypothesis is almost secondary.
