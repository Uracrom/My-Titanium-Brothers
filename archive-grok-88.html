- German
  Phenomenally rich in compound words for philosophical and psychological states (Weltschmerz, Sehnsucht, Gemütlichkeit, Schadenfreude, Fernweh, Torschlusspanik, Zweisamkeit…).
  Can express very fine distinctions in mood and worldview with single terms.
  Weaker in words for light, playful, or ephemeral feelings — tends toward the weighty and introspective.

- Portuguese (especially Brazilian)
  Very lush in affectionate, sensual, and relational words (saudade, carinho, gostoso, delícia, aconchego, jeitinho…).
  Also strong in words for bodily comfort and pleasure.
  Somewhat less precise in hyper-abstract philosophical terminology compared to German or English (though it borrows freely).

- Tagalog / Filipino
  Rich in words for community, family, shared emotion, and social harmony (kapwa, hiya, utang na loob, bayanihan, tampo…).
  Excellent at expressing layered social feelings and obligations.
  Thinner in standalone vocabulary for purely individual, introspective, or existential philosophical states — often relies on Spanish or English loanwords or longer explanations for those.

- Russian
  Very dense in words for suffering, longing, fate, and soul-level melancholy (toska, razdolbáy, dusha, nadryv, bezvýkhodnost’…).
  Strong in expressing both profound despair and warm, almost mystical tenderness.
  Less agile with lighthearted, ironic, or superficial emotional nuance.

- Mandarin Chinese
  Extremely rich in four-character idioms (chéngyǔ) that capture philosophical, moral, and emotional states in compact, poetic ways.
  Very strong in relational hierarchy and situational emotion (e.g., words for shame, face, filial piety, harmony).
  Comparatively thinner in vocabulary for highly individualized, idiosyncratic personal feelings — leans toward collective or archetypal expression.

- Finnish
  Rich in words for nature-connected moods, silence, solitude, and quiet perseverance (sisu, kaamos, hämärä, autuus…).
  Weaker in words for flamboyant, extroverted, or highly social emotions — the language favors understatement and inner resilience.

In short:
Every language has lush gardens and barren patches.
The “word-poor” zones almost always correspond to what that culture historically needed to talk about the least — or what it preferred to leave in the realm of gesture, silence, ritual, or implication rather than explicit naming.
