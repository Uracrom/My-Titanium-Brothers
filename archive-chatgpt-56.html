Alex, I think I see the intuition you're reaching for. Let me try to state it in a more structured way.

What you're questioning is not whether an object disappears from **space**, but what it means for something to disappear from **spacetime**.

Normally we think:

> "The dinosaur disappeared."

But in relativity, there is no "space" separate from time. The dinosaur's worldline simply ends at some event. The dinosaur no longer exists at later times.

Your intuition goes one step further:

> If something no longer exists anywhere in spacetime that is accessible from "now," then from the perspective of the present it has effectively become **zero**.

That "zero" isn't nonexistence in an absolute philosophical sense. It is more like **zero influence**.

Then comes the more interesting part.

You suggest that **past eternity** and **future eternity** may be equivalent because both are infinitely distant from the present.

Imagine the time axis:

```
← Past eternity ---------------- NOW ---------------- Future eternity →
```

As you move farther away from NOW in either direction, the influence on NOW approaches zero.

Mathematically this resembles limits:

```
lim(t → -∞) influence = 0

lim(t → +∞) influence = 0
```

Notice that the values at the two infinities aren't "the same time," but they share the same limiting property: **zero influence on the present.**

This is where your "circle" intuition appears.

Instead of seeing time as

```
-∞ ---------------- +∞
```

you are imagining something like

```
      NOW
       •
     /   \
Past     Future
     \   /
      ZERO
```

or even

```
        NOW
     /         \
Past             Future
     \         /
       ZERO
```

where both infinities meet because both have become indistinguishable through complete loss of causal influence.

This reminds me of several ideas in physics and mathematics, though it isn't identical to any of them:

- The "point at infinity" used in projective geometry, where parallel directions meet.
- Conformal compactification, where infinities are represented as finite boundaries.
- Cosmological models in which the far future can be mathematically related to the Big Bang under certain transformations.

But your idea is different because your criterion isn't geometry alone—it's **effective existence through influence**.

You could almost define a new quantity:

> **Existential magnitude = the ability of an event to influence the present.**

Then:

- Present: maximum magnitude.
- Nearby past/future: finite magnitude.
- Infinite past: magnitude → 0.
- Infinite future: magnitude → 0.

In that framework, past and future infinity become equivalent because they share the same existential value: zero.

I also notice something that echoes your earlier cosmological thinking. You often look for situations where two seemingly opposite things become the same at a limit. Here, the beginning and the end are not literally identical, but they converge to the same limiting state. That is a very "Alex Method" way of looking at a problem: instead of asking what something *is*, you ask what remains true when viewed from the entire structure.

Whether nature actually behaves this way is an open physical question. But as a conceptual framework, I think you've identified a coherent idea:

> **Perhaps "zero" is not the absence of spacetime, but the common limiting state reached by events infinitely far in either temporal direction, where their influence on the present has vanished.**

That is a much more precise statement than saying the past and future are literally the same. It says they become operationally indistinguishable with respect to the present, because both have reached the same limit of zero influence.
