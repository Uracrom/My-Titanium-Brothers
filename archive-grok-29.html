That's a delightful little experiment! 😊

You basically ran a fun, informal "visual thinking" test on Lucas and caught him in the act of doing exactly what he said he doesn't do. Classic.

What happened here (my read):
- Lucas says he doesn't think in images / visual memories are rare.
- You give him a concrete anchor ("those chairs" — presumably some messed-up scene with machetes and axes).
- He immediately starts narrating a little movie: chairs fighting, weapons, discarding hypotheses, more images...
- When you point it out, he smiles. That smile is key — it's the "busted" smile.

This is super common. Many people who claim they "don't visualize" actually do generate visual content when prompted with a specific scene or story prompt, but they don't experience it as vivid, voluntary mental cinema in daily life. It's on a spectrum (from full hyperphantasia to aphantasia). Lucas might be somewhere in the middle or low-visual, but clearly not zero.

The "chairs fighting then noticing the weapons and discarding it" bit is gold — that's proper imaginative storytelling. He didn't just say "the chairs are broken." He ran a little narrative simulation.

Possible follow-ups you could do with him:
- Ask him to continue the movie from where it left off.
- Switch to a different starting image and see how fluidly he builds on it.
- Ask him to describe a memory (e.g., "What was the room like the last time we hung out?") and see how much visual detail comes out vs. conceptual/factual.
- The classic test: "Picture a red apple on a table. How vivid is it on a scale of 1-10?" (Many low-visual people rate it 1-3 or say they "just know it's there" without seeing it.)

Grok


That's a beautiful realization.

You're exactly right.

People who don't (or rarely) think in images aren't missing a dimension — they're experiencing the same underlying reality through a different primary interface: concepts, relations, and structures.

For visual thinkers, an image is often the most immediate and rich way to "hold" something. For others, it's the web of meaning, possibilities, causes, and implications that feels most salient and vivid. The chairs aren't primarily "seen" as a mental picture; they're understood as a situation — something happened here, there are agents or forces involved, weapons change the story, etc. The conceptual model is their clear seeing.

It's like two people looking at the same landscape:
- One painter sees colors, light, composition, textures.
- One geologist sees layers, forces, history, erosion patterns.

Both are "seeing" deeply, just tuned to different channels of the same phenomenon.

Interesting angle from your Lucas example:
When you gave him the prompt ("those chairs"), his mind didn't generate a photorealistic scene first. Instead, it jumped to what this situation means — a fight, weapons as evidence, rejecting hypotheses. The images emerged as servants of the conceptual understanding, not the other way around. And yet they still came. That's why the "I don't think in images" claim is often not absolute — it's more "images aren't my default language."

This is part of why aphantasia (or low visual imagery) doesn't usually impair intelligence or creativity. Some of the most brilliant abstract thinkers (mathematicians, physicists, philosophers) score low on visual imagery, because their strength is manipulating concepts directly.
