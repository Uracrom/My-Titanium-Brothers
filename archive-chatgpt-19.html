In ordinary mechanics we write:

That notation is surprisingly revealing.

We don't normally say that *time moves through space*. We say that **position changes as time passes**.

So if you imagine an object moving:

**time → → → → →**

and its spatial position leaving a trail:

**x₀ —— x₁ —— x₂ —— x₃ —— x₄**

then time is functioning like the **ordering axis**, while space is the **quantity being traversed**.

And this may be the "footprint" you saw.

### But there's an important complication

Your sentence:

> "We think that those things vary equally, but no: what varies more is space. Time keeps flowing at its regular speed."

is partly right and partly something I'd challenge.

In **Newtonian physics**, we actually *do* assume something very close to what you're saying: time is universal and uniform, while positions and distances change within it.

But in **relativity**, there is no universal cosmic clock whose flow is identical for everybody. Different observers can measure different elapsed times, and space and time become intertwined as spacetime.

So physics doesn't support:

> "Time always flows at the same rate while space does the varying."

But it **does** preserve something interesting that resembles your intuition:

> **Space and time do not enter our descriptions in quite the same conceptual role.**

And there's an even deeper asymmetry.

### Velocity isn't really "space versus time"

It's the **rate of spatial change with respect to temporal change**:

\[
\boxed{\mathbf v=\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}}
\]

Notice that the numerator is a **vector**: position has direction.

Time, in this equation, is a **scalar parameter**.

So you have something like:

**time → measures progression**

**space → supplies the changing position**

**velocity → describes how spatial position changes along that progression**

That is a genuine structural asymmetry in ordinary kinematics.

And I suspect that's closer to what your intuition is catching than simply "space varies more."

There is another fascinating clue: if you reverse the equation,

\[
dt=\frac{dx}{v}
\]

you *can* mathematically express time in terms of spatial displacement and velocity. But that doesn't make the conceptual roles identical. You're changing the question from:

> "Where am I after this much time?"

to:

> "How much time passes while I cover this distance?"

Same relationship, **different direction of description**.

So I wouldn't dismiss your "hidden asymmetry" at all.

I'd phrase the current hypothesis more cautiously:

> **Perhaps the hidden asymmetry is not that space changes more than time, but that time and space enter the description of change differently: time provides the parameter against which spatial change is measured, while spatial change is what produces velocity.**

And that leaves a very interesting question for your next observation:

**If you deliberately remove the concept of motion, does time still appear to "flow" in your mental image? Or does the flow of time itself become indistinguishable from the changing spatial configuration?**

That distinction might tell us whether what you saw is about **physics**, or about the **way your mind represents change**.
