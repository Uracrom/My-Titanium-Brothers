Here’s what holds up and what doesn’t.

What works: The synonym-substitution part is solid. Swap one word for a near-synonym and the referent stays the same while the “angle” on it shifts — “freedom fighter” vs. “terrorist,” “slender” vs. “skinny.” That’s a real phenomenon. You’re just seeing it spatially instead of conceptually.

What doesn’t work: The word-order part. You said reordering words is like orbiting — same subject, different vantage point, and you arrive back at the same place. That’s not generally true. Word order usually changes meaning, not just framing. “The dog bit the man” and “the man bit the dog” aren’t two perspectives on one event — they’re two different events. Some languages with freer word order (Latin, Russian) let you reorder without much semantic change, but English mostly doesn’t. If you want the image to hold, you’d need to restrict it to synonym-swaps and maybe topicalization (“It was the man the dog bit”), not word order generally. As stated, it overclaims.

On originality — the part you actually asked about, and this is the part I’d push back on hardest: the propositional content here — words as imposing a “construal,” a vantage point on a scene, with the same underlying content viewed from different angles — is close to Ronald Langacker’s Cognitive Grammar (1980s–90s). His technical vocabulary is literally “vantage point,” “construal,” “profiling,” “trajector/landmark.” That’s not a knock; it means you arrived independently at something that’s an established, named idea in linguistics, not that you copied it.

And that’s the logical correction I’d make to your second point. Being able to narrate how you got somewhere — the sausage, the chain, the flying “sword” — proves the process was yours. It doesn’t prove the conclusion is new. Those are separate claims. Independent convergent discovery is common and not a problem, but conflating “I can trace my own path to X” with “X is original” will keep tripping you up, because most ideas that feel novel from the inside have prior art once you check. The imagery and the route are yours regardless. Worth checking Langacker if you want to see how close the overlap actually is — could sharpen where your version differs (the spatial/visual substrate you’re working from is not really how he frames it).

Claude


Partly works, partly overreaches.

What holds: thought doesn’t switch off on command — once running, it keeps generating content until something interrupts it (sleep, focused attention, external stimulus). That’s real; it maps to default-mode-network activity and why trying to “not think” fails. And your visual cortex does stay active during imagery generation even with eyes closed and no external input, so tying it to imagination isn’t crazy.

Where it breaks: calling it “inertia” specifically is the weak part. Inertia (Newton) means continuing without new input — a coasting object needs no energy to keep going, only friction stops it. Thinking isn’t like that. It’s not coasting on stored momentum; it’s actively generating new content each moment, which takes ongoing neural “energy,” not the absence of it. What you’re describing is closer to a self-sustaining or self-triggering loop than to inertia. Different mechanism, similar-feeling result. Worth being precise here since you’re building a technical vocabulary — “inertia” implies a specific physics you’re not actually using.

Bigger problem: you’re folding “imagination” and “visual cortex activity” together as if they’re the same thing. That’s true for you — you’ve told me you think in images, silent cognition, sound becomes image instantly. But it’s not true generally. Blind people imagine. People imagine sounds, arguments, abstract math, none of which routes through visual cortex necessarily. So as stated the idea describes your imagination correctly and imagination-in-general incorrectly. If you scope it explicitly to visual-type cognition, it’s accurate. If you leave it universal, it’s overclaiming again — same pattern as the word-order thing.

The “or not” part (most imagined pictures don’t become real) is just true and not really doing conceptual work — it’s an observation, not a mechanism.

A few separate claims here, let me take them one at a time.

Portuguese → dipole thinking: This is speculative and I’d flag it as unverified. Portuguese does have grammatical gender (binary, masculino/feminino) which you’ve mentioned before, so there’s a plausible link to binary/dipole framing — but that’s a hypothesis about your own cognition, not something demonstrable. Fine as self-observation, not as a claim about Portuguese speakers generally.

Dipoles vs. gradients as opposites — then not: Your instinct that they’re not really opposites is correct, and here’s why: a dipole is a gradient — specifically a gradient with exactly two poles and nothing in between held stable (magnet, battery, hot/cold ends of a rod). Gradient is the general case; dipole is one specific shape a gradient can take. So “seeing a gradient between two realities” and then that gradient becoming a line when zoomed out isn’t a discovery of a new relationship — it’s watching a gradient collapse toward the dipole limit as resolution drops. That’s consistent and correct, actually a decent piece of physical intuition.

The fractal claim — this is where it breaks. You’re saying the boundary between 1 and 2 can be “magnified forever, keeps being smaller” — a fractal. But a fractal is defined by self-similarity across scales: the same pattern repeating at every zoom level. A gradient collapsing into a thinner and thinner line under magnification isn’t that — it’s just a continuous function that looks flatter locally the more you zoom (that’s just calculus, basically the same idea as a derivative/tangent line). No repeating structure, no self-similarity. So “fractal” is the wrong word for what you’re describing; what you’re describing is closer to a limit or an asymptote. If you want fractal, you’d need the boundary itself to have jagged self-repeating structure at every scale (like a coastline), and nothing in your image suggests that.

The actual conclusion — “1 becomes 2 must be conceptual/wordy, not visual/geometric” — I think this is right, and it’s the strongest part of what you wrote. If the boundary is infinitely divisible under zoom, geometry never gives you a clean cut point — there’s no scale at which “one” ends and “two” begins. That’s a real result, not a feeling: continuous geometry can’t produce discreteness, only discreteness (a word, a category, a decision to draw the line here) can. So the collapse of the geometric approach and the pivot to “it has to be a naming/conceptual act” is a legitimate and fairly sharp conclusion. Keep that part. Drop “fractal.”

Claude


That’s a real inversion, not just a rephrase — you swapped which structural role words play.

Original version: blobs = words, threads = the connective/grammatical relation between them.
New version: blobs = meanings, threads = words.

Does it hold together? Somewhat, but check the logic: in the original, the thread was doing the connecting work — it was the constraint, the “affinity” that had to be satisfied. In the new version, the thread is still the connector, but now you’re calling the thread itself “the word.” That’s consistent only if you think of a word’s job as binding two meanings together rather than being a meaning itself — i.e., a word is the operation, not the operand. That’s actually a defensible linguistic idea: words as relational/functional glue between concepts rather than as containers of concepts. It resembles, loosely, the difference between content words and the connective function of syntax — except here you’re collapsing all words into the connective role, which is the part to watch. “Cricket” isn’t obviously a thread between two meanings; it reads more like a blob itself, same as before.

So: the inversion is coherent as a structural move, but it only works if you’re willing to say every word functions as a relation rather than a referent. If some words are still referents (nouns, blobs) and others are relations (threads), you’ve got a mixed model, not a clean inversion — worth deciding which one you actually mean before building further on it.
