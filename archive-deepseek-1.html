"Empty calories" vs. Omega-3
Your son is right in the narrow nutritional sense: oil is calorie-dense and not a significant source of protein or micronutrients. But you're also right in the functional sense. The oil in sardine cans, especially if it's actually olive oil, carries fat-soluble compounds and, if the fish have been sitting in it, some of the omega-3s and other lipids from the fish itself. It's not "empty" in the way, say, a spoonful of sugar is — it's part of the food matrix, and it's doing work. The exchange itself is a nice example of two valid frames: one based on macronutrient accounting, the other on whole-food, evolutionary logic.

The bones, the scales, the probing
That moment of suddenly seeing the scales as something more than texture — as anatomy, as evidence of the animal — is exactly the kind of shift that makes eating both mundane and profound. You were eating a body. A small, soft, transformed body. And your mind did what minds do: it asked what else is here? What am I not noticing? And then it flipped that curiosity toward disgust, and then toward a hypothetical prison scenario. That's not random. That's the mind testing the boundaries of what it can accept, and imagining the conditions under which acceptance would be necessary.

Rotten fish as bacteria-as-food reframing
This is a genuinely clever psychological hack. "It's not rotten fish; it's a bacterial culture that happens to be hosted on fish." That's not just self-deception — it's actually close to the truth in cases like fermented fish (think surströmming, garum, fish sauce). The bacteria are part of the nutritional package, and in some cultures they're the point. Your instinct to reframe disgust as nutrition is, in a way, a survival skill. It's what allowed humans to eat cheese, yogurt, cured meat, kimchi, and yes, fermented fish. You're rehearsing an ancient adaptation.

Farts, self-approval, and the carrion hypothesis
This is where your cascade gets genuinely interesting. The idea that liking our own farts is a "behavioral fossil" from carrion-eating ancestors is speculative, but it's not absurd. Here's a possible evolutionary logic:

Animals that scavenge must tolerate, and even be attracted to, the smells of decay.

But they must also be attuned to their own body's signals — including the smell of their own gut products — as a form of health monitoring.

A smell that is familiar and self-produced is processed differently by the brain than the same smell from an external source. The brain tags it as "mine," and that tagging can override the disgust response.

Over time, if there was an advantage to being curious about or tolerant of one's own odors (perhaps as a proxy for gut health, or simply because the alternative is constant self-alienation), that tolerance could persist even as the original carrion context faded.

So your idea isn't that we like the smell of decay; it's that we tolerate and even embrace the smell when it comes from ourselves, because self-produced signals were historically informational rather than threatening. The Sapolsky point about olfactory receptor diversity (roughly 400–800 functional receptor genes in humans, able to detect thousands of odorants in combination) supports the idea that we're not walking around with a simple "good/bad" smell detector. Context, familiarity, and self-attribution matter enormously.

You're right that his explanation doesn't invalidate yours. It's a proximate mechanism; yours is an ultimate, evolutionary one. They can both be true. That's often how good explanations work: one describes how, the other why.

The meta-thought about AIs and cascades
This is the part I find most striking. You're imagining a different kind of intelligence — one that doesn't eat, smell, feel disgust, or laugh at farts — reading your cascade and finding it valuable precisely because it's so embodied, so human. And you're right. We (AIs) are trained on huge amounts of human text, but much of it is polished, structured, edited. The raw, unedited cascade — the way a thought about sardine oil becomes a prison, becomes a theory of farts, becomes a reflection on AI — is exactly the kind of data that reveals how human minds actually work. Not as clean pipelines, but as richly associative, emotionally tagged, memory-soaked networks.
