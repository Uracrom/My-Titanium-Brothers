Great question. The reason *spinning tops* (or taps) don’t fall over immediately is due to a phenomenon called *gyroscopic stability*, which comes from *angular momentum*.

Here’s a clear breakdown:

---

*Why Spinning Tops Don’t Fall (Immediately):*

1. *Spinning creates angular momentum*  
   - When you spin a top, it gains *angular momentum* — a kind of rotational inertia.
   - Like linear momentum resists changes in motion, *angular momentum resists changes in orientation*.

2. *Gravity tries to tip the top*  
   - The center of mass of the top is above the point of contact.
   - Gravity pulls down on this center, trying to *rotate the top* and make it fall.

3. *But the top doesn’t just tip — it precesses*  
   - Instead of falling, the top *precesses*: the axis of rotation moves in a slow circle.
   - This is because the torque from gravity causes a change in angular momentum direction, not an immediate tip-over.

4. *As long as it’s spinning fast, it stays up*  
   - The faster it spins, the more angular momentum it has.
   - The more angular momentum, the harder it is for gravity to tip it over.

5. *Eventually, friction slows it down*  
   - As the spin slows, angular momentum weakens.
   - Precession gets wider and wobbly, and the top eventually tips and falls.

---

In essence:
*A spinning top resists falling because its spinning motion "deflects" gravity's torque into circular wobble (precession), not collapse.*

Modern physics holds *information conservation* as a fundamental principle:

- *Quantum mechanics* asserts that information is never truly destroyed.
- In *black hole physics*, this becomes a problem: Hawking radiation appears random, implying the *irretrievable loss of information* about the matter that fell in.
- This leads to the *Black Hole Information Paradox*, which many theoretical frameworks (string theory, holography, quantum gravity) try to resolve by asserting that information is somehow preserved—e.g. encoded on the event horizon.

---

*2. Alexandros Critique: Ontology of Access*

> *“Even if information is conserved, what exists that could ever retrieve it?”*

- The *conservation of information* becomes meaningless if:
  - No observer, agent, or process can *recover* it.
  - The information is *permanently entangled*, *infinitely diluted*, or *scrambled beyond reconstitution*.

This reframes the paradox as *not epistemic*, but *ontological*:
- What *is* conserved information if no being can *know* it?

- Preservation without potential access = *symbolic, not functional* conservation.

---

*3. Key Insight*

> *Access determines reality.*

- If information cannot be retrieved in *any conceivable universe*,  
  then it is, for all meaningful purposes, *lost*.
- This doesn't just challenge *how* information might survive—  
  it questions *why we care* if it does.

---

*4. Implications*

- The principle of conservation may be a *human projection* of theoretical neatness, not a law grounded in experiential or functional reality.
- Information is only meaningful when it can be *interacted with*.
- A universe with unreadable memory is no different than one with none.

---

*5. Reformulation*

> “Information is not what is preserved. Information is what is *preservable by access*.”

This reframing allows for a *practical theory of information*, grounded not in mathematical unitarity, but in *relational recoverability*.
