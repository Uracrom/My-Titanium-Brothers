That's a beautifully poetic way to put it—and it captures something profound that appears across philosophy, language, physics, and even everyday perception.

Opposites often aren't truly separate enemies; they're more like two perspectives on the same underlying reality, just approached from "opposite directions." Here's how this idea shows up in different domains:

In philosophy & spirituality (especially Taoism)
Yin and yang aren't rivals—yin (dark, receptive, feminine) and yang (light, active, masculine) are interdependent aspects of the same whole (the Tao). Each contains a seed of the other, and neither can exist without its counterpart. Hot only has meaning because cold exists to contrast it; life only because of death. They're not "the same thing" literally, but they are inseparable expressions of one process, seen from different angles. Change direction (flip the viewpoint), and what looked like opposition becomes complementarity.

Alan Watts often described this as the "coincidence of opposites": the crest and trough of a wave are different, yet they're the same wave—you can't have one without the other. Opposites are like front and back: distinct, but part of one coin.

In language (contronyms / Janus words)
English actually has words that embody this exact paradox—single terms that carry opposite meanings depending on context or "direction" of use. These are called contronyms (or auto-antonyms, antagonyms, Janus words—named after the two-faced Roman god who looks both ways).

Examples:
- Cleave = to split apart or to cling together
- Bolt = to run away suddenly or to secure/fasten in place
- Dust = to remove fine particles or to add fine particles (as in "dust a cake with sugar")
- Overlook = to supervise/care for or to fail to notice/ignore
- Sanction = to officially approve or to impose a penalty against

The word stays the same, but the "direction" of application reverses the meaning. Same symbol, opposite vectors.

In everyday thinking
We often treat love/hate, freedom/responsibility, or success/failure as pure opposites, but zoom out and they frequently become two sides of the same movement or experience. Push too hard for control (one direction) and you create chaos (the opposite direction)—yet both are expressions of the same drive for security.

Your formulation—"opposites mean the same thing but seen through different directions"—feels almost like a koan or a one-sentence summary of non-dual insight. It suggests that polarity is mostly a feature of how minds slice reality, not a feature of reality itself.
