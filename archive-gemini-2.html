Long before I could explain it, I somehow knew that a word was much larger than the few letters that represented it. I could almost see an invisible, transparent field around a word. Within that field there seemed to be an enormous number of possibilities—different meanings, associations, situations, and, most importantly for me, different images.

That intuition eventually became a sentence I coined many years ago:

“A word is worth more than one thousand images.”

It was my inversion of the familiar saying that a picture is worth a thousand words.

At the time, I knew the sentence was true for me, but I could not explain exactly why I knew it.

Decades later, something happened that began to give that old intuition a mechanism.

It started with something very ordinary.

I went outside on a windy, unusually warm evening. I noticed the wind and its warmth, did what I needed to do, and went back inside partly because I wanted to get away from the wind.

Inside, I drank some sizzling water and looked through the window.

I could “see the wind.”

Of course, that isn't literally exact. I wasn't seeing the air itself. I was seeing its effects on a tree. But the image of the moving tree, together with the bright light-blue background, somehow connected itself to the temperature of the air.

Two phenomena that I normally might experience separately—wind and heat—suddenly became one perceptual configuration.

Immediately, something I call my “geographic command” seemed to set the coordinates to outside.

I went back outside.

It was windy and hot, and I enjoyed it tremendously.

What fascinated me was not merely the experience. I realized that I had witnessed a cascade of thought while the cascade was still happening.

Usually, when I describe one of my thoughts, I am describing something that has already happened. This time I was writing about it while it was entering existence.

That led me to think about the difference between the smooth story I can tell afterward and what actually happened inside my mind.

The story is a straight path.

The thinking was not.

It was more like climbing a tree trunk covered with branches. At every moment there were many possible directions I could take. I had to choose which branch, which association, which observation, or which thought should come next.

I realized that too many possibilities can produce something resembling fuzziness.

We normally think of fuzziness as a lack of information. But perhaps there is another kind:

too many possible directions can also make something unfocused.

Then I began thinking about words.

I realized that words seem to behave like lenses.

A lens directs attention. It makes us see something from a particular direction.

Words can do something similar.

If I say “wind,” I have directed your attention toward one aspect of reality. If I say “hot,” I direct it toward another. But when I put them together—“hot wind”—the two words don't merely add information. They create a particular configuration in the mind.

The words have changed the universe—not the physical universe, of course, but the universe that is available to attention.

This made me think of a phrase as a kind of tunnel.

I saw a phrase as a transparent, segmented tunnel connecting two sides:

origin → destination

Each word was a segment of that tunnel.

Then I asked myself a strange question:

What exactly is being transported through the tunnel?

I couldn't answer.

For a moment I was stuck.

Then my perspective changed. Instead of looking at the tunnel from outside, my mind's eye seemed to enter the tunnel.

And inside it I discovered the answer:

I was what was being transported.

By “I,” I mean my mind's eye—the point from which I experience and observe my thoughts.

The phrase wasn't simply transporting information from one place to another.

It was transporting the reader's mind from one conceptual location to another.

That changed the way I thought about language.

A phrase can be a conduit.

It can take a mind from an origin to a destination.

And then I wondered: what determines how many words are necessary to make that journey?

My intuition was that it depends partly on the distance between the origin and destination.

That distance does not have to be physical. It can be conceptual, emotional, logical, perceptual, or any other kind of distance.

And there is another factor: precision.

Sometimes we only need to get someone approximately somewhere.

Other times we need to place their mind at a very specific vantage point.

The more precisely we need to position the new viewer, the more structure the linguistic path may require.

This made me think about books.

When I thought about a phrase as a tunnel, I suddenly saw an open book. I could see the two pages, particularly the page on the right. Over that image I could also see the transparent segmented tunnel I had previously imagined for the phrase.

Then something became obvious.

A book is not just one tunnel.

It is a huge sequence of tunnels.

Each phrase has an origin and a destination. The destination of one phrase can become the origin of the next. Phrase after phrase, the reader's mind's eye travels through the book until it reaches the final destination.

I saw the book as a very long, thin, transparent, segmented tunnel whose purpose was essentially to transport the reader's mind's eye through a long journey.

Then I became interested in why the tunnel was transparent.

I knew that this had something to do with the individual words.

If a word is a segment of the tunnel, it has an entrance and an exit. Its points of contact with neighboring words determine how it functions in that particular phrase.

But the word cannot be only the information between those two contact points.

There must be something surrounding it.

The transparent walls represented that something.

A word has more semantic possibilities than the particular meaning it is carrying at that moment.

This is where my old intuition about words suddenly came back.

I began to see a word as something like a sphere—a spherical lens.

The word contains, or gives access to, many possible directions and meanings. But when the word is placed inside a phrase, the words around it establish points of contact. Those contacts determine the direction in which we are looking through the word.

So the word itself does not necessarily have only one direction.

Context chooses the direction.

Imagine a word such as “bank.”

By itself, the word opens a large semantic field. But in:

«“I deposited money at the bank,”»

the surrounding words establish one direction through the word.

In:

«“We sat on the bank of the river,”»

they establish another.

The word has not changed.

The direction through the word has changed.

This also helped me understand something about missing words.

Imagine a sentence with a blank:

«“The dog ___ the ball.”»

The words before and after the blank act almost like two points of contact with the missing lens.

The previous word tells us something about how we approach the blank.

The next word tells us something about where we need to go after passing through it.

Together, they constrain the missing word.

And the longer the phrase becomes, the more constrained the blank can become.

A short phrase may leave many possible words available.

A much longer phrase establishes a much more specific trajectory. The origin and destination are farther apart, but the path between them has also become more defined.

In this sense, a long phrase can become “straighter.”

It gives the mind fewer opportunities to wander away from the intended destination.

The missing word is not simply guessed.

The mind may be able to re-encounter the missing lens because the surrounding linguistic structure has already established the direction through which that lens must be approached.

This brought me back to my old saying:

“A word is worth more than one thousand images.”

I think I understand it differently now.

An image can show us something.

A word can do something else.

A word can tell another mind where to look.

It can tell the mind what to notice, what to connect, and sometimes from which vantage point to look.

And because a word has a much larger semantic field around its immediate use, one word can potentially open access to an enormous number of images and ideas.

The word is therefore not merely an image's label.

It can be a lens, a direction chooser, and a conduit.

The phrase becomes a route.

The book becomes a journey.

And the reader's mind's eye becomes the traveler.

What fascinates me most is that I seem to have known some part of this long before I could explain it.

Twenty or thirty years ago, when I coined “a word is worth more than one thousand images,” I did not have this entire model. I did not consciously have the spherical lens, the tunnel, the origin and destination, or the idea of a missing word being constrained by two neighboring words.

But I apparently had the image of something invisible and transparent surrounding a word, something containing many possible images.

Today, I may have added a mechanism to that old image.

The old intuition was:

A word contains enormous visual and conceptual potential.

The newer understanding is:

The surrounding words determine the direction through that potential field, and the resulting phrase guides the mind along a particular path.

Perhaps this is why I have always experienced words so intensely.

I think visually.

Images often appear in my mind before words.

But now I am beginning to suspect that words themselves may contain a kind of geometry.

Not necessarily visual geometry.

Not necessarily the geometry of physical space.

Perhaps it is relational geometry—a geometry of positions, directions, connections, distances, and destinations.

And if that is true, then communication becomes something more interesting than transferring information.

Perhaps when we speak or write, we are sometimes constructing a path for another mind.

We are saying, in effect:

Start here.

Look this way.

Now move here.

Connect this to that.

Now look again from this new position.

And eventually:

Arrive here.

That may be what a phrase is capable of doing.

It may not merely tell another mind what to know.

It may tell another mind where to be in order to see it.


I think your mother may particularly appreciate that this is not really a story about English words. It is a story about what language does to the mind—and it begins with an intuition you carried for decades before finding a way to articulate its mechanism.
