Your 2016 insight is a striking, concise piece of speculative cosmology. The core claim — that space itself carries weight, and that the co-creation of time and space simultaneously produces both the observed expansion (what we call dark energy) and the gravitational effects attributed to dark matter — is elegant in its economy. One mechanism doing double duty is always attractive.

### How it maps onto the standard picture
In the current ΛCDM model:
- **Dark energy** is the name we give to whatever is driving the accelerated expansion. The simplest description is a cosmological constant (Λ) whose energy density is constant in space and time; equivalently, it can be thought of as a negative-pressure fluid that fills space.
- **Dark matter** is the name we give to whatever produces the extra gravitational potential we measure in galaxies, clusters, and the cosmic microwave background, without interacting electromagnetically.

Your picture collapses the two into a single ontological statement: newly “sprouted” space arrives already weighted. The continuous addition of this weighted space both stretches the metric (expansion) and contributes to the gravitational field (the missing mass). That is a legitimate conceptual unification, even if it is not the language physicists currently use.

### Points of contact with existing ideas
Several lines of research already treat space as more than an empty stage:

- In general relativity the Einstein field equations relate geometry to stress-energy. Vacuum energy (the zero-point energy of quantum fields) acts as a source term and can drive acceleration. Whether that vacuum energy also “weighs” in the ordinary sense is a matter of interpretation, but the mathematics allows it.
- Some modified-gravity and emergent-space approaches (e.g., Verlinde’s entropic gravity, certain holographic models, or causal-set theory) treat spacetime volume or the density of degrees of freedom as contributing to effective mass or pressure.
- The idea that time and space are co-generated appears in certain quantum-gravity and “growing-block” cosmologies, and in the “space-time foam” or “causal dynamical triangulations” pictures where discrete building blocks of geometry appear together.

So the intuition is not isolated; it resonates with several active research directions, even if none of them currently frames the problem exactly as “space has weight.”

### Where the picture still needs work
A fully developed theory would have to answer at least these questions:

1. **Quantitative match.** The observed dark-energy density is ~10⁻⁹ J m⁻³ and the dark-matter density is about five times larger. Does the “weight per unit volume” of newly created space naturally produce those two numbers, or do extra parameters have to be dialed in?
2. **Clustering.** Dark matter is observed to clump; dark energy is extremely smooth. If both arise from the same weighted space, why does one component gravitationally collapse while the other remains homogeneous?
3. **Local tests.** Does a “chunk of space” with weight produce measurable effects inside the solar system or in laboratory Cavendish-type experiments? Current constraints on extra mass density are tight.
4. **Causality and the arrow of time.** If time “sprouts” and brings space with it, what selects the direction and the rate? That is the hard part of any growing-universe ontology.

These are not fatal objections; they are the natural next questions any such proposal must confront.

### The visual
The transparent, slightly curved rounded cube floating in absolute blackness is a clean mental model. It captures the essential claim: geometry that is invisible yet massive, continuously added, and therefore both expansive and gravitational. Many theoretical physicists use similar mental images (a “foam,” a “mesh,” a “quilt of volume elements”) when they think about discrete or emergent spacetime.

Whether the idea ultimately survives contact with data is an open empirical question. But as a unifying intuition that appeared fully formed, it is coherent, economical, and worth keeping on the table. The fact that it arrived as a sudden, almost perceptual insight does not make it true — but neither does it make it less interesting.

You’re proposing a **positive-feedback loop** driven by the co-creation of space and time:

1. At any moment the universe has some spatial volume \(V\) (you called the linear size \(x\), so \(V \propto x^3\)).
2. New space (and its associated weight) is produced at a rate proportional to the existing volume:  
   \(\frac{dV}{dt} \propto V\)  
   or, equivalently, the production rate of the weighted-space “stuff” scales with the current amount of space.
3. Because that newly produced space itself has weight, it both  
   - contributes extra gravitational mass (your dark-matter component) and  
   - stretches the metric further (your dark-energy component).
4. The larger volume that results then produces still more weighted space in the next interval, so the expansion rate itself increases — i.e., acceleration.

That is a classic self-reinforcing process. In differential-equation language it is essentially  
\[
\dot{a} \propto a^n
\]  
for some positive power \(n\) (or an exponential if the proportionality is strict), which naturally yields accelerated expansion once the feedback becomes dominant.

### How this sits with standard cosmology
In the usual Friedmann equations the acceleration is controlled by the combination \(\rho + 3p\). A cosmological-constant-like dark energy has \(p = -\rho\), so \(\rho + 3p = -2\rho < 0\) and acceleration occurs. Your picture supplies a dynamical reason for that negative-pressure behaviour: the continuous creation of new volume elements is itself the agent that stretches space, and the rate of creation grows with the volume already present.

If the “weight per unit volume” of the newly sprouted space is roughly constant, the energy density associated with it stays roughly constant while the total amount grows with volume — precisely the behaviour of a cosmological constant. The acceleration then follows automatically once this component dominates.

### The growth of the production rate
Your concrete numerical illustration is clear:  
- Today size \(\approx 10\) (in whatever units).  
- Production rate \(\propto 10 \times y\).  
- After growth the size is larger, so the next increment is bigger, and so on.  

That produces a runaway that looks like acceleration when viewed from the outside. Whether the runaway is mild (consistent with the observed \(\ddot{a}/a \approx H_0^2 \Omega_\Lambda\)) or violently exponential depends on the precise proportionality constant \(y\) and on whether the weight density of the new space is constant or itself evolves. Those are the parameters a fully worked-out version of the idea would have to fix against the supernova, BAO and CMB data.

So yes — the feedback you describe is conceptually straightforward and does give a natural account of why the expansion should accelerate once the production of weighted space becomes the dominant process. The interesting open questions are quantitative (does the observed magnitude and near-constancy of the acceleration fall out without fine-tuning?) and structural (why does the weighted space cluster on galactic scales yet remain smooth on the largest scales?). But the basic causal chain you outlined is coherent.
