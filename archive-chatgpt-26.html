Alex, **I think there is a genuinely interesting idea here — but I would separate the quality of the intuition from whether the physics currently works.** And I want to take the image seriously, because the image is doing something important in your reasoning rather than merely illustrating a conclusion.

What appeared was essentially:

> **space itself is an entity with a physical property normally attributed to matter: weight.**

And then, almost immediately, you got a unification:

**time sprouts → space sprouts with it → newly created space has weight → two observable consequences appear: expansion + gravitational effect.**

That is a surprisingly economical conceptual move.

### The strongest part

The really interesting part isn't actually "space has weight."

It's this:

**You are proposing that what we currently call dark matter and dark energy might be two manifestations of the same underlying thing.**

That is a legitimate kind of question to ask in cosmology. Dark matter and dark energy are presently inferred from very different phenomena:

- dark matter behaves gravitationally like something that clusters and attracts;
- dark energy is associated with the accelerated expansion of the universe.

So your intuition is essentially asking:

**What if we are looking at two effects produced by one deeper component of reality?**

That's a good question.

And your image captures the proposed component unusually cleanly: an **invisible volume**, spatially extended, not made of ordinary matter, but nevertheless possessing a physical property capable of affecting the universe.

That is conceptually much more specific than simply saying "maybe dark matter and dark energy are connected."

### But here's where I have to push back

There is a major problem with:

> "If space has weight, that explains dark matter."

Not automatically.

In modern general relativity, **space isn't normally treated as an inert empty container whose weight could simply be added to the universe.** Spacetime geometry itself participates in gravity. And energy, momentum, pressure and stresses all contribute to the gravitational field.

So there is already a sense in which **the geometry of spacetime has physical gravitational consequences**.

But dark matter has some very specific observed behavior.

For example, whatever produces the dark-matter effect appears to:

- form halos around galaxies,
- gravitationally lens light,
- influence galaxy rotation,
- participate in the formation of large-scale structure,
- leave characteristic signatures in the cosmic microwave background.

A completely uniform "weight of space" wouldn't obviously do that.

That's the first enormous test your idea has to pass.

### Dark energy has a different problem

Your proposed chain is:

**time sprouts → space sprouts → universe gains more space → expansion occurs.**

That's wonderfully intuitive geometrically.

But there's an important distinction:

**expansion itself isn't the same thing as accelerated expansion.**

The universe can expand without dark energy. What we need to explain is why the expansion is accelerating.

And in general relativity, the relevant property isn't simply "how much energy something has." **Pressure matters too.**

Dark energy has an unusual effective pressure—roughly, sufficiently negative pressure—which produces accelerated expansion.

So your "weight of space" would need to acquire a precise physical definition that reproduces something like that.

### And here's the really interesting possibility

Your idea might become much more interesting if **"weight" isn't meant literally as ordinary mass.**

Suppose what you intuitively called weight is actually your mind's way of representing:

> **space has an intrinsic gravitational/energetic property.**

Then your rounded transparent cube becomes almost like a **unit cell of spacetime with physical properties**.

And your statement

> "when time is created, it brings/creates space at the same time"

becomes a much stronger hypothesis:

**the emergence of temporal extent and spatial extent is accompanied by the emergence of a physical gravitational property.**

Now you're no longer merely saying "space weighs something."

You're proposing a relationship between:

**time ↔ space ↔ gravitational effect ↔ cosmic expansion.**

That's much more interesting.

And it connects surprisingly naturally to something you've been saying about your cosmology: **time and space aren't independent ingredients; they appear together.**

### But there's a beautiful empirical trap waiting for your idea

If dark matter and dark energy really are "created together," they shouldn't merely coexist.

There should be a **relationship between their observed quantities or evolution.**

That's where your hypothesis becomes testable.

For example, if some amount of newly generated space produces both effects, then perhaps there should be a mathematical relationship of the form:

**dark-matter effect = F(space creation)**

and

**dark-energy effect = G(space creation)**

with both ultimately coming from the same underlying variable.

If the relationship predicts the observed cosmic history—early universe, structure formation, galaxy evolution, lensing, present expansion rate, and acceleration—**then you've got something much more substantial than a philosophical model.**

If it can't reproduce those things, nature is telling us where the intuition breaks.

And that's exactly the kind of test I'd want to give it.

That would be a *serious* test of the little transparent rounded cube you saw at 1 AM.

That amount of existing space produces some amount of new time/space, and therefore some amount of your proposed "space-weight" effects.

**space = 10 → 11**

**10 → 11 → 12.1 → 13.31 → 14.64 → ...**

- **constant addition:** 10 → 11 → 12 → 13 → 14
