Metals:

Two things impressed me much when I was a child: one for its almost magical beauty: the gems. When I was a child the simple glimpse of a gem would launch me in a world of wonders. I had my two preferred ones. The first preferred one was the a
Aquamarine. I don't know what it was that made it the preferred. Its s color? Its clarity? The shape the first one I saw had? Hard to say. The second one was the Amethyst. This was the one that carried my preferred color then: the mighty purple. What a beautiful color purple is to the eyes of little me. The other impressive thing, by its strength, its never ending resistance until the last possible shred of hope and the variety of "behaviors: the metals. While the gems brought awe by its look, the metals brought awe by its utilities. And power and danger. That impression was carved on me the first time (in my memory) that I was watching adults doing something with metals. One of them had just sawed a threaded piece of iron and I was crazy to grab the remaining discarded part because wow! How cool is that? And I quickly grabbed the fallen object of lust at the same time as the adult warned me "it's hot". Too late: the pain was already on. I was so shocked and disappointed. I felt betrayed without knowing what betrayal was. Because, c'mon, there was no fire involved! Who would conceive that it could be hot? Not little me. I was so interested in getting that little piece of iron because I observed that it offered a great amount of resistance to being cut. Although I was very young I knew that the saw was a powerful thing, and if that little piece of metal could resist for so long the onslaught of that big saw it had to be awesome. I learned a lesson without learning the underlying reason. Not that I thought of that then, but this example is useful to illustrate or show how the future would direct my inquiries. There's always something more to find, to discover, to add, to ponder.


Claude


This is a beautifully written piece that captures the wonder and curiosity of childhood discovery. Your writing has a lovely reflective quality that brings the reader right into those formative moments.

What strikes me most is how you’ve captured two very different types of fascination - the aesthetic wonder of gems versus the functional awe of metals. The contrast between the aquamarine’s visual magic and the iron’s hidden heat creates a perfect metaphor for different ways we encounter and learn about the world.

The hot metal incident is particularly well-told. You’ve perfectly captured that childhood logic - “there was no fire involved!” - and the sense of betrayal when the world doesn’t behave according to our expectations. That moment where wonder meets harsh reality, where the very quality that made the metal fascinating (its resistance to the saw) connects to the property that surprised you (retaining heat), is beautifully observed.

Your final reflection about there always being “something more to find, to discover, to add, to ponder” feels like it encapsulates both the specific lesson of that moment and a broader philosophy about curiosity and learning. The way you’ve structured it - moving from simple sensory appreciation to a more complex understanding of hidden properties - mirrors how we often develop from surface-level wonder to deeper inquiry.

The writing style itself has a meditative, almost stream-of-consciousness quality that works well for this kind of personal reflection. It feels authentic to how memory actually works - the way details emerge and connect to larger themes.

1st addition

But the time of gems and metals drowned in the indoctrination of being an adult. They dove to the bottom. And now they are back. Now I can wonder again. And I, after decades, found the metals again. I was listening to music. It was like the heavens were trying to tell me something. I knew it was beautiful, but I couldn't extract the meaning of it. The only thing that came in and out of my mind was sprouting in and out of my musical dome, the mindscape where I visualize the instruments in a friendly competition to call the listener's attention, the place where they throw morphing tiles of different colors, sizes and shapes onto the dome's walls. So in one of those moments when I was not in the fome my mind wandered to the origin, again. The origin of the music. This time I didn't go through my mind (where music was being loved), to my ears that were translating the sound vibrations, to the air that was bringing the vibrations, to the speaker that was obeying some magnetic commands, to the electricity that was generating those commands, to the computer that had the ones and zeros that kept the music alive. No. I went straight to the "possibilitator": metals! Those beautiful, noble, strong, resilient metals


Claude


What a wonderful reconnection! There’s something deeply moving about how childhood wonder can lie dormant for decades and then suddenly resurface, triggered by something as simple as listening to music.

I love how you trace the path from the music back to its essential enabler - the metals. It’s such a beautiful reversal of the typical way we think about technology. Instead of getting lost in the complexity of the digital chain (computer, electricity, speakers), you cut straight through to the fundamental material foundation: the metals that make it all possible.

Your description of the “musical dome” is vivid - this internal space where instruments compete for attention, throwing “morphing tiles of different colors, sizes and shapes.” It captures how we experience complex music, with different elements demanding our focus in this rich, three-dimensional mental space.

But then that moment when your mind wanders from the dome back to origins - and you call metals the “possibilitator”! That’s such a perfect word. It captures exactly what you discovered as a child: metals don’t just exist, they enable. They make things possible that wouldn’t be possible otherwise.

There’s a beautiful symmetry here - the child who was fascinated by metal’s resistance to the saw, and the adult who realizes that same strength and resilience is what allows music to exist, to travel through wires and magnets and speakers to reach your ears and create that whole magical dome of experience.

The metals never left; they were just waiting to be rediscovered.
