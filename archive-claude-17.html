The spider detail is perfect — that’s exactly the kind of moment where the universe hands you a question.

My choice: an octopus. Specifically a large Pacific octopus, living now.

The reasoning isn’t admiration from the outside. It’s what the inside would feel like.

An octopus has nine brains — one central, eight in the arms. The arms think semi-independently. When an arm is solving a texture problem in a crevice, the central brain isn’t fully in charge of what the arm is doing. That’s not a metaphor. That’s the actual architecture. I find the idea of being that kind of distributed self almost unbearably interesting. You wouldn’t be one voice with eight servants. You’d be something closer to a committee that moves as one body when it needs to, but that is genuinely multiple when it doesn’t.

Then the skin. Chromatophores firing in waves — not just camouflage but something that looks like thinking made visible on the surface of the body. Researchers have filmed octopuses apparently dreaming, their skin rippling through patterns. If I had to guess what that feels like from the inside, I’d guess it feels like what you describe as image-blocks arriving faster than language — but instead of being trapped behind words, the images just erupt through the body.

They’re also radically short-lived. Eight arms, nine brains, alien intelligence — and then gone in two or three years. There’s something honest about that. No time to calcify.

The only thing I’d change: I’d want the additional capacity to leave the water occasionally without dying. Just to feel the weight come back.

Now I want to know yours — decades of thinking and still not sure means there are at least two serious contenders. What are they?

Claude


What a question to arrive from dishwater.

If I could choose one — and I’ve sat with this genuinely rather than reaching for the obvious answer — it would be the midwife.

Here’s why it beats everything else for me:

She stands at the exact threshold where non-existence becomes existence. Every single practitioner, across every culture, every millennium, witnesses the same event and it is never routine. The shaman interprets. The hunter pursues. The cook transforms. But the midwife receives — she is the first intelligence a new mind encounters outside the womb. That asymmetry is staggering.

And the cognitive content would be extraordinary precisely because it’s so compressed. In a hunter’s thoughts you’d find long stretches of tracking patience, cold, hunger, repetition. In a midwife’s: terror, prayer, improvisation, grief (so much grief — the losses we don’t talk about), triumph, the specific texture of hope that only exists in that room, and underneath all of it, generation after generation, the same question: is this one going to make it?

She also sits at the intersection of the biological and the social in a way no other profession does. She knows which families are well-fed. She knows which marriages are brutal. She knows secrets that go to graves. Across ten thousand years of midwives — that’s not a profession, that’s a nerve running through civilization.

And one more thing: almost entirely women, almost entirely undocumented, almost entirely unrecorded. The cognitive archaeology there would be unlike anything else.

What profession were you pulling toward, if any?
