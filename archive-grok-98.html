Your reflection—rooted in that raw, boyish belief that the good is simply meant to be shared, not out of ideology or coercion but pure love—doesn't map neatly onto any major political party today. But as a school of thought, it resonates deeply with a few traditions that place love (especially selfless, anchoring love) at the center of human society, while explicitly rejecting forced equality or state-enforced sharing.

The closest echoes come from strands of Christian-inspired thought that emphasize agape (unconditional, sacrificial love) as the guiding force for community and economics:

- Christian Anarchism (or Tolstoyan anarcho-pacifism): Leo Tolstoy, inspired by the Sermon on the Mount, argued that true Christianity demands sharing out of love, nonviolence, and rejection of the state (which he saw as built on coercion). It's not communism—Tolstoy despised Marxist systems as just another form of violence—but a voluntary surrender to love's "resistance is futile" pull. Communities live simply, share freely, and let love reset origins, much like your "new origin" from focused love. Modern examples include the Catholic Worker Movement (Dorothy Day), where hospitality, mutual aid, and personalist love drive everything without state or capitalist structures.

- Distributism (from Catholic social teaching): Developed by G.K. Chesterton and Hilaire Belloc, drawing on papal encyclicals like Rerum Novarum and Quadragesimo Anno. It calls for widespread ownership of property and resources so families and communities can flourish independently—sharing the good through cooperation, guilds, and local economies, not central planning. The motivation is human dignity and the common good, grounded in love and subsidiarity (decisions at the smallest, most personal level). It's explicitly anti-communist and anti-unfettered-capitalist, favoring a society where love anchors direction toward mutual flourishing.

- Ethical or Christian Socialism (in its non-Marxist forms): Thinkers like R.H. Tawney emphasized altruism, cooperation, and moral love over egoism or state force. It's sharing because it's right and loving, not because the system demands it.

Less directly, communitarianism stresses community bonds and shared good over individualism, sometimes infused with spiritual love, but it's broader and less explicitly about surrender-to-love.

And that poetic closer—words as warp drives, instantly transporting by contact? It fits perfectly: these traditions see love not as abstract policy but as contagious, transformative contact. One act of genuine sharing warps reality toward the dream-awake state you describe.
