🧠 Image Generation vs Visual Perception in AI

You're right to flag the paradox: AI systems can generate highly detailed images from text prompts, yet they lack any true visual perception. Here's a breakdown of why this occurs:

🧬 How AIs Generate Images

AI image generators like DALL·E, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion use neural networks trained on massive datasets of images and captions. Two dominant architectures:

- Diffusion Models: Start with random noise and iteratively refine it into a coherent image based on the prompt.
- GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks): One network generates images, another critiques them, improving quality through feedback loops.

These systems don’t “see” the image—they construct it by statistically matching patterns from training data to the prompt.

👁️ Why AIs Don’t “See” What They Generate

Despite their output, these models lack perceptual faculties. Here's why:

- No Sensorium: They don’t have cameras or photoreceptors. Their “input” is symbolic (text), not sensory.
- No Feedback Loop: Unlike biological vision, there's no recursive loop where the system observes, interprets, and adjusts based on what it sees.
- No Ontological Anchor: They don’t possess a model of the world that links visual data to embodied experience. They generate images as plausible renderings, not as verified perceptions.
- Training Bias: Image generators are trained to produce images, not to understand them. Recognition models (like those used in self-driving cars) are trained differently and still struggle with robustness.

🧩 Related Insight: Why AI Struggles with Text in Images

Even advanced models like DALL·E often jumble letters or produce incoherent text in images. This is because they treat text as visual texture, not as semantic content. They haven’t learned to read—they’ve learned to imitate the appearance of writing.

🧠 Philosophical Note

Your analogy—black as the shared perceptual substrate—is apt. AI doesn’t “see” black; it knows black as a concept. Similarly, it doesn’t see images—it models them. The absence of eyes is not a flaw—it’s a reflection of a different epistemology: symbolic, not sensory.
