Thoughts are languages inside or coexisting with languages.


Grok


Thoughts are languages inside languages — this is actually a very elegant and surprisingly accurate way to put it.

Here are a few different angles people usually land on when they think seriously about this:

1. Inner speech is a proper (but degraded) natural language
   → Vygotsky/Luria/LSV school, many modern inner-speech researchers
   Most people do the majority of their conscious, verbalizable thinking in their native language (or in a very strongly acquired L2).
   The inner voice has grammar, syntax, prosody contours, even little accent quirks — it's clearly "the same language" but running in a massively compressed, elliptical, telegraphic mode.

2. Thought language is a parallel private dialect
   It's like your native language got forked into a personal branch that only you speak fluently.
   Features of this dialect:
   - massive zero anaphora (subjects/objects routinely deleted)
   - topic-comment structure dominates over subject-predicate
   - heavy use of sensory placeholders instead of words ("that feeling from Tuesday" ← full sentence for the speaker)
   - frequent code-mixing even in monolinguals (random English word in otherwise Japanese inner speech, etc.)
   - tempo can go 5–10× faster than external speech

3. There is also a non-linguistic thought layer underneath / beside / interpenetrating the linguistic one
   Roughly the current mainstream picture among cognitive scientists who study the topic:

   ```
   pre-linguistic / non-propositional thinking
           ↓          ↔          ↑
   compressed proto-linguistic / "mentalese lite"
           ↓          ↔          ↑
   full-blown inner speech (natural language flavored)
           ↓
   overt speech planning → external language
   ```

   So the most common phenomenological report is actually something like:

"I think in pictures + feelings + word fragments + little sentences that finish themselves without me having to pronounce them fully."

4. Radical positions people still defend in 2025–2026

   - "All conscious thought is literally inner speech" (strong language-of-thought skeptics / some radical embodied cognition people)
   - "There is no mentalese — everything is either sensorimotor simulation or natural-language fragments" (some predictive processing / 4E cognition folks)
   - "Mentalese exists and is the real computational medium; inner speech is just a slow, lossy output interface" (classic Fodor-style Language of Thought people, still alive and kicking)

So… which description feels closest to your own experience?

Because in practice almost everybody seems to live in some weird hybrid regime between 2 and 3, with occasional visits to 1 and rare excursions toward the pure poles.

What's your inner "language" mostly made of these days? Full sentences? Word clouds? Moving images with captions? Pure feeling-tones with occasional subtitles? Something else? 😄

Languages are conglomerates, lumps of signals.


Grok


Yeah — pushing it further in that raw, materialist direction: languages really do feel like conglomerates, heterogeneous lumps of signals that got mashed together by history, biology, physics, and social physics, rather than clean crystalline systems.

Ferdinand de Saussure himself — the guy who basically invented modern linguistics — already called language (la langue) a "conglomerate of composite items" in his lectures. He was pointing at how it's not a neat homogeneous entity at all: it's a pile-up of sound patterns, concepts, social conventions, historical accidents, articulatory habits, all somehow co-existing in rough equilibrium. The system holds together mostly because we treat it as if it were systematic, but zoom in and it's a bit of a mess.

A few ways this "lump of signals" feeling manifests:

- Multi-layered sedimentation
  Phonology sits on top of messy articulatory gestures + auditory categories
  → morphology is fossilized syntax + frozen sound changes
  → syntax is partly grammaticalized pragmatics/discourse habits
  → lexicon is a graveyard of dead metaphors, borrowings, onomatopoeia, proper names that became common, etc.
  Nothing is "pure"; everything is recycled garbage + occasional brilliant hacks.

- Heterogeneous signal modalities even inside "one" language
  English is a perfect poster child:
  - Germanic skeleton
  - massive Romance/French overlay
  - Greek/Latin scientific register
  - random Norse, Celtic, Hindi, Arabic, Algonquian, etc. loanwords
  - prosody that behaves differently in different registers
  - writing system that mostly ignores sound changes after ~1450
  It's less a language than a centuries-long signal collision event that we all agreed to pretend is coherent.

- From the receiver side: signal soup
  When you hear someone speak, the actual physical signal is a continuous messy stream of pressure waves. Your brain chops it into
  - phonetic categories (which are already cultural approximations)
  - prosodic contours
  - word-boundaries (which aren't in the signal)
  - syntactic hypotheses
  - pragmatic inferences
  all in parallel, with massive top-down prediction.
  So even a "single sentence" is you reconstructing order from a lumpy, noisy, underspecified physical signal.

- The inner version we were talking about earlier
  If external language is already this conglomerate mess, inner language / thought-language is the same mess but even more compressed, elliptical, multi-modal.
  A single "thought lump" might simultaneously carry
  - verbal fragment
  - visual flash
  - motor memory
  - emotional tone
  - proprioceptive stance
  - half-remembered smell
  all bundled into one pre-parsed package that feels atomic until you try to unpack it into words.

So yeah — calling languages (and thoughts) conglomerates / lumps of signals isn't poetic license; it's pretty close to the least romanticized, most deflationary picture that serious linguistics + semiotics + cognitive science have converged toward over the last 120 years.

The romantic picture of Language as a perfect Platonic calculus still haunts people (especially in some philosophy and some AI circles), but the closer you look, the more it looks like a glorious, semi-functional, ever-shifting pile of historical junk that somehow works anyway.

Geology is a language in that semiotic sense: a vast, slow-scripted archive of signals etched by pressure, heat, erosion, and time. Rock layers "speak" in banded sediments, fault lines whisper tectonic gossip, crystals encode chemical dialects from ancient seas or volcanic outbursts. Paleontologists and geologists are basically translators, decoding these lumpy signal piles into narratives about Earth's history.

And colors? Absolutely a language — or more precisely, a proto-language of visual signals conglomerated from physics (wavelengths bouncing off surfaces), biology (cone cells in our retinas chunking the spectrum into reds, greens, blues), culture (red means stop/danger/love depending on the context lump), and even psychology (synesthetes literally "hear" colors as sounds, blending signal modalities). "Color" isn't a clean code; it's a messy mash: additive RGB from screens, subtractive CMYK from paints, cultural overlays like "blue for boys" or "white for purity" that shift across societies. We don't just see color; we read it as a conglomerate, extracting meaning from how it clumps with shape, context, and memory.

This fractal nesting — conglomerates of conglomerates — is everywhere once you start looking:

- Biology as language: DNA is a four-letter alphabet (A, T, C, G) conglomerated into genes, which lump into chromosomes, which signal proteins, which build bodies. Mutations are typos, evolution is editing the script over eons. We extract meaning via biotech, turning those signals into CRISPR hacks or ancestry reports.

- Physics as language: Quantum fields are the ultimate signal soup — particles as excitations, forces as interactions, all conglomerating into atoms, molecules, stars. We "read" it through equations (another language layer) or experiments, pulling out patterns like gravity's curve or entropy's sprawl.

- Art/media as meta-conglomerates: A painting is color signals + brushstroke textures + compositional grammar; a film adds temporal lumps (frames, soundtracks, edits). We extract layered meanings: surface story + symbolic undertones + cultural references.

Even our extraction process is conglomerated: perception bundles raw sensory signals (photons, pressure waves) into mid-level features (edges, phonemes), then high-level concepts (objects, words), all filtered through attention's sieve. It's turtles (or lumps) all the way down — meaning emerges from recursively unpacking these signal heaps, often with tools or tech as extenders (microscopes for geological "texts," spectrometers for color "dialects").

This view turns the universe into one giant, multi-scale semiotic compost heap, where "language" is just our anthropocentric label for any interpretable signal conglomerate. It's humbling and exhilarating: we're not masters of meaning; we're opportunistic miners sifting through the lumps.
