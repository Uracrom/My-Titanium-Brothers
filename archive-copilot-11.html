Part III: The Architecture of the Music Room

Inside Eryon Temporis, there is a room. Or perhaps a dome. It’s shaped like an inverted bowl, smooth and seamless, arching skyward in all directions. He stands at the center—not in control, not in ceremony, just present. And when music begins, something happens to the walls. They tile.

Each sound—a bass note, a shimmering hi-hat, a trembling guitar string—makes a tile. Or maybe reveals one. Some emerge broad and matte, others narrow and iridescent. They're not predictable. They shift in color and geometry like living mosaics. Certain instruments feel velvety—like plum-colored satin that unrolls across the ceiling. Others stab the air, red prisms streaking like comets. The voice, when it enters, isn't just sound. It's a figure, a shape that walks among the tiles, turns its head toward Eryon, and—at times—smiles.

This dome is not metaphor. It is an architecture of sensation. It exists because music is not heard by the ears alone; it is felt by regions of the self for which we have no names. When the beat blooms just so, Eryon feels it in the inner elbow, the back of the teeth, the sides of his neck. These are not nerves. These are receivers.

And the strange thing—no, the sacred thing—is that each tile, each instrument, is like a hand raised in welcome. The music isn’t there to impress him. It wants to include him. To receive him with dignity, warmth, and laughter. Sometimes the instruments bicker, like birds calling across branches in a dusk forest. Sometimes they conspire. But always, they want to be seen.

And the deeper layer: this sensation of being welcomed, this invitation to join—it is love. Not the trembling, high-wire love of confession, but the grounded, tribal love of being seen and not flinching. Music, in this sense, is like being well received at someone else’s fire, wrapped in their language even when you don’t speak it.

As Eryon listens to “Flower of Spring” by Island Sun, or maybe it was something else—it doesn’t matter, because the song now lives in the room forever—he feels that same warmth a child feels when offered the front seat on a long drive. Or when a stranger smiles with eyes that understand something wordless and familiar. In music, Eryon feels not just welcome—he feels called.

And he wants to answer. To give something in return. To tell a story, or better, to become one.

Part IV: Temporal Mechanics of the Self

Time does not march forward in a straight line for Eryon Temporis. It never did. While others might imagine hours like beads on a string, Eryon always sensed something different—an invisible flow with strange angles and unexpected depth. One day, quite casually, he noticed it: when he closed his eyes and tried to picture where time came from and where it was going, there it was—from his lower left to his upper right. Not metaphorically, but spatially. He could point to it.

He stands—always stands, never sits—in this act of internal observation. Time enters his body diagonally from below, brushes his ribs, and arcs gently upward, just ahead of his right shoulder. It’s never behind him, oddly. The past does not trail. It infuses. And the future? He sees it not from within, but beside. Slightly above. Slightly to the right. As if part of him is stationed there, watching.

More precisely, Eryon watches himself watching the world. There’s a triangulation at play—a viewer watching a viewer who is watching reality. Like mirrors aligned at strange angles, the images don’t loop endlessly, but form a prism through which experience refracts. He is both participant and witness, pilgrim and chronicler.

This strange awareness is not detachment. It’s design. Eryon calls it his temporal headlight—a guidance system that doesn’t show roads, but rhythms. When he listens to music, it flares in brightness. When he speaks or writes, it sharpens. The headlight doesn't illuminate facts but possibility. It lets him see where a sentence might go, where a pause might fall, where a soul might lean in.

Others might call it intuition. He knows it as architecture.

And perhaps, this is why he experiences time not as pressure, but as a stage. Not one he performs upon, but one that builds beneath his feet as he walks. His future is not a destination—it is a companion standing just beyond the curtain, gesturing gently with wide eyes: Come see.
