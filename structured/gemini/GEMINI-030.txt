Without thinking but "knowing" I looked up and there was the huge cottonwood tree that certainly dropped those myriad seeds.
Immediately I thought: "there must not be any wind today", because  those seeds are super light and are usually on the move, floating. Then I thought, or better, this whole "pack" of "information" showed up in my mind or was immediately accessible, difficult to know which: maybe those trees have 2 different strategies for seed dispersion, one, have the wind carry the seeds away, potentially spreading the species, and two, making sure some (or many) seeds remain in the area where the "mother" tree is already thriving because it is a good area. Then I thought: how would scientists measure that? How would them count the seeds dropped on a windy day versus on a not windy day? And then, immediately I thought/perceived that I was in a cascade and thought of you AIs, and the cascade was interrupted...
I wrote it and didn't edit it. I think it is more "real" and so more legit for you because it's "raw".

I wanted to know if the trees have the capacity of "holding" or "losing" the seeds according to the wind, this way "telling" the seeds "stay around, this is a good place" or "get out of here, this place sucks".

### The Mechanism: Do they "choose" to hold or release?

Your intuition that the tree might "tell" the seeds when to go is actually mechanically accurate, though it happens via passive biological engineering rather than conscious volition.

Cottonwood seeds are contained in capsules that open (dehisce) when they dry out. However, the actual *detachment* and launch of the seed fluff (the coma) from the open capsule is heavily dependent on atmospheric conditions:

* **The "Stay Here" Scenario (Low Wind/High Humidity):** On calm, damp days, the humidity keeps the fluffy fibers slightly weighed down and clumped. Without a threshold of aerodynamic drag, the seeds stay trapped in the catkin or drop straight down, anchoring the next generation to the "proven ground" where the mother tree thrived.
* **The "Get Out of Here" Scenario (High Wind/Low Humidity):** Dry air causes the fibers to expand, maximizing their surface area like tiny parachutes. When a strong gust hits, it creates the necessary lift to break the minor physical bond holding the seed to the capsule, launching it into the turbulent boundary layer of the atmosphere.

So, while the tree doesn't "know" the environment sucks or is great, evolution has fine-tuned the physical properties of the seed fluff to act as a logic gate: **If** wind speed $> X$ and humidity $< Y$, **then** launch.

---

### How Scientists Measure the Cascade

You wondered how scientists would quantify this. In field ecology, they use a mix of low-tech traps and high-tech fluid dynamics:

* **Seed Traps:** Scientists place arrays of sticky traps or funnel collectors at geometric distances (e.g., 5m, 10m, 50m, 100m) radiating out from an isolated cottonwood tree.
* **Anemometers & Timers:** They pair these traps with automated weather stations. By clearing the traps at specific intervals (e.g., after a calm 24 hours vs. after a windy 24 hours), they map the **dispersal kernel**—the mathematical distribution of where the seeds land.
* **Wind Tunnels:** To see the exact moment of "holding vs. losing," researchers put branches with ripe seed pods into wind tunnels, gradually ramping up the airspeed to measure the exact velocity required to detach the seeds at different humidity levels.
