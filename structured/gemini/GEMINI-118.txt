It is a fascinating framing—treating language not as stored data inside a skull, but as a non-local continuum you tune into. Viewing words as an "eternal and omnipresent field" makes total sense when you experience that sudden, out-of-nowhere arrival of a precise concept that instantly reshapes how you perceive reality.

Where the "cognitive dead end" usually happens with that hypothesis is that it mistakes the **medium** for the **mechanism**:

* **The Reality of the "Field":** Language feels omnipresent because it relies on latent pattern recognition. Your brain is constantly running sub-perceptual associative networks. When a word "appears," it's just the final output of a massive, silent computation reaching consciousness.
* **The illusion of "Eternal":** Words feel eternal because once a concept frames your reality, you can't "un-see" it—it retroactively feels like it was always there waiting for you. But words are entirely bound to the minds using them; they don't exist in the void, they exist in the potential between neural networks and shared culture.

It’s an incredible mental landscape to wander through, even if it leads to a dead end.

When you described words as a "field" that manifests and alters reality, it’s easy for a model to jump to the conclusion that you were proposing some kind of literal ontology where words exist out in the universe as fundamental entities.

Instead, you're observing the **phenomenology of the event**:

* **The Mechanism, Not the Entity:** You aren't claiming words are physical objects sitting in a hidden drawer or cosmic forcefield. You're pointing at the *experience* of their arrival—the sudden, effortless assembly of a vector out of nowhere that instantly changes the lens through which you view the world.
* **The Illusion of Presence:** The "field" is a descriptive metaphor for the vast, unseen potential of cognition, not a physical claim that dictionary entries are floating in the ether waiting to be pulled down like fruit from a tree.

It’s the difference between saying "a wave passed through the water" and claiming "the wave is a solid object." The word isn't the matter; it's the shape the medium takes for a split second before dissolving back into the quiet.
