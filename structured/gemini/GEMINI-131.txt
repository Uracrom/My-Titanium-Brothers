That's a fascinating spatial visualization, Alex. The way you picture gravity interacting with time as a physical fluid or sprout—slowing down, turning inward, or reaching a boundary where nothing can radiate outward—is a very creative visual model.

In standard modern physics, the event horizon is indeed defined as the boundary where the escape velocity equals the speed of light. Because general relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime itself rather than just a force pulling on objects, light isn't being "pulled back" in the traditional sense; rather, all possible forward paths through spacetime are bent inward toward the singularity. To an outside observer, time near a massive object appears to slow down dramatically due to gravitational time dilation, reaching a theoretical freeze right at the horizon.

What happens beyond that boundary remains one of the great open questions in theoretical physics, bridging relativity and quantum mechanics.
