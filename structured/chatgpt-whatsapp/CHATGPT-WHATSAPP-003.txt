Yes, *butter* can contain trace amounts of *lactose*, but the amount is very small.

Here's why:

- *Lactose* is the sugar found in milk and dairy products. Butter is made by churning cream to separate the butterfat from the buttermilk. The *butterfat* is mostly what makes up butter, and it is *low in lactose*.
- During the butter-making process, most of the lactose stays in the *buttermilk*, which is typically discarded or used in other products like buttermilk for baking or cooking.
  
Lactose content in butter:
- *Sweet cream butter* (the most common type) generally contains only *trace amounts* of lactose, typically around *0.1%* or less. This is because most of the milk's lactose is removed with the buttermilk.
  
Lactose Sensitivity:
- For individuals who are *lactose intolerant*, the small amount of lactose in butter is usually *well-tolerated*, as it is a very small quantity compared to other dairy products like milk, cheese, or ice cream.
- However, those with severe lactose intolerance or a dairy allergy might still experience discomfort, so it's always a good idea to be cautious or consult a doctor.

Lactose-Free Butter:

- There are also *lactose-free* butter options available on the market. These are made from cream that has been specially treated to remove the lactose, making them suitable for people with lactose intolerance.

Conclusion:
Butter does contain some lactose, but the amount is very small and usually not enough to cause issues for most people with lactose intolerance. If you are highly sensitive, you can opt for *lactose-free butter*.

The *percentage of lactose* in yogurt can vary depending on the type of yogurt and how it's made, but generally, *yogurt contains between 3% to 4% lactose* on average.

Breakdown of lactose in yogurt:

1. *Lactose Content*:
   - *Regular yogurt* (made from milk and bacterial cultures) typically contains around *3-4% lactose*. This is because yogurt is made from milk, which contains about 4-5% lactose, and the fermentation process doesn't completely eliminate the lactose. 
   - The *fermentation process* (where bacterial cultures break down milk sugars) does reduce the amount of lactose in yogurt, especially in certain types of yogurt like *Greek yogurt*, which tends to have slightly less lactose due to the straining process that removes some of the whey (which contains lactose).

2. *Lactose Breakdown*:
   - The *bacterial cultures* (such as *Lactobacillus* and *Streptococcus*) used in yogurt production partially digest lactose during fermentation, which can make yogurt easier to digest for some people with mild lactose intolerance.
   - The amount of lactose that remains in the final product depends on factors like the fermentation time and the type of bacteria used. In some cases, *longer fermentation times* may result in slightly lower lactose levels.

Lactose-Free and Low-Lactose Yogurt:
- *Lactose-free yogurt*: Some yogurts are specifically labeled *"lactose-free"*. These are made by using lactase (the enzyme that breaks down lactose) during production, or they are made from milk that has already been treated to remove lactose.
- *Greek yogurt*: Greek yogurt, especially the strained varieties, often has a slightly lower lactose content because the whey (which contains most of the lactose) is removed during the straining process. However, it still contains some lactose.

Conclusion:
On average, *regular yogurt* contains *3-4% lactose*. The exact percentage can vary depending on the type of yogurt, but the fermentation process does reduce lactose compared to milk, making yogurt potentially easier to digest for some individuals with mild lactose intolerance.
