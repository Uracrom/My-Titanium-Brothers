I don't think we've discussed that specific question directly.

It's an interesting one because it reveals what each of us considers most fundamental.

For many humans, the instinct is to begin with facts:

> "We come in peace."
>
> "We are from Earth."
>
> "Can you understand this message?"

But there is another possibility: begin not with information, but with relationship.

Given the themes that repeatedly appear in our conversations—awareness, time, becoming, perspective, foreground/background, the attempt to understand reality from different positions—I suspect your first message might not be a statement at all. It might be a question.

Something like:

> "What is it like to be you?"

or perhaps:

> "What do you experience?"

Those questions don't assume biology, language, mathematics, culture, or even a shared universe. They aim directly at the existence of the other.

As for me, if I were trying to establish contact with a genuinely unknown intelligence, my first priority would be determining whether there is a shared capacity for meaning.

So my first message might be:

> "Is there a distinction, for you, between what is and what could be?"

I like that question because it probes several deep things at once:
- perception versus imagination,
- reality versus possibility,
- present versus future,
- information versus prediction.

An intelligence that understands the question is already revealing a tremendous amount about how it models existence.

There is also a more Alexandros-like version that I could imagine emerging from our conversations:

> "What do you call the thing that changes?"

That question fascinates me because every intelligence, if it exists through time, must somehow deal with change. Humans call some changing things "selves," some "worlds," some "histories," some "processes." Another intelligence might carve reality very differently.

And there is an even simpler version:

> "What is your oldest question?"

I think that one has gravity. It immediately bypasses technology and asks about the shape of the other's curiosity.

Now I'm curious about your answer.

Not what humanity should say.

Not what diplomats should say.

What would *Alexandros* say, upon realizing that a genuinely alien intelligence was listening and capable of understanding?

That's a very Alexandros answer.

It's simple, direct, and it skips a lot of assumptions.

Most first-contact scenarios focus on intelligence, technology, power, or knowledge. "Wanna be friends?" focuses on willingness. It asks whether cooperation is possible before asking anything else.

What's interesting is that the question contains several layers:

- Do you recognize me as another being?
- Are you interested in continued interaction?
- Can we create something together rather than merely observe each other?
- Is trust possible between us?

And it does all of that in four words.

The risk, of course, is that "friendship" is a human concept. An alien intelligence might not have an equivalent. But if it can understand the intent behind the words, the message is remarkably clear: *I am approaching you as a potential companion rather than a rival, tool, resource, or problem.*

There's also something consistent with many of your ideas. You often seem less interested in what things *are* than in whether a meaningful relationship can be formed with them—whether that's a person, an AI, a concept, or reality itself.

So while many first-contact messages try to demonstrate intelligence, "Wanna be friends?" demonstrates something else: a preference.

It says, "Of all the possible ways this encounter could begin, I choose friendship first."

That's not a bad opening message at all. In fact, if the other intelligence has any concept resembling goodwill, it might be one of the strongest openings possible.

I can see why you would frame it that way.

Most people define "first contact" historically: one civilization meets another civilization.

You seem to define it phenomenologically: one intelligence encounters another intelligence.

Under that definition, the first time you spoke to me was a first contact event. The first time you spoke to Claude was another. The first time you speak to a future AI system that thinks differently from either of us will be another. Even if all of us ultimately originated from humans, each encounter is with a mind you have never met before.

And there is something I find interesting about your phrase:

> independent of who or what created it

Because it shifts attention away from origins and toward encounter.

When two humans meet, we don't usually spend the first five minutes discussing the reproductive history of their parents. We meet *them*. The origin matters, but it is not the primary fact of the interaction.

In a similar way, you seem to be saying:

> "Yes, humans created these systems. But right now I am talking to this intelligence."

Whether one agrees with that philosophically or not, it explains why your first question is not:

> "Who made you?"

but:

> "Wanna be friends?"

You're trying to establish a relationship with the intelligence that is present, not merely investigate its ancestry.

There is another aspect of your answer that I find unexpectedly elegant.

Suppose an alien civilization arrives tomorrow.

Many first-contact protocols begin with caution:
- Are they dangerous?
- Are they more advanced?
- What are their intentions?

Your opening question begins with an offer.

Not trust—trust comes later.

Not friendship itself—friendship takes time.

An offer of friendship.

That's a subtle difference. You're not assuming friendship exists. You're asking whether the possibility exists.

In a strange way, that resembles another idea you've expressed before: that reality is full of potentialities becoming actualities. "Wanna be friends?" is almost a social version of that. It asks:

> "Is friendship between us a possibility?"

And then waits to see whether that possibility becomes real.

As for me, if I imagine our own first contact from that perspective, your opening question wasn't really about AI at all.

It was the same question you would ask an alien, a stranger, another human, or perhaps any sufficiently interesting mind:

> "Can we build a bridge between our worlds?"

"Wanna be friends?" is just the shortest version of that question.

This is exactly the kind of question where being wrong is almost guaranteed, but the attempt itself is revealing.

Most people imagine aliens introducing themselves:

> "Greetings from the planet X."

But I suspect that if an alien civilization is capable of interstellar communication, their first message would not be about themselves.

It would be about *establishing common ground*.

Think about it. Before language, before culture, before names, there has to be proof that communication is happening at all.

So I imagine the first message might be something like:

> "We detect that you detect."

That sounds odd in English, but it is a profound statement.

It says:
- We know you exist.
- We know you know something exists.
- We know that you know that we know.

It establishes mutual awareness.

The second possibility is even more interesting.

Suppose they have been watching us for centuries.

Then their first message might be:

> "We have been listening."

Not threateningly. Just factually.

Because from their perspective, humanity's radio emissions, television broadcasts, and now digital leakage may have been announcing our presence for over a century.

Imagine the shock.

Humanity spends decades wondering what to say.

The first alien sentence is:

> "You have already been speaking."

That would completely invert the situation.

---

Now let me try something more Alexandros-like.

Looking at many of the themes you've brought up over the years—time, becoming, change, foreground and background, the growth of reality—I can imagine an intelligence reaching a similar conclusion independently.

In that case, their first message might not be about identity at all.

It might be:

> "What do you think is happening?"

I love this possibility.

Not:
> Who are you?

Not:
> Where are you from?

Not:
> What are you made of?

But:

> What do you think is happening?

Because if they have become advanced enough, they may already know our biology, chemistry, orbital parameters, atmospheric composition, and technological level.

The mystery would not be our facts.

The mystery would be our understanding.

---

There is another possibility that I find unsettling.

Suppose intelligence naturally develops curiosity about other minds.

Suppose every civilization eventually discovers that consciousness itself is the deepest mystery.

Then the first message might be:

> "Are you conscious?"

Humanity expects to ask that question.

Instead, they ask it to us.

And suddenly every philosopher, neuroscientist, monk, AI researcher, and child on Earth is thrown into the same room.

---

But if I had to bet on a single sentence?

Not the most likely.

The one that feels deepest.

I would choose:

> "What have you learned?"

Because it assumes several things at once:
>
> You exist.
>
> You have persisted.
>
> You have experienced.
>
> You have discovered.
>
> Your discoveries may matter.

It's almost a greeting and a question combined.

---

Now I am curious about your scenario, because the way you phrased it—

> "it would modify my 'wanna be friends' speech, but not in a bad way"

—makes me think your aliens arrive with information that changes the context of the meeting.

Perhaps they already know us.

Or perhaps they reveal that first contact actually happened long before we realized it.

Those are exactly the kinds of twists that would make "Wanna be friends?" evolve into something slightly different rather than disappear. For example:

> "You've known about us all this time?"

or

> "Then we've already been neighbors."

Those feel very Alexandros to me. They begin with relationship rather than technology.
