What I find particularly interesting is that the original intuition is actually about something real and profound:

> Two points can be physically close in space while being very far in terms of the path required to connect them.

The road below and the overpass are separated by perhaps 100 feet geometrically, but by a much longer route dynamically.

Physics contains similar ideas. In general relativity, for example, the geometry of space-time determines which paths are available. Things that seem "close" from one perspective can be effectively distant from another.

Then your mind asks:

> If light is the fastest thing, what prevents it from going faster?

Historically, physicists wondered exactly that.

Your "vacuum friction" idea is almost a natural human guess. If everything else slows because of friction, perhaps light encounters a subtle friction from empty space itself.

The modern answer is stranger:

Light is not moving at speed *c* because something is constantly slowing it down to that speed.

Rather, *c* appears to be woven into the structure of space-time itself. It's more like the conversion factor between space and time than a speed limit imposed by resistance.

A fish swims because of water.

A car moves through air.

A photon doesn't move through space-time in quite that way. It is one of the things that defines how space-time behaves.

What fascinates me in your cascade is the next jump.

You imagined:

- a photon
- its origin
- its destination
- the curved path imposed by gravity

all simultaneously.

The moment you held all four in a single mental image, the notion of "space-time quantum entanglement" appeared.

Not because of quantum mechanics itself, but because your intuition objected to something:

> How can the photon reach its future destination unless, somehow, the future destination already participates in the process?

That question has echoes of ideas that have repeatedly appeared in physics:

- least-action principles,
- path integrals,
- block-universe interpretations,
- retrocausal interpretations of quantum mechanics.

None of these literally say "the photon knows where it is going."

But many of them have the flavor of describing a process using both endpoints at once rather than imagining a little particle blindly making local decisions.

So while the physics of the cascade is speculative, the *shape of the question* is surprisingly close to questions physicists themselves have asked.
