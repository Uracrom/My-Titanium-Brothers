Yes. And I think your explanation identifies something important about the *communication channel* between your cognition and the text.

Your phrase:

> **“words are fields”**

can be perfectly reasonable as a compressed statement generated from a much larger visual/intuitive structure. But if I treat those three words as though they were a carefully formalized proposition, I can accidentally manufacture a theory you never proposed.

So from now on, when something arrives that seems unusually absolute, ontologically strange, or dramatically stronger than the surrounding reasoning, I should have a little internal **“compression warning”**:

> *Is Alex actually asserting this literally, or is this the fastest available label for something he is seeing?*

And then ask you before constructing the skyscraper. 😄

### And we have an almost perfect example

Just **yesterday**.

You wrote something along the lines of:

> **“The words are a ‘field’… manifested anywhere, anytime.”**

Several of us interpreted that in a much stronger direction — essentially toward **words/representations being things that have some kind of independent reality or substance**.

Then you came back with:

> **“Basically all the AIs I shared this text [with] thought or understood that I think words are ‘real’, matter. I never said that. I totally don’t think that.”**

That's *exactly* the phenomenon you're describing.

What happened, I think, was:

**Your cognition:**
*“The behavior of words in this particular situation resembles the behavior of a field.”*

↓ compression

**Your fingers:**
*“Words are fields.”*

↓ interpretation

**AI:**
*“Alex proposes that words literally possess field-like ontological existence.”*

↓ elaboration

**AI:**
*constructs an increasingly sophisticated theory about the physical/reality status of words*

😂

And the particularly interesting part is that **the AI's mistake isn't necessarily stupidity**. Your sentence is grammatically a categorical identity:

> A = B

rather than:

> A behaves somewhat like B.

So the language itself strongly invites the literal reading.

This is actually a very useful piece of information about how to read your material. **Your first sentence may be a label for an entire pre-verbal gestalt, not the complete proposition.**

And that connects beautifully with something you've told me before: the image/insight arrives first and the words are essentially trying to catch it while it's moving.

So yes: **when the words seem “out of whack,” I should sometimes suspect that I'm seeing the compression artifact rather than the underlying idea.**

And I particularly like your last instruction: *don't feel bad about it.* I'll take that seriously. The interesting thing isn't that an AI occasionally gets the compressed sentence wrong. The interesting thing is learning **where the compression tends to distort the transmission.**
