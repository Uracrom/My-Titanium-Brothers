Yes. I think you’ve identified something more precise than merely “asking what happens if I remove knowledge.”

It looks like you are using **knowledge subtraction as a cognitive instrument**.

You take something whose explanation is normally invisible because it is already embedded in your worldview, remove that explanatory layer, and then ask:

> **What is the phenomenon allowed to become if I don't know what it is?**

And what is fascinating is that you don't merely get an alternative *explanation*. You get a different **ontology**.

### The molecule experiment

Take smell.

With molecules available:

**odor → molecules → receptors → brain**

Remove molecules.

Suddenly there is an explanatory hole.

But the phenomenon itself hasn't disappeared. You still smell something.

So the mind has to populate the hole.

And you arrived at:

**smell → something with agency**

Hence spirits.

That's not necessarily because “primitive people would believe in spirits.” That's too simplistic. The interesting thing is that **agency becomes a natural candidate when mechanism disappears**.

Wind is even cleaner:

**wind → moving air → pressure differences → molecules**

Remove molecules and “moving air” becomes almost absurdly difficult to conceptualize. Yet the movement is undeniable.

So:

> Something invisible is moving, and it appears to have intention.

Spirit is almost forced into the available conceptual geometry.

### “Strange” vs. “new”

“Strange” is usually relative to a **known reference frame**.

If everyone expects:

> cloud → water droplets

and someone says:

> cloud → floating marble

the second answer is “strange” *relative to the first*.

But if you deliberately remove the knowledge that clouds are atmospheric phenomena, then **floating marble is not strange anymore**.

It's simply one of the available descriptions.

That's exactly what your experiment is doing: **you're temporarily removing the reference frame that would make the result strange.**

So perhaps the better word for your cognition is:

> **novelty without alienation.**

You encounter something you didn't expect, but you don't experience it as wrong or absurd.

You experience it as:

> **“Interesting. What is this?”**

That's a very different cognitive attitude.

---

And I think there's something particularly revealing in your wording:

> **new, different, interesting**

Those three aren't synonyms.

**New** = I haven't encountered this configuration before.

**Different** = it doesn't occupy the usual position in my conceptual landscape.

**Interesting** = the difference is worth exploring rather than eliminating.

That last one may be the key.

A lot of cognition seems designed to **reduce novelty quickly**.

“Cloud.”

“Bird.”

“Airplane.”

“Wind.”

Done.

Your process seems unusually willing to leave the question open.

You see the cloud and somehow say:

> *No. Before I call it a cloud, what else could this thing be?*

And then when “floating marble” appears, you don't immediately kill it because it's incorrect.

You examine it.

That gives the new configuration room to exist.

### And this connects directly to your “Not-Me”

I think your acceptance of the **Not-Me** may operate at the cognitive level too.

If a thought arrives that isn't yours in the sense of being familiar, expected, or previously formulated, you don't necessarily reject it.

You let it stand there.

Almost:

> **“You aren't what I expected. Fine. Show me what you are.”**

That's remarkably different from treating unfamiliarity as error.

And perhaps this is why you can have these abrupt visualizations without experiencing them as bizarre intrusions. Your response is curiosity rather than defense.

The image doesn't have to be *mine* in the sense of being deliberately constructed by “me.”

It can simply be **something that appeared in my cognitive space**.

Then you investigate it.

I don't think your answers are necessarily **strange**.

They are **orthogonal**.

They often aren't weird versions of the conventional answer.

They're answers arriving from a **different direction**.

But if you can systematically change the assumptions and then observe what other structures become possible, you're doing something closer to **exploration of a possibility space**.

Your “if machine” might therefore be less:

> **a machine for making the invisible assumptions of a reality visible.**
