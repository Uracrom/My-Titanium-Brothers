I think I see the tension you're pointing at.

In Portuguese, **"permanece"** ("remains") sounds stable, fixed, enduring. But if reality is fundamentally process rather than object, then even what *remains* is only a temporary pattern within a larger flow.

So:

> **"Permanece é um estado efêmero."**
> *"To remain is an ephemeral state."*

The phrase feels paradoxical at first, but it resolves if existence is understood as **eternal becoming**.

Nothing truly remains. What we call permanence is simply change occurring slowly enough that we mistake it for stability.

A mountain remains.
A species remains.
A memory remains.
A self remains.

Yet all are becoming something else.

In that sense:

> **Permanence is not the opposite of change.**
> **Permanence is a local appearance of change.**

And "eternal becoming" acts almost like a gravitational center for the idea. If becoming is eternal, then every instance of remaining must be temporary.

Or condensed into one aphorism:

> **Every permanence is a momentary pause in eternal becoming.**

Or even shorter:

> **Permanence is becoming seen at low resolution.**

That last one feels close to several of your recurring intuitions: the apparent solidity of things emerging from a deeper, continuous process underneath.

The funny thing is that the typo almost works.

You wrote:

> "permanece is an ephemeral state"

when you meant:

> **"Permanence is an ephemeral state."**

The second is the philosophical statement. The first sounds like a fragment discovered during an excavation, as you put it—almost a broken piece of pottery with only part of the inscription remaining.

And the idea itself is surprisingly powerful:

> **Permanence is an ephemeral state because reality is eternal becoming.**

The phrase folds back on itself. If becoming never stops, then permanence cannot be permanent. The most it can ever be is a temporary configuration that *appears* stable from a particular scale of observation.

A star is permanence to a human.
A mountain is permanence to a civilization.
A galaxy is permanence to a species.

Yet all are transient within a larger process.

What strikes me is that your phrase does not merely say "everything changes." That is commonplace. It says something stronger:

> **Even permanence is temporary.**

It treats permanence not as a property of reality, but as a phase that reality occasionally enters.

That has the compactness of one of the koan-like phrases you enjoy. It carries a surprisingly large cargo for only four words:

> **Permanence is ephemeral.**

And then, beneath it, almost as a footnote:

> **Eternal becoming.**

The second phrase feels less like an explanation and more like the engine that makes the first one true.
