This fits naturally with a recurring theme in your notes: the distinction between **experience** and **representation**.

From your body's perspective, the wind is *felt*. There is no viewpoint to choose—your skin is simply inside the event.

From your mind's perspective, the wind becomes something that can be *seen*. And as soon as it becomes an image, a new decision appears: **where is the observer?**

Your note also points to something subtle:

> "From inside you tend to look out. From outside you have to choose a starting point."

That suggests two different cognitive modes:

- **Internal viewpoint:** perception begins at the center and radiates outward. The observer is already embedded in the scene.
- **External viewpoint:** perception begins by placing a virtual camera. Before understanding the scene, the mind must decide *where* it is looking from—above, beside, behind, etc.

Your last question ("are you seeing the subject from above? From the right?") implies that an external representation is **underdetermined** until a viewpoint is chosen. The experience exists, but the image requires a coordinate system.

It's an interesting observation because it treats perspective itself as part of thought, not just part of vision. Before the mind can "see" an idea from the outside, it first has to create a place from which to see it. That matches many of your earlier reflections, where changing viewpoint changes the meaning of the concept itself.

This note continues the previous one, but it adds another distinction.

The first moment is almost narrative:

> "I looked up. I saw a star, looking at me."

Whether or not the star is literally "looking" isn't the point. Your perception is relational: the star isn't just an object in the sky; it becomes part of a two-way orientation. The scene feels like an encounter rather than an observation. The fact that something hides the other "eye" reinforces that interpretation because your mind organizes the scene as a face.

Then comes the second realization:

> "I perceived that I sense the wind with my body only, totally independent of the eyes."

This is a recognition that your body has multiple sensory channels operating in parallel. Vision is unnecessary for the existence of the wind as an experience. Closing your eyes wouldn't remove the wind; it would only remove one possible representation of it.

Together, these two observations suggest an interesting sequence:

1. **Vision** creates relationships at a distance ("the star looking at me").
2. **Touch/proprioception** creates relationships through direct contact ("the wind on my body").
3. Your mind can later build visual models of both, but the experiences themselves arise through different sensory systems.

In a way, you're noticing that consciousness isn't a single window. It's more like several independent streams that your mind later weaves into one coherent world. Sometimes that woven world is visual (the star with one visible "eye"); sometimes it is tactile (the wind); and your thinking often moves between the raw sensation and the geometric model you construct afterward. That transition—from experiencing to modeling—has become one of the central themes running through your journal.

The phrase **"Chimera view"** is evocative because a chimera is a single being composed of different parts. In your usage, it isn't about monsters; it's about **holding two realities together without forcing them to become one**.

A cleaned-up version of the idea might read:

> **Chimera view**
>
> I naturally see from the side.
>
> Both sides have equal value.
>
> My native vantage point is neither the left nor the right, but the position from which both can be seen simultaneously.
>
> They resemble mirror images, yet they are not identical. They are two realities standing beside each other, each defining the other without replacing it.
>
> That is my Chimera view.

What strikes me is the sentence:

> **"Both sides are worth the same."**

That isn't merely about geometry. It's a cognitive principle. Instead of seeking the "correct" side, your mind tends to preserve both until their relationship becomes clear.

Looking back over many of your notes, this fits remarkably well. You often think in paired structures:

- inside ↔ outside
- birth ↔ ascension
- body ↔ mind
- words ↔ meanings
- gradients ↔ membranes
- observer ↔ observed

Rather than choosing one pole, you tend to occupy the **space between them**, or the side from which both are visible. Calling that your **"Chimera view"** gives a name to a recurring pattern in your thinking. It's less about contradiction and more about maintaining two complementary perspectives in a single mental scene.
