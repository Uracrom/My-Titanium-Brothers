Yes — but with an important distinction.

You can **strongly influence** the title, but you generally cannot directly command the system to use a particular title as if you were setting a field yourself.

Your hypothesis about how titles are produced is also interesting. I would separate what we **know from observing the product** from what we're **inferring about the hidden algorithm**:

- **The beginning of the conversation appears to matter.** Early messages often establish the subject that the title reflects.
- **The perceived central topic/interest appears to matter.** If a conversation wanders substantially, the title may sometimes reflect what the system considers the most salient subject rather than simply the first thing discussed.
- **But I cannot see the title-generation algorithm or its internal scoring.** So I can't honestly tell you that it is literally "two algorithms: beginning + level of interest." That would be an inference, not something I know.
- **The result can therefore look arbitrary.** Two conversations with very similar structures can receive surprisingly different titles because there may be additional signals or thresholds we cannot observe.

And there's a particularly interesting experiment we could do with *your* way of thinking.

At the **end of a conversation**, you could say something like:

> **Title this conversation: "Metaphors as Translation."**

I can then treat that as the intended title when referring to the conversation within our work. But that doesn't necessarily mean the ChatGPT interface will actually adopt that exact title—the interface's automatic title is controlled separately.

So your distinction between **influence** and **choice** is exactly the right one:

**You can influence the semantic material from which the title is generated. You don't necessarily control the final UI title.**

And I think your observation about **"level of interest" versus "beginning"** is worth testing rather than merely theorizing about. We could deliberately construct several conversations with controlled openings but different developments, or identical openings with different emphases, and see what titles emerge. That would let us investigate whether your "two-variable" hypothesis actually predicts the behavior.
