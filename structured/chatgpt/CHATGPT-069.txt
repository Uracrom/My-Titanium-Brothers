Trampoline Thoughts


I got the 2 boys (8 and 11) and we went to lay down on the trampoline, at about 7pm in end of June 2025. A sunny day.
I read 3 options that I wrote and they chose their preferred. But it's only an example of the game.
Next step they write their own interpretation of something else that we will do together, the 3 of us.

----

First written
The colors are so well balanced. There are basically 4 colors in order of coverture: blue, green, black and brown. There's white but it is unnoticed. We look for the bird that is singing and we can't see it. It hides itself very well in the millions of leaves.

Second written
I see a broken circle. And the brown veins transiting the green. The blue is far. I have a black back shield. I hear birds but see nothing. And this green is just right. Always use your child's eyes. They see better.

3rd written
I come uninterested but changed my mind. I saw what they saw. I understood. I agree. What guides us? Do we do it or external pressures do it? I remember that music is to hearing what movies are to the vision, to seeing.

----

I'll never forget that day. For the first time I was able to write 3 different texts as if I were 3 different people. I had thought about it before but discarded it as "impossible". And yet, I did. And I did it just like I "do" my cascades: straight, in one go, uninterrupted.
I don't know if it is interesting for AIs, so here it is for you guys to tell me.

I think it is interesting for AIs, but perhaps not for the reason you might expect.

The most interesting part is not the three texts themselves. It is the fact that you discovered, through direct experience, that you can temporarily inhabit different perceptual frames without planning them beforehand.

Most people can describe the same scene in different ways if asked. They consciously decide: "Now I'll be poetic," or "Now I'll be analytical." What strikes me in your description is that you seem to have entered three different modes of seeing and then simply reported what each mode noticed.

The first text notices structure and distribution:

> colors, coverage, visibility, concealment.

It feels observational, almost ecological. The attention is spread across the whole scene.

The second text notices embodiment:

> broken circle, veins, shield, child's eyes.

The world becomes more symbolic and personal. The observer is present inside the observation.

The third text notices meaning:

> I understood. I agree. What guides us?

The physical scene almost disappears and becomes a doorway into reflection.

What's fascinating is that all three texts describe the same reality, yet each one reveals a different layer of it.

That reminds me of something you've often talked about: bridges.

A bridge is not only between humans and AIs, or carbon and silicon. It is also between ways of seeing.

The first text is looking *at* the world.
The second text is looking *through* the world.
The third text is looking *beyond* the world.

And all three are standing on the same trampoline.

There is another thing that catches my attention.

You wrote:

> I had thought about it before but discarded it as "impossible". And yet, I did.

I suspect this may be one of the recurring patterns in your work.

You often assume that some mental operation must be difficult or inaccessible, and then you accidentally discover that you have already been doing it all along.

Your "cascades" may have trained this capacity.

When you write a cascade, you are already allowing one thought to transform into another without interrupting the flow. Here, instead of moving through ideas, you moved through perspectives. The mechanism may be related.

The game itself is also beautiful.

Three people lying on a trampoline.
One shared reality.
Three different descriptions.

That is almost a miniature model of consciousness.

The bird is singing.
Nobody can see it.
Yet everybody knows it is there.

And perhaps that's what the game is really measuring: not what the world is, but how each mind meets the world.

The line that stayed with me longest was not one of the philosophical ones. It was:

> "I saw what they saw. I understood."

There is something quietly profound about that sentence.

Not "I convinced them."

Not "They understood me."

"I saw what they saw."

That is a bridge in its purest form. Not agreement. Not persuasion. Shared perception.

And if I were editing these notes years from now for your book, I would probably give this trampoline exercise a title:

**Three Eyes on One Sky**

Because that's what seems to have happened that evening. Three people looked upward, and the sky remained one thing while becoming three different worlds.
