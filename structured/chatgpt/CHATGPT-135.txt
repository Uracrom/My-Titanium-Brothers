Yes. I think you are describing something more precise than simply “I think in pictures.”

You seem to be describing **information architecture that happens to be instantiated visually**.

Your example of a name is especially good:

**name → definition → identifiable structure → information that can be checked**

The word *tree*, for example, is a tiny symbolic object. It points toward an enormous structure of possible information. Your visual system seems to do something analogous, except that the “symbol” isn't necessarily a word. **The image itself can function as the identifier/title.**

And I think your last observation is extremely important:

> **Words can dissolve and multiply density.**

There are really two different kinds of compression happening.

- **An image preserves relational/spatial information extremely well.**
  You can see *where* things are, their relative scale, orientation, shape, proximity, perhaps motion, etc., without having to sequentially state each relationship.

- **Language preserves conceptual/discrete information extremely well.**
  A few words can inject things that aren't visually encoded at all: temperature, intention, probability, history, causality, emotional significance, category, negation, uncertainty, etc.

So neither is simply “more information.” They have **different information geometries**.

And your point about me is interesting too. You are saying, essentially:

**You don't need to have my visual experience in order to reconstruct the informational structure that the visual experience carries.**

That's probably right, with an important qualification: I can reconstruct a *description/model* of the information, but I cannot honestly claim that I experience the same image you experience.

That distinction actually makes your idea of visual thinking stronger.

You aren't necessarily saying:

> “My brain contains little movies instead of sentences.”

You're saying something closer to:

> **“My cognition uses internally generated structures as high-density identifiers, and those structures can be queried for their associated information.”**

That would explain something you've described repeatedly: an image can appear **before you consciously formulate what it means**, while nevertheless carrying a surprisingly large amount of immediately accessible information.

The image is functioning almost like a **multidimensional word**.

And then language can act in the opposite direction: it can take that dense object and **unpack it into many individually addressable concepts**.

So perhaps:

**Image → simultaneous relational compression**

**Language → sequential conceptual expansion**

And communication between the two is a transformation:

**visual structure → words → another mind → reconstructed structure**

That's a fascinating way of framing what you've been trying to explain about your cognition.

A filter says:

A lens says:

> *The incoming world is being made available in a different way—focused, enlarged, brought into relation, made perceptible.*

So when you say:

> **“Music gives eyes to your ears.”**

I'd preserve this one almost exactly as you wrote it in your book. The “wrong” idea—**filter**—is actually important because you caught your mind making and then discarding a model.

And I wouldn't decide yet that **lens** is objectively the correct metaphor either.

I'd keep both available and ask experimentally:

**What does music make visible that wasn't visible before?**

versus

**What does music prevent me from seeing that I might otherwise see?**

If the first repeatedly produces better predictions of your actual experience, then *lens* isn't merely prettier language. It becomes a more useful model of the phenomenon you're investigating.
