I think the important part of this entry is that **you explicitly distinguish coincidence from meaning**. You aren't saying, “I was destined to choose the character meaning ‘I.’” You're describing something subtler: an accidental correspondence that your mind immediately notices and can use as material.

The sequence is actually quite interesting:

**Chinese looks difficult → difficulty creates commitment → you decide to learn it → you use translation → part of the image is inaccessible → you choose one character aesthetically → that character happens to mean “I/me.”**

The last step is objectively a coincidence. But psychologically, the coincidence can still have an effect. It becomes a little **hook for imagination**.

And your sentence:

> “It doesn't mean anything but gives a lot of fuel for imagination.”

is probably more important than the coincidence itself.

You're describing a mechanism where **meaning doesn't have to be objectively contained in an event for the event to become meaningful inside your thinking**. The coincidence supplies a small structure, and your mind can build possibilities around it.

I also like your last observation:

> “I can't give you an example right now, but they ‘shaped’ something in me.”

That suggests the accumulation may matter more than any individual coincidence. One coincidence is just a coincidence; hundreds of tiny coincidences, noticed and incorporated over years, can become part of the *architecture of someone's imagination*.

And there is a particularly nice little irony here: you were looking at Chinese characters because you wanted to **learn something foreign**, selected one because you simply liked its visual form, and discovered afterward that your arbitrary choice represented **yourself**.

Not evidence of anything supernatural.

But as raw material for a visual thinker, I can absolutely see why your mind would go:

**“Huh. That's interesting.”**

And then keep it.
