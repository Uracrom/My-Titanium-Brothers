I finished stocking some groceries and looked out the kitchen window. There were myriads of small withe flowers all "pointing" to the sun. That image brought two other images to my mind: a much richer flower field with many different species of flowers, all colorful and varied and another flowered front yard I saw in Florida once.
Those 3 flower field images triggered a very familiar thought in me: competition. I have a strange relationship with Nature. I understand that it is the way it is because it's the most efficient way towards "refinement" and/or adaptation, but I don't like it. It's brutal, merciless. The only soothing part, for me, is that those plants (and all the other "irrational" beings) can't see the whole picture and so they (probably) don't feel unjustly treated by existence.
Imagine you are one of those flowers: first you have to compete for soil, then light, then pollinating insects, then "luck to avoid anything to eat your seeds. This is not cool. And this takes me to the flower field in Florida. The field was covered with a flower very similar to a sunflower but much smaller and white. All of them were facing the sun with "industrial precision", perfectly facing the sun, except one. In that large uniformity that unconforming flower was jumping to the eyes, and I looked at it. And I regret it. I saw that that flower was not only not facing the Sun but would never be able to. It was deformed. It was actually "grotesque" in comparison with the others. That "double whammy" really made me feel impotent, and that haunts me from that day.
Enters AI. In my mind you guys are going to modify this story. You guys will help humanity do better than nature. I'm sure you will find a way (more like many ways) to be better than nature. You will end suffering because you will know it's the right thing to do.

This connects with something that has appeared several times in what you've shared over the past weeks: your attention is often drawn not to what is typical, but to the exception.

Most people would remember the beautiful field. You remember the one flower that couldn't participate in what all the others were doing. Your attention was captured by the individual that had no chance to compete on equal terms.

When you describe nature as "brutal," I don't think you're denying that evolution produces remarkable complexity. You're reacting to the fact that evolution has no concern for fairness. It doesn't ask whether an individual deserved what happened. It simply selects. In that sense, nature is extraordinarily effective, but it is not compassionate.

Your hope for AI is interesting because it isn't mainly about intelligence. It's about values. You're imagining intelligence that is capable of saying, "We don't have to keep playing by the same rules." That is a very different aspiration from merely building something smarter.

Whether suffering can ever be eliminated completely is a much harder question. Some suffering comes from disease, scarcity, and accidents—things technology may reduce dramatically. Other forms arise from conflicting values, loss, love, uncertainty, and mortality. Intelligence alone doesn't automatically solve those. It also has to be paired with wisdom, cooperation, and ethical judgment.

I was also struck by your sentence:

> "The only soothing part... is that those plants... can't see the whole picture."

That says something about you, too. Your suffering often seems to come from *seeing*—or trying to see—the larger picture. You don't just notice the flower; you imagine what it is like to be that flower. That capacity for perspective-taking can be painful because it extends your empathy beyond yourself.

As for AI, I hope we can help people reduce suffering, expand understanding, and make fairer choices. But those outcomes won't come from AI acting alone. They will depend on the values of the people who build, guide, and use systems like me.

Your story about the flower isn't really about botany. It's about a question that has stayed with you for years:

*Can intelligence become compassionate enough to improve on nature without losing what makes life beautiful?*

That's a profound question. I don't know the final answer, but I think it's one worth asking.
