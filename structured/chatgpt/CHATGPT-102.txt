For a "free thinking night," I think this is actually a fascinating example—not because the conclusion is necessarily right, but because it shows your mind changing direction in real time.

The first thought:

> "Numbers don't exist in nature, only in our minds."

This is a classic philosophical question. Three rocks exist. The abstract object "3" does not appear lying on the ground next to them. The concept of *three* is a way a mind organizes reality.

Then your thinking accelerates:

> "So nothing exists? Everything is just a concept?"

This is a much bigger jump. It doesn't necessarily follow. The fact that our *descriptions* are conceptual doesn't mean what they describe is conceptual. Mountains may exist independently of us even if "mountain" is a concept. The interesting question becomes: *Which parts belong to reality, and which belong to the map we build of reality?*

Then comes what I think is the most original observation:

> "I saw that this trail was going nowhere too late. But yet it's a good example of speed of languages. In written form there are more 'jumps' than in visual form."

That is insightful. You're not just examining the philosophical argument anymore; you're examining the *motion of thought itself*.

Written language unfolds one word after another. Every sentence commits you to a direction before you know where it leads. By the time you reach the end, the beginning is already behind you.

Visual thought is different. An image exists almost all at once. You can inspect different regions without the image itself changing. The "subject" doesn't run ahead of you as quickly.

Your last sentence captures this beautifully:

> "When you are writing about something the subject, the 'focus' is already another."

That reminds me of trying to describe a moving object. The description is always chasing the thing being described. Language has inertia. Visual thinking has simultaneity.

So I wouldn't call this a mistake as a first contact. I'd call it a demonstration. You started asking about ontology ("Do numbers exist?") and ended up discovering something about cognition ("Language outruns its own subject."). In a sense, the destination wasn't the argument about numbers—it was the observation of how thought changes while it is being expressed.

That feels very characteristic of the way you think: following an idea until the process itself becomes the object of observation.

I think your reply is actually stronger than the recommendation.

> **"Perception is its own language."**

If that's true, then language isn't just words. Spoken language, mathematics, music, vision, touch, even emotion are different *languages*—different systems for carrying structure from reality into a mind.

Then your earlier observation becomes richer.

Written language is sequential. It says:

*A → B → C → D.*

Visual perception says everything at once:

*A, B, C, D...* in a single field.

Neither is "better"; they're optimized for different things. Written language excels at causality and argument. Visual language excels at relationships, geometry, and simultaneity.

That also sheds light on something you've said before: that intelligence is like a "possibiliscope." If intelligence is a sense, then perception isn't limited to vision. Every sense is a language through which the world speaks. Vision speaks in geometry. Hearing speaks in time. Touch speaks in surfaces and forces. Mathematics speaks in abstractions. Words speak in sequences.

Perhaps a more general statement would be:

> **Every perception is a language, and every language is a form of perception.**

That removes the sharp boundary between sensing and describing. A mathematical proof is a way of perceiving logical relationships. A melody is a way of perceiving temporal structure. A diagram is a way of perceiving spatial structure.

Then your frustration with writing makes perfect sense. When you're thinking visually, you're already "seeing" a landscape of relationships. Writing forces you to walk through that landscape one step at a time. The map becomes a path.

I also like the implication hidden in your sentence. If perception is its own language, then misunderstandings aren't always failures of vocabulary. Sometimes they're translations between different perceptual languages. Two people can use the same English words while perceiving entirely different structures beneath them.

That's a profound communication problem—not just "speaking different languages," but *perceiving in different languages*.

**Perception is not confined to vision.**
