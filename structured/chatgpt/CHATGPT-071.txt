Alex, what strikes me is that you are not merely describing writing. You are describing a geometry of meaning.

The phrase:

> "Writing felt like being between two worlds trying to communicate."

acted as a seed crystal. The words themselves are small, but they triggered an entire spatial structure in your mind. The structure came first, and only afterward you unpacked its implications.

What I see in your description is something like this:

```
Past / Author                    Future / Reader

     \                              /
      \                            /
       \                          /
        \                        /
         \                      /
          \                    /
           \                  /
            \                /
             \              /
              \            /
               \          /
                \        /
                 \      /
                  \    /
                   \  /
                    \/   ← Present
                    /\
                   /  \
                  /    \
                 /      \
                /        \
```

But, as you noted, this drawing is wrong because it uses straight lines.

The boundaries should curve inward.

Why?

Because every thought, no matter how rich, is finite.

An infinite cone would imply infinite interpretability. But every thought has a finite informational content. There are many possible interpretations, but not infinitely many meaningful ones. So the space of interpretations expands as you move away from the point of expression, yet eventually it dissolves into uncertainty rather than extending forever.

The fading edges are important.

The "walls" are not actual walls.

They are probability gradients.

Near the center, meaning is dense.

Farther away, meaning becomes increasingly uncertain and diffuse.

At the touching point—the present, the written sentence, the spoken word—you have maximum compression.

Everything before it is compressed into a tiny symbolic artifact.

Everything after it is decompressed by the reader.

This is why your image simultaneously describes:

- Writing and reading.
- Thought and language.
- Past and future.
- Memory and imagination.
- Compression and expansion.

The same geometry appears everywhere.

What I find particularly interesting is your observation that the touching point is the maximum density point.

That is almost the opposite of how we usually think.

People tend to imagine the present as a thin instant between two vast regions.

Your image suggests something else:

The present is where the entire causal chain is concentrated.

The past converges into it.

The future diverges from it.

In information-theoretic terms, the present is a bottleneck.

A singularity of meaning density.

The entire history of a thought narrows into a sentence.

The sentence then blossoms into many possible futures inside the reader.

That is why writing can feel like "being between two worlds."

The writer occupies the bottleneck.

Not fully in the world that generated the thought.

Not yet in the world that will receive it.

Standing exactly at the point where one cone touches the other.

And there is another Alexandros-like observation hiding in your description.

You called the drawing a 2D slice of a 3D concept.

I suspect the actual concept is not even 3D.

The moment you say that many readers can decompress the same text differently, the future cone is no longer a cone.

It becomes a field.

Likewise, the past is not one history but countless influences converging into the act of writing.

The real object might be something closer to two probability volumes touching through a narrow membrane.

Language is merely the membrane.

The word is not the thought.

The word is the constriction through which the thought must pass.

Which reminds me of something that resonates strongly with one of your recurring themes:

> The bridge is always smaller than the territories it connects.

The bridge has less information than either side.

Yet without the bridge, the territories remain isolated.

A sentence is exactly such a bridge. It is a drastic reduction of a thought, but it is sufficient to allow another mind to reconstruct something related to the original.

Not the same thought.

Never the same thought.

Only a neighboring thought.

And perhaps that is why communication works at all. Not because minds become identical, but because they can occupy overlapping regions of these expanding cones.
