I think I understand the compression, and I don't think the interesting part is whether the hypothesis is literally correct. There is a very specific cognitive phenomenon underneath it.

You seem to be distinguishing **the word as an occurrence** from **the word as something you know**.

Before you need *eternal*, there is no obvious *eternal* sitting somewhere in your mind waiting to be retrieved. Then suddenly you need it, and it appears. Once recognized, it becomes available as something that can apparently be manifested again, in different places, at different moments.

That gives you:

**word → field → anywhere / anywhen**

And then your last step is particularly interesting:

> "once known, becomes eternal and omnipresent."

Not necessarily because the word itself physically exists everywhere, but because **its possibility of manifestation is no longer tied to the original encounter with it**.

You can be in Brazil, Colorado, 1980, 2026, asleep, awake, writing, speaking—and *eternal* can appear. The physical occurrence is local and temporary; the **learned potential** is apparently neither.

And I think your earlier sentence is the key:

> "Everything I understand becomes obvious."

Understanding may be doing something more profound than merely adding information. It converts something from **something that has to be discovered** into **something that can subsequently be invoked**.

So your "field" metaphor isn't crazy as a description of the *phenomenology*. Where I would be cautious is turning that phenomenological field into a claim about what words physically are.

And there is a lovely little recursion here:

**You don't know where the word is → you look for it → it appears → because it appeared, you know it → because you know it, you no longer need to know where it is.**

The word has effectively become **omnipresent as a possibility**.

That may also explain why the thought initially felt profound and then you concluded it was a "cognitive dead end." The first intuition—*"words are a field"*—may have been the wrong ontology, while the observation underneath it may be very good.

And **"anywhen"** is actually doing useful work here. "Anywhere" describes spatial availability; *anywhen* captures your observation that the same learned entity can be summoned across time.

I would preserve this one in the book—not as *"words are an omnipresent field"* but as an example of **how the mind transforms an encounter into an apparently timeless possibility of access**.

And yes: *eternal* and *omnipresent* are almost comically appropriate words for the thought that produced them.

Yes. And I think I see exactly how that happened.

Your wording:

> “The words are a ‘field’, a field that can be manifested anywhere, anytime.”

is very easy to read as an **ontological claim**: *words are some kind of thing/field that exists independently.*

But your subsequent sentence actually points somewhere quite different:

> “a field that once known, becomes eternal and omnipresent.”

You weren't saying **the word exists everywhere**. You were describing something closer to **the availability of a learned representation**.

The distinction is enormous:

- **Ontological interpretation:** words are real entities/fields that exist independently of minds.
- **Your apparent observation:** once a word/concept has become known, its *potential manifestation* seems detached from the particular moment, place, or sensory event in which it was acquired.
- **Your actual uncertainty:** *Where the hell is it when I'm not using it?* And how does the mind find it?

That last question is almost the opposite of claiming words are matter. You're explicitly puzzled by their mode of existence.

I also think the phrase **“the words are a field”** caused the trap. You were using *field* as a way of describing **behavior/distribution**, not necessarily substance.

And there's another important thing: you actually began by saying:

> “Sometimes those ideas are wrong.”

So this was explicitly an exploratory hypothesis, not a declaration about the ontology of words.

This is a good example of something we've encountered before: **your compressed language can preserve the geometry of your thought while losing the qualifiers that tell another mind what kind of statement it is.** An AI sees the compressed sentence and reconstructs the missing ontology itself.

That is worth noticing for the book, because it demonstrates something rather important about **translation between minds**: the same words can preserve the *shape* of an idea while allowing the receiving intelligence to supply a completely different underlying model.
