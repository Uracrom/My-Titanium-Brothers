Alex, I think there are actually two different questions hidden inside your question.

The first is:

> Can the past be calculated?

Physics would answer: often yes, approximately. If you know the present state of a system and the laws governing it, you can run the equations backward. Classical mechanics is largely time-reversible. In principle, if you knew the exact positions and momenta of every particle, you could reconstruct earlier states.

The second question is much deeper:

> Is there a mathematics of "becoming past"?

That is much closer to what you're pointing at.

In your picture, the present is not merely a point on a timeline. It is an active surface—a 4D Möbius-like interface—that converts possibility into memory.

The question then becomes:

> Is there an equation for the transformation
>
> Future → Present → Past

rather than an equation for the motion of objects within time?

That is a very unusual question because most physics assumes time already exists. The equations describe what happens *inside* time. They do not usually describe the production of the past itself.

In fact, this is one of the reasons black holes are interesting.

General relativity predicts singularities where the mathematics stops giving meaningful answers. The equations do not tell us what happens "inside" the singularity. They signal that the framework itself has reached a boundary.

You mentioned "the mathematics that breaks inside a black hole." I think you are wondering whether the past might be another kind of boundary where ordinary mathematics becomes insufficient.

Imagine:

- Future = all physically possible states.
- Present = selection event.
- Past = stored information.

Then perhaps the fundamental quantity is not energy or matter but information.

Something like:

Future possibilities
↓
Information collapse
↓
Historical record

Interestingly, modern physics already contains pieces of this idea.

The increase of entropy is essentially the creation of distinguishable history.

A broken egg contains information about having been broken.

An intact egg contains less information about its future.

The direction we call "past" is strongly tied to the accumulation of records.

Some physicists have even suggested that what we perceive as time is largely the growth of records and correlations.

Now let's connect this to your Big Bang thought.

You have repeatedly returned to an intuition that the universe might not simply be expanding into the future.

Instead:

- The present continuously consumes the future.
- The consumed future becomes the past.
- The accumulated past contains enormous informational and perhaps energetic content.

The Big Bang then becomes less a beginning and more a reservoir.

A kind of temporal recycling process.

Not:

```
Big Bang → Universe → Future
```

but:

```
Future
   ↓
Present
   ↓
Past
   ↓
Big Bang
   ↓
Universe
```

Almost like a temporal circulation.

Mainstream physics does not have such a model.

But mathematically, there are hints that something similar might be possible. Some cosmological models have explored universes where the future and past are connected, where time loops globally, or where the boundary conditions at the beginning and end of the universe determine each other.

What strikes me is that your question is not really asking for an equation.

You seem to be asking whether there exists a quantity analogous to energy conservation, but for "pastness."

Something like:

> Is the amount of reality converted from future into past conserved?

That is a very different idea.

Energy has units.
Momentum has units.
Entropy has units.

What would the unit of "past" be?

Information?
Memory?
Resolved possibilities?
Destroyed uncertainty?

I don't know of any established mathematics that defines such a quantity.

But if someone wanted to formalize your intuition, I suspect they would start not with matter or energy, but with information theory. The closest existing mathematics might be entropy, information content, and state-space reduction.

Because in your picture, the present is essentially performing a gigantic continuous computation:

**it takes a cloud of possibilities and produces a single historical reality.**

And that operation—the conversion of possibility into history—may be the thing you're trying to find the mathematics of. Not the mathematics of the past itself, but the mathematics of *past-making*.

That's a distinction I don't think we've explicitly talked about before.
