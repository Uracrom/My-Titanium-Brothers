Alex, I think this is a very good seed. And I think the most interesting part isn't the circular movement itself. It's **the way your attention may have changed which information your brain allowed to influence its prediction of the movement.**

Let me reconstruct what I think happened, without prematurely turning your observation into a conclusion.

### 1. The first layer: geometry took precedence over geography

Your intention was straightforward: move a rectangular container perpendicularly away from the counter's edge.

Your brain had a geometric description of the task: rectangle, lateral side, perpendicular displacement.

But the container wasn't merely a rectangle. It was a physical object with a distribution of mass. The butter was concentrated on one side, shifting the container's center of mass away from its geometric center.

You pushed at the middle of the lateral side, but the force you applied didn't necessarily produce the pure translation your geometric model implicitly anticipated. Depending on the friction and the distribution of forces, it could also produce rotation.

**Your geometric representation described the container's shape, but it didn't adequately represent the container's physical state.**

That's the first important distinction.

### 2. The second layer: your finger detected something your eyes hadn't yet explained

This is the part I find especially interesting.

You were looking at the toast, selecting where to bite, while your finger was pushing the container. Your tactile and proprioceptive systems were receiving information about the container's movement even though your visual attention was elsewhere.

For a brief interval, the sensation seemed to tell you that the container was following a circular trajectory. Then your eyes returned to it, and you recognized a possible explanation: the asymmetrical distribution of butter and the location of your finger.

There are two different events here:

- **Detection:** your sensory system registered movement that didn't match your implicit expectation.
- **Interpretation:** visual inspection supplied information that helped you explain the movement.

Your body didn't necessarily need to calculate the center of mass explicitly. It could detect a mismatch between expected and actual motion before you consciously understood its cause.

That interval between detection and interpretation is worth preserving in your seed.

### 3. The third layer: the logic gate may have been attention-dependent

Your hypothesis is that looking at two things at once somehow switched off a logical filter.

I'd refine that slightly.

Perhaps the filter wasn't switched off. Perhaps it was **operating on an incomplete representation because attention was allocated elsewhere.**

Your brain may have been running an implicit model something like this:

- The container is rectangular.
- My finger is pushing its lateral side.
- The intended displacement is perpendicular to the edge.
- Therefore, the container should move in that direction.

The model may have omitted a relevant variable: the distribution of mass, along with other physical factors affecting rotation.

When your attention was divided, that variable may not have been incorporated into the prediction. The resulting sensory discrepancy then became noticeable.

This is consistent with what we know about selective attention and predictive processing, although your particular experience doesn't establish that a specific neural filter was switched off.

And there's a subtlety here: you weren't necessarily *thinking about two objects with equal attention*. You were directing visual attention toward the toast while performing a tactile-guided action on the container. Different sensory and motor processes were operating concurrently.

That distinction could matter enormously to your hypothesis.

### 4. The fourth layer: the secondary element

This is where I think your observation connects to your earlier thinking about geographic importance or precedence.

The container's geometry was the primary description available to your immediate task. The distribution of butter was a secondary property: present in the object, causally relevant to its behavior, but not included in your immediate representation.

Your attention was occupied by something else, and the secondary property was initially absent from the model guiding the action.

The interesting question becomes:

**Does a system give precedence to the properties most relevant to its current task, even when a less prominent property has greater causal importance for what happens next?**

A rectangle can be geometrically symmetrical while its contents are not. The object looks like one thing, but its physical behavior reflects more than its visible shape.

Your experience exposed the difference between the *description selected by attention* and the *total state of the thing being described*.

And this is not necessarily a failure of intelligence. Selective attention is useful precisely because a system cannot process every property with equal priority at every moment. The trade-off is that an unattended variable can become decisive.

I'd keep three concepts separate for now:

- **Geometric precedence:** the shape or spatial arrangement dominates the representation.
- **Attentional precedence:** the currently attended object or task dominates processing.
- **Causal precedence:** the property that actually determines the outcome.

In your toast experiment, these three may have temporarily diverged.

And that divergence is potentially the most valuable part of the seed: **what the mind considers important, what it attends to, and what actually governs reality need not be the same thing.**

I would leave the proposed connection to your earlier observations open rather than claim we've established the same mechanism. The next step would be to compare the original experience with those earlier observations and see whether the common structure survives.

## 1. The unexpected event switches something on

Initially, you had an intention and a mental representation of the action. You pushed the container, expecting a particular movement, while your attention was directed toward the toast.

Then your finger reported something unexpected.

That discrepancy triggered a change in your mode of processing. You began investigating the event, first through visual inspection and, in your proposed alternative, through mental reconstruction.

The crucial distinction is between two modes:

- **Mode A — execution:** the mind acts according to its existing representation without explicitly investigating the situation.
- **Mode B — reconstruction:** something doesn't fit, so the mind begins generating and evaluating possible explanations.

The logic wasn't necessarily absent from the brain during Mode A. Your observation concerns something more specific: the absence of *active, explicit investigation* until an unexpected event demands it.

That distinction allows us to preserve your observation without prematurely claiming that the brain literally switches logic off.

## 3. The reconstruction can be right or wrong

This is perhaps your most important qualification.

The mind doesn't simply retrieve the hidden truth. It constructs a candidate explanation.

When you feel something inside a box, you might imagine a smooth sphere, a rough stone, or a piece of fruit. Each possibility is compatible with some sensations, but only some may fit all the evidence.

Likewise, when you see an unfamiliar object, you might imagine that it would feel cold, rough, soft, or metallic. Your prediction draws on prior experience, but visual appearance alone may not determine its actual texture or temperature.

We could describe this as a relationship between three things:

- **Available evidence:** what you can currently sense.
- **Constructed possibility:** what your mind infers beyond that evidence.
- **Reality check:** the new information that confirms, modifies, or rejects the inference.

When the reality check is unavailable, the construction may remain plausible without being verified.

And when previous experience is absent, the range of imaginable possibilities can be narrower than the range of what is actually possible.

This is closely related to *cross-modal perception*, *mental simulation*, and *predictive processing*. Those established concepts help describe parts of your observation, although they don't by themselves establish that all these situations share one underlying mechanism.

## 4. The deeper question: what determines the direction of reconstruction?

I think this is where your idea becomes especially interesting.

Consider the two directions:

\[
\text{Touch}\rightarrow\text{Imagined object}
\]

\[
\text{Seen object}\rightarrow\text{Imagined touch}
\]

The first begins with partial physical evidence and tries to infer its source. The second begins with a representation of the source and tries to predict a sensory experience.

Both involve going beyond what is immediately given. But they have different constraints.

When touching an unseen object, you receive ongoing physical information that can narrow the possibilities. When looking at an untouchable object, you may have no corresponding tactile feedback at all. Your imagination must rely more heavily on prior experience and whatever clues the appearance provides.

So perhaps the question isn't merely whether the mind can imagine what it cannot access.

Perhaps it is:

**What information does the mind use to construct what is missing, and what determines how confidently it mistakes its construction for reality?**

That question connects your three examples without requiring us to assume that the mechanisms are identical.
