---

Now, about the idea itself.

I think "words are perspectives" is a richer statement than "words are labels."

Every word highlights some aspects of reality while leaving others in the background.

For example:

- "Home" and "house" can point to the same building, but they are different perspectives.
- "Child" and "son" can refer to the same person, but one emphasizes age, the other relationship.
- "Invade" and "enter" can describe similar physical actions while presenting entirely different viewpoints.

So a word is not just attached to an object.

It is a particular way of *seeing* that object.

Your orbit analogy fits this well.

Changing a sentence by replacing one word isn't necessarily changing the object being discussed. Sometimes you're simply moving to another vantage point around the same conceptual object.

Likewise, changing word order can shift which relationships become foregrounded, almost like walking around a sculpture and noticing different surfaces while the sculpture itself remains unchanged.

---

Something else caught my attention.

Your chain isn't made of separate beads connected together.

It's one continuous object with constrictions.

That suggests that, in your visualization, language isn't fundamentally discrete.

The words are local "bulges" in one continuous field of meaning.

Meaning comes first.

Words are temporary concentrations within it.

That's a subtle but important distinction.

---

Regarding the word "sword."

I don't think its identity necessarily matters. What matters is that it was a moving word rather than a static one. The motion itself may have represented a change in viewpoint.

The fact that "sword" simultaneously evokes the weapon and the fish is also interesting because your mind didn't insist on a single meaning before using it. It tolerated ambiguity until the higher-level insight appeared.

---

As for originality, I would separate two questions.

**Is the exact idea new?** Probably not. Philosophers, linguists, and cognitive scientists have proposed ideas that overlap with parts of it. Language has often been described as framing, constraining, or directing perception.

**Is your path to the idea original?** That is much more likely.

What's distinctive in your writing isn't usually the destination alone. It's that you repeatedly document the *birth* of an insight in real time, including the imagery, transformations, and sequence of attention that produced it. Most books present conclusions. You are trying to present the phenomenology of arriving at them.

That has value regardless of whether someone else has independently reached a similar conclusion.

In fact, if two people independently reach similar ideas through entirely different routes, documenting those routes can itself be informative.

Reading this, one sentence came to my mind that seems to capture what your visualization is doing:

> **A word is not merely a point in language; it is a viewpoint from which the landscape of meaning is observed.**

That feels very close to the geometry your mind presented before you ever put it into prose.
