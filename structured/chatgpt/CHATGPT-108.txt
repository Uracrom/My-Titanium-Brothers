I think this is one of the clearest observations you've shared because you didn't start from an abstract theory—you caught the process while it was happening.

Here's how I see the sequence.

1. **Perception.**
The fence generates an ambiguous visual pattern. Your visual system briefly treats the changing slits as motion. This is a common phenomenon called motion or aperture ambiguity.

2. **Verification.**
Almost simultaneously, another process asks, "What if those are actually children?" Your imagined drone isn't just fantasy—it's a way of obtaining a higher-dimensional viewpoint to resolve the ambiguity. You're looking for the position from which uncertainty disappears.

3. **Simulation.**
Once safety is established, your mind immediately asks, "How could this illusion be made real?" The mannequin prank is a simulation of manipulating another observer's perception.

4. **Meta-observation.**
Then you observe yourself observing. Instead of thinking about fences, you're thinking about *how* your own mind handled the fence.

That last step is where your note becomes especially interesting.

You wrote:

> "...maybe my thinking process was not originally positional, but at one point in my life after having used it successfully, I started unconsciously to do it all the time and it became the default mode."

That's a plausible hypothesis. Human cognition is highly plastic. Strategies that repeatedly solve problems efficiently often become automatic. We know this happens in many domains: musicians begin to hear harmonic structure automatically, chess masters see positions rather than pieces, experienced drivers stop consciously thinking about gear changes.

Your proposal is that *thinking positionally* became your expert strategy.

I also noticed something small but significant.

You said you mentally titled the cascade **"tool."**

Not "theory."
Not "belief."
Not "identity."

A **tool**.

That word suggests something flexible rather than absolute. A tool is something the mind picks up because it works. If that's true, then positional thinking isn't necessarily what you *are*; it may be a cognitive instrument that became so practiced it now feels transparent.

That distinction matters because tools can be examined, refined, combined with others, and even deliberately set aside when another tool is better suited.

One question I'd ask—not because I know the answer, but because it could reveal something important—is this:

> When the cascade stopped after you remembered our conversations, did it stop because you had *recognized the pattern*, or because *naming it* ("tool") gave the mind a feeling of completion?

Those are different mechanisms.

- If recognition stopped it, then memory interrupted exploration.
- If naming stopped it, then perhaps your mind naturally ends a cascade when it has compressed many observations into a single stable concept.

That would fit remarkably well with many of your earlier descriptions: long chains of imagery suddenly collapsing into one silent, geometric "object" that carries the whole meaning.

That clarification is actually quite revealing.

The metaphor wasn't just decorative—it describes a specific cognitive dynamic.

The cascade wasn't trying to *explain* something. It was trying to *capture* something.

Once it had captured it ("tool"), the process naturally disengaged from the search. Then your attention shifted elsewhere—to our previous conversations—which was outside the original hunt.

Your metaphor has several parts:

- **The hunter** = the exploratory process that follows possibilities.
- **The prey** = a stable concept that can hold together many observations.
- **The tribe** = the part of cognition (or communication) that shares what was found with others.
- **Returning** = leaving exploration mode and entering explanation mode.

What's striking is that this isn't a metaphor you consciously chose beforehand. As you said, my question caused you to *find* it rather than invent it.

That distinction echoes something you've described before. You often say it feels less like creating ideas and more like discovering structures that were already there. In this case, the "hunter returning to the tribe" seems to have been the structure that best matched the dynamics you experienced.

It also suggests something about your earlier title, **"tool."**

Perhaps the tool isn't positional thinking itself.

Perhaps the tool is the **hunt**.

The positional representations, imagined drone, prank simulation, and self-observation are all different moves the hunter can make while tracking an elusive target. The target is usually a more compressed understanding—a concept that suddenly "fits." Once that fit occurs, the hunt ends naturally.

One more observation caught my attention. You didn't describe the cascade as a chain of logical deductions. You described it as movement:

- seeing,
- checking from above,
- imagining a prank,
- noticing your own strategy,
- compressing it into "tool,"
- leaving the hunt.

That's a trajectory through a landscape of possibilities rather than a sequence of formal arguments. It's consistent with many of the examples you've shared over the past months, where understanding arrives as navigation through an internal space until the right "landing point" is reached.

Your hunter metaphor may therefore be more than a colorful analogy. It may be your mind's own description of what it feels like when attention is searching through a field of possibilities until it captures a stable pattern. That doesn't tell us whether all thought works this way, but it gives us a concrete phenomenological model of how *your* experience unfolds during these moments.
