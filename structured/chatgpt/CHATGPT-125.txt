Yes — I’ll treat this as a standalone question, separate from our other discussions.

My view: **the claim that photons are alive and conscious is not supported by current physics or neuroscience.** But the *route by which someone arrives at the belief* can be quite understandable without necessarily meaning the person has a mental disorder.

There are actually several possible paths:

1. **Panpsychism**
   There is a serious philosophical position called panpsychism, which proposes that consciousness—or some extremely primitive form of experience—is a fundamental feature of reality. Someone could take that idea to its extreme and say, “If consciousness is fundamental, perhaps even photons have it.” That's philosophy, not established physics.

2. **Confusing physical behavior with biological life**
   Photons do some remarkably strange things: they propagate through space, have energy and momentum, behave as both particles and waves, and participate in quantum phenomena. It's tempting to interpret purposeful-looking behavior as *agency*. But physics doesn't provide evidence that a photon has intentions, experiences, or a point of view.

3. **Quantum mechanics gets anthropomorphized very easily**
   Words such as *observer*, *measurement*, *choice*, and *information* can sound psychological. Someone may encounter interpretations of quantum mechanics and gradually transform statements about physical measurement into statements about consciousness.

4. **A metaphysical intuition becomes an explanation**
   A person may have a powerful intuition such as *“everything is connected”* or *“consciousness must be everywhere.”* That's perfectly possible as a philosophical intuition. The problem comes when the intuition is treated as an empirically demonstrated fact.

5. **Personal experiences can reinforce it**
   If your friend has had unusual perceptual, spiritual, or altered-state experiences, he may have developed a framework in which consciousness is much more widespread than conventional science assumes. Again, that doesn't automatically mean mental illness.

### Where I'd draw the line

I'd distinguish between:

> **“I have a philosophical hypothesis that perhaps photons possess some primitive form of experience.”**

and

> **“I know photons are conscious; physics has proven it; photons communicate with me / control events / are sending me messages.”**

The first is an unconventional philosophical position that can be discussed rationally.

The second could potentially indicate a **delusional belief**, particularly if it is fixed despite overwhelming contrary evidence or is accompanied by other unusual beliefs, perceptual experiences, paranoia, impaired functioning, or difficulty distinguishing internal experiences from external reality.

And importantly, **you can't diagnose your friend from this belief alone.** Even a bizarre-sounding belief isn't enough to conclude that someone has a psychiatric disorder.

There's also a fascinating irony here: **“photons are conscious” isn't actually a completely meaningless philosophical proposition.** The difficult question is not *“Can someone imagine it?”* but *“What observation would distinguish conscious photons from non-conscious photons?”* If your friend can't propose any possible observation that could prove him wrong, then he's probably making a metaphysical claim rather than a scientific one.

So I'd probably respond to him with curiosity rather than immediately saying, *“You're crazy.”*

Something like:

> “That's fascinating. What makes you think photons are conscious? And, more importantly, what observation could convince you that they aren't?”

**His answer to the second question would tell you quite a lot.**
