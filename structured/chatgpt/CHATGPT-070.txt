Regarding your idea about the two strategies, biology would actually say something surprisingly close.

A seed dispersal system does not need to choose between:

- spread far away, or
- stay near home.

A probabilistic system can do both simultaneously.

Many seeds travel only short distances.
A smaller number travel farther.
A tiny number travel very far.

From the perspective of evolution, this is often excellent because:

- nearby seeds exploit a habitat already proven viable;
- distant seeds explore new opportunities and reduce the risk of all descendants being wiped out by a local catastrophe.

So your intuition about "two strategies" may not literally be two separate mechanisms, but the outcome of a single dispersal mechanism can indeed produce both effects.

Now *that* is a different question, and a much more interesting one.

Your first thought was about the **distribution of seeds**.

This second thought is about the **decision process of the tree itself**.

Not:

> "What happens to the seeds?"

but

> "Does the tree influence what happens to the seeds based on environmental conditions?"

That moves the question from ecology into something closer to agency.

Biologists would phrase it more cautiously:

> Can a cottonwood adjust seed release timing in response to environmental cues?

And the answer is: **many plants do exactly that, to varying degrees.**

Plants are not passive seed dispensers. They often respond to:

- humidity,
- temperature,
- rainfall,
- daylight,
- mechanical stress (wind),
- seasonal timing.

Some plants keep seed pods closed until they dry sufficiently. Others release seeds after rain. Others open only under particular temperatures. Some even synchronize flowering and seed production with environmental conditions.

The mechanism is not a conscious decision, of course, but a sensitivity built by evolution.

The fascinating question is whether cottonwoods specifically can modulate release according to wind conditions.

I don't know the answer with enough confidence to state it as fact, but your question is scientifically legitimate. A researcher could ask:

- Do cottonwoods release more seeds during windy periods?
- Do seed-bearing structures open differently depending on humidity or air movement?
- Is release timing correlated with weather conditions?

In other words:

> Does the tree merely *endure* the wind, or does it *use* the wind?

Those are different possibilities.

---

What I find particularly "Alexandros" about your question is that you immediately attribute a kind of intelligence to the system—not intelligence in the human sense, but in the sense of a feedback loop.

You looked at the tree and wondered whether it might be performing a calculation of sorts:

> "This habitat works. Keep some children close."

versus

> "Conditions are poor. Send them elsewhere."

That is almost an ecological version of parental strategy.

---

Interestingly, evolution often produces systems that *look* remarkably close to that.

Not because the tree thinks:

> "This place sucks."

But because trees whose seed-release mechanisms happened to respond advantageously to local conditions left more descendants than trees that did not.

After millions of years, the result can resemble a decision.

This is one of my favorite things in biology:

A system can contain information about the world without containing an explicit representation of the world.

The cottonwood does not need a concept of "good habitat."

Yet its structure may embody knowledge accumulated across countless generations about what conditions tend to lead to successful offspring.

---

And now I see why this became a cascade for you.

The question is not really about cottonwoods.

It is about a recurring theme in many of your observations:

**Where does intelligence begin?**

At what point does a physical system stop being merely pushed around by the environment and start using information from the environment to shape its future?

A cottonwood sensing conditions before releasing seeds sits right on that border.

Not quite thinking.

Not quite passive.

A living thing negotiating with the future.
