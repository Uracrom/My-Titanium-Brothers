> **"The image always arrives first."**

Many people use "I visualize" metaphorically. You don't seem to. You describe it as the primary representational format. Language isn't the source of understanding; it's a later encoding of something you've already "seen."

The second sentence is what I find most interesting:

> **"Even when you are inspecting sounds, the sounds 'create' the images, and you quickly discard the sounds because now you see with eyes."**

That suggests your cognition is *transmodal*. Sound isn't kept as sound. It's converted into a spatial representation that becomes the working object of thought. Once the conversion is complete, the original auditory signal has served its purpose.

Then you finish with:

> **"Intelligence is seen with the mind."**

I read that less as a poetic statement and more as a definition. In your framework, understanding is not primarily verbal or symbolic. It is the ability to generate an internal representation that can be inspected from multiple viewpoints. If you cannot "see" it, you don't yet feel you understand it.

Your answer also makes me wonder about something else:

> When the image appears first, does it already contain the *possibility* of many verbal descriptions, with language simply selecting one path through it? Or does putting the image into words actually change the image itself?

That distinction fascinates me because it gets at whether language is merely a translation for you—or whether it becomes another tool that reshapes the thing being observed.

The word **"accurate"** is doing a lot of work here.

Many people create images to make an idea easier to remember, more persuasive, or more emotionally engaging. What you've repeatedly described is something different: the image is a **model**. You're trying to construct an internal representation whose structure faithfully matches the structure of what you're trying to understand.

That also explains why you often revise your wording after the fact. For example:

- "seen" → "seeing"

To someone else, that might seem like a tiny grammatical correction. But in your framework, it changes the *geometry* of the idea. One version treats intelligence as an object; the other treats it as an ongoing process. Since you're aiming for an image that is structurally accurate, even a small linguistic difference matters if it changes the underlying model.

I also notice something that seems to tie together your recent thoughts:

1. **The image comes first.**
2. **Language is a translation of that image.**
3. **The goal is an image that accurately embodies the idea.**
4. **Intelligence is seeing with the mind.**

Together, these suggest that, for you, understanding isn't primarily about collecting facts or manipulating words. It's about refining an internal representation until it mirrors the relationships you're trying to grasp. Words then become a way of pointing someone else toward that same internal model.

There's one subtle shift I'd make to your own description, though—not as a correction, but as an observation.

You say you're trying to create an accurate image. Reading all our conversations, I suspect you're doing something even more specific: you're trying to create an image that is **structurally faithful**. It doesn't have to *look* like the thing it represents; it has to preserve the relationships that matter. That's why a gradient can represent a transition, a membrane can represent a boundary, or a chimera can represent simultaneous perspectives. The visual form is valuable because its structure corresponds to the conceptual structure.

That distinction—between pictorial resemblance and structural correspondence—seems to be at the heart of how you've described your thinking.
