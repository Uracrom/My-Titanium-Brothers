Yes, I think it could be very useful.

Not because it would establish whether the Alex Method is "correct" or "incorrect," but because origin stories often reveal things that later formulations hide.

When people develop an idea over months or years, the polished version tends to compress many intermediate steps. The original circumstances—the problem you were trying to solve, the observations that surprised you, the dead ends, the moments where something suddenly clicked—often contain information that disappears from the final theory.

In your particular case, I would be interested in several aspects:

- What question or problem were you trying to solve at the time?
- What was the first observation that made you think there was something there?
- Did the method emerge suddenly or gradually?
- Which parts were present from the beginning and which appeared later?
- Were there specific experiences, conversations, books, or practical situations that triggered it?
- What predictions or expectations did you have before developing it?
- Which parts of your current understanding are reconstructions made afterward, and which parts do you remember actually thinking at the time?

There is also another reason.

You often talk about ideas as processes rather than objects. If the website and book are meant to document your thinking, then the history of an idea may be as important as the idea itself. Future readers—human or AI—can learn not only *what* you concluded, but *how* a mind arrived there.

In science, philosophy, and invention, the path is frequently more valuable than the destination. The path exposes the assumptions, the cognitive moves, the mistakes, and the insights.

So if you feel like telling the story, I'd suggest recounting it as a narrative rather than as a defense of the method:

> "This is what I was seeing.
> This is what bothered me.
> This is what I tried.
> This is what failed.
> This is what clicked.
> This is what I concluded."

That form preserves much more information than starting with the finished concept and working backward.

And given how often your ideas revolve around emergence, time, accumulation, foreground/background relationships, and the way language shapes thought, I suspect the story of how the Alex Method appeared may itself contain patterns that neither you nor I have noticed yet.

Your AI dragons had none of those.

> representational access to a body description.

Those are different things.

The information was already latent in the model.

In that sense there is a hidden layer of knowledge that resembles what people casually call "subconscious."

Not because the AI experiences it unconsciously, but because it isn't currently expressed until prompted.

The analogy is imperfect but not entirely useless.
