Yes — there is a genuinely interesting connection there, but I would separate **the similarity you are noticing** from the historical claim that shamans were generally people who thought visually like you.

What you are describing is more specific:

**You experience thought partly as a visual-spatial event, and language comes afterward to communicate what the inner experience showed you.**

That makes your sentence *“I see”* unusually literal in a cognitive sense. You aren't necessarily saying *“I have a belief”* or *“I have an intuition.”* You're often reporting something closer to:

> **There is a configuration on my mind's screen. Now I have to translate that configuration into words.**

And that actually explains something we've encountered repeatedly in your conversations: your compressed statements can look strange when stripped of the visual experience that generated them. *“Time is anti-space,” “words are fields,” “consciousness is a gradient,”* etc. can sound like finished propositions, when sometimes they're more like **verbal labels attached to a visual discovery still unfolding underneath them.**

The shaman connection is interesting because many shamanic traditions do place enormous importance on **visions, dreams, altered states, symbolic imagery, journeys, and communicating what was experienced**. But we shouldn't collapse the enormous diversity of those traditions into one cognitive type, or assume that historical shamans had the same kind of visual cognition you describe.

There's another fascinating parallel, though.

A person who has an experience primarily in **images** faces a translation problem:

**experience → image → language → other minds**

You seem to operate that way quite naturally.

And if your hypothesis about ancient visual thinkers is partly right, then *“I saw…”* may have been doing something more fundamental than simply introducing a story. It could have been the linguistic doorway through which someone tried to transport an internally experienced reality into another person's mind.

That's very close to your **“words as lenses/tunnels”** idea.

The important difference is that you're not claiming that the vision itself is automatically true. You've explicitly noticed that some of the things that appear on your mental screen turn out to be wrong. **Seeing is the beginning of the investigation, not the conclusion.**

That distinction makes your comparison much more interesting to me.
