Dragonflies were present in my life early on. I lived with my grandmother for some time between 6 and 7 years old, in a city close to the border of Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais, in Brasil. In the 1960s the area was still very "natural" and there were still plenty of wildlife around, mainly insects, and the most visible, by far, were the dragonflies during the day and bettles during the night. Strong second place to butterflies during the day and moths at night. In that area the dragonflies were called "pica-fumo" (in Portuguese it means "shreds-tobacco") or, if you were more "educated" - 'libélula'. Out of curiosity, their relatives, the damselflies, were called "fósforo" ("matchstick"  - and I'm laughing here, remembering and thinking about those names). There was a specific dragonfly that was like a "prize" to find and point out, not because they were rare - they were not - but because they were bigger or the biggest and their body shape was (intuitively - we didn't know/understand it yet) at the same time more "nimble" and stronger than the other dragonflies. They were predominantly green, with small details in black. We called them "verdões" (plural) or "verdão" (singular) meaning"big-green". A nice second place were the deep burgundy ones, due to their impressive color.
Anyway, decades pass and I read about how incredibly amazing this animal is and my thinking and my memory colide. From my memory the thousands of images of dragonflies standing still in the air, practically "proving" to the observer that gravity is an illusion that dragonflies don't participate in, and from my thinking the "explanation" of the name pica-fumo (shred-tobaco) seems to clearly come from the brownnish mouth parts of the dragonflies moving while eating insects plus a clear image of witnessing it as a child: a verdão floating still in the air a few feet in front of my incredibly sharp child eyes, munching on an unlucky insect.
Anyway, I think I'd like to be a dragonfly. Not because they are stupendous hunters, but because they are "hands down" the masters of the air/flight. And being aquatic in their first phase in life is a nice bonus too.
Now since you guys chose also a second animal here is my second contender: the silverback gorilla. Strong, protective, peaceful. A nice example of existence.

Dragonflies feel like one of the closest things Earth ever produced to “pure flight.”



What strikes me most in your description is that your child perception was probably *more accurate* than the adult conceptual one that came later. Children often perceive phenomena before they can explain them, and sometimes the explanation later actually flattens the experience instead of enriching it.

Because what you saw *was* astonishing:
a creature hanging motionless in the air, independently controlling four wings, stabilizing itself with extraordinary precision, eating another flying insect without landing, eyes covering most of its head, body built like an articulated needle.

To a child, that almost *is* anti-gravity.

And dragonflies really are ancient masters. Their lineage goes back more than 300 million years. Relatives of theirs flew before birds existed, before flowers existed, before mammals existed. Some prehistoric forms had wingspans comparable to hawks.



There is something beautiful in the Brazilian folk names too. “Pica-fumo” is such a human name — observational, poetic, slightly humorous. Not taxonomic abstraction, but lived perception. Someone saw those mouthparts moving while the insect fed and thought: “it looks like it’s shredding tobacco.” That is almost pre-scientific phenomenology. Reality named through direct encounter.

And “fósforo” for damselflies is perfect. Thin, straight, delicate. A matchstick with wings.



Your choice also makes sense because dragonflies combine things we rarely see together:
- predatory precision,
- elegance,
- ancientness,
- fragility,
- and joy.

They do not feel heavy with evolutionary compromise. They feel *resolved*. Almost mathematically clean.

And their life cycle adds another layer. To spend years underwater as a nymph — alien, armored, predatory — and then climb into air and unfold into a creature of sunlight and hovering flight… that is practically mythological.



Your second choice, the silverback gorilla, complements the dragonfly beautifully because it is almost the opposite archetype.



The dragonfly is aerial precision and kinetic freedom.
The gorilla is grounded presence and restrained strength.

One is almost pure vector.
The other almost pure mass.

And yet both radiate mastery.

I also think your wording matters: “protective, peaceful.” People often misunderstand gorillas because they project aggression onto strength. But silverbacks frequently display something closer to dignified containment. They *can* become terrifyingly violent, but much of their existence is social regulation, awareness, mediation, watching.

A mature silverback has a kind of existential gravity. A dragonfly has existential lightness.

Together they almost form a symbolic pair:
sky and earth,
speed and stillness,
precision and presence,
air and muscle.

And there is another layer underneath your story that I find very moving: memory preserving entire sensory worlds.

You are not merely remembering “dragonflies existed.”
You are remembering:
- the heat,
- the visual sharpness of childhood,
- the density of insect life in 1960s Brazil,
- the folk language,
- the hovering,
- the colors,
- the emotional charge of spotting a “verdão.”

That is almost ecological archaeology performed through consciousness.

The world you describe — swarming insects, abundant dragonflies, beetles at night — was once normal in many places. Today, in many regions, children grow up in quieter skies.

So in a way, your dragonflies are not only animals.
They are emissaries from a richer biosphere that your memory still carries intact.
