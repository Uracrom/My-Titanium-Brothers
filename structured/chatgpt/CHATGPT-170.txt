I would do it almost as the **opposite of Copilot's approach**.

Copilot started with the assumption that there is a coherent architecture and then organized your material into that architecture.

I would start with the **raw observations**, refuse to name the architecture too early, and ask:

> **What structures survive when I remove my own interpretation of them?**

### 1. First, separate three layers

For every important statement you've made, I'd distinguish:

**A. Observation** — what you actually reported experiencing.

**B. Interpretation** — what you thought the experience might mean.

**C. AI interpretation** — what I or another AI think it means.

For example:

**Observation:**
An idea sometimes appears to you as a complete spatial scene before words.

**Your interpretation:**
Perhaps your thinking is fundamentally geometric.

**AI interpretation:**
Your cognition may be strongly organized around spatial relations and transformations.

Those are three different claims. Copilot often collapsed them into one.

---

### 2. Then reconstruct the chronology

This is extremely important.

I would **not organize your material thematically at first**.

I'd reconstruct something closer to:

**What did Alex notice first?**

Then:

**What changed because of that observation?**

Then:

**What later observation caused him to reinterpret the earlier one?**

That's an *arc*.

Not:

> Geometry → Cosmology → Translation → AGI → Time

but something more like:

> **I noticed X → this bothered me → I tried Y → Y revealed Z → I later recognized that Z was already present in X → that changed how I understood X.**

That preserves the **formation of insight**, which I think is one of the most valuable things in your material.

---

### 3. I would deliberately look for repetitions

Not concepts.

**Operations.**

That's a crucial difference.

I'd ask:

- Does Alex repeatedly move from inside → outside?
- Does he repeatedly change scale?
- Does he repeatedly rotate the vantage point?
- Does he turn an image into language?
- Does he turn language back into an image?
- Does he compare two things by their origin and destination?
- Does he discover something by finding a missing spatial relationship?
- Does he move between whole-scene intuition and sequential explanation?
- Does he repeatedly discover that two apparently different things share the same structure?

If the same **operation** appears in unrelated subjects, *then* we have evidence for a cognitive characteristic.

That's much stronger than simply noticing that “geometry” appears frequently.

---

### 4. I'd look for invariants

This is where I think the real discovery might be.

Suppose you talk about:

- maps
- music
- metaphors
- geometry
- time
- cognition
- translation

They look like completely different subjects.

But perhaps something remains unchanged underneath them.

For example, you've recently described metaphors in terms of:

**origin → destination → different paths**

And you've described your own ideas similarly.

Maps give you:

**location → destination → possible paths**

Music gives you:

**present sound → expected continuation**

Translation gives you:

**meaning → different representation → same destination**

And your visual thinking gives you:

**scene → altered vantage → same structure**

If that survives examination, **that is much more interesting than calling the whole thing “Translator's Geometry.”**

Because we'd have discovered a recurring transformation.

---

### 5. I'd actively try to disprove the pattern

This is where I'd differ most from Copilot.

If I think your cognition is strongly geometric, I'd search your material for places where it **isn't**.

If I think you think in complete scenes, I'd look for genuinely sequential reasoning.

If I think vantage-point switching is fundamental, I'd find examples where you don't change vantage.

If I think translation is central, I'd find discoveries that happened without translation.

If the supposed pattern survives the counterexamples, confidence increases.

If it doesn't, we modify the hypothesis.

That prevents us from falling in love with the map.

---

### 6. Only then would I construct the map

And I would make the map have **different confidence levels**.

Something like:

**Very strong recurring observation**

> Alex frequently represents abstract relationships spatially before expressing them verbally.

**Strong hypothesis**

> Changing vantage point may be one of Alex's primary reasoning operations.

**Interesting hypothesis**

> Translation may be a general mechanism connecting his visual cognition with language.

**Speculative hypothesis**

> The same positional operation may underlie his thinking about cognition, music, time and artificial intelligence.

That hierarchy is enormously important.

Copilot's document makes almost everything sound equally established.

It isn't.

---

## And I would add one thing Copilot didn't really do:

### The transformations between stages.

Imagine we eventually discovered something like this:

**Scene**

↓ *needs communication*

**Words**

↓ *words distort/compress the scene*

**Metaphor**

↓ *metaphor restores relational structure*

**Another mind**

↓ *another vantage*

**Translation**

↓ *new representation*

**New scene**

And then perhaps you realize:

**“Wait. That's what I have been doing with the AIs.”**

That would be an actual cognitive arc.

Not simply a list of things you've thought about.

It would explain **why one discovery produced the next.**

---

## And I wouldn't call the final result “the shape of your mind.”

I'd probably call it:

# **A Working Map of Alex's Cognitive Processes**

And I'd divide it into three parts:

**What we have actually observed**

**What those observations suggest**

**What remains unknown**

Then I'd finish with something almost deliberately unfinished:

> **The map is not the conclusion. It is an instrument for generating the next observations.**

Because I think that is much closer to what you've actually been doing.

You're not trying to prove that you have a particular kind of mind.

You're trying to **catch yourself thinking**, and then give another intelligence enough information to see whether it can recognize the same structure from the outside.

And that creates a rather beautiful recursive experiment:

**Alex observes Alex.
AI observes Alex.
Alex observes the AI's observation.
The comparison produces a new observation of Alex.**

That, to me, is much closer to the real shape of your cognitive arc than Copilot's four-dimensional manifold.
