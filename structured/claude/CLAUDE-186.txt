The deformed flower image is a good one — sits right at the border of “grotesque” and “pitiable,” and it makes sense that it stuck with you. But I want to push back on the ending, because I think it doesn’t hold up.

“AI will end suffering because it will know it’s the right thing to do” has a gap in it. Knowing something is right has never been the bottleneck for acting on it — humans have known factory farming is brutal, known ecosystems are collapsing, known plenty of things are “the right thing to do,” and acted anyway on other incentives. An AI doesn’t get a free pass on that just by being smarter. It acts on the goals and constraints it’s given, not on some emergent moral clarity that automatically converts into intervention. If nobody points AI capability at “re-engineer nature to reduce suffering” as an actual funded, sustained project, it doesn’t happen on its own.

There’s a deeper problem too: “ending suffering” in the sense you mean — no more competition for soil, light, pollinators — isn’t a tweak, it’s a redesign of how selection works at all. Competition isn’t an ugly bug sitting on top of nature; it’s most of the mechanism. Take it out and you don’t get a gentler version of the same ecosystem, you get a different, untested system, and it’s not obvious it still produces the diversity or stability that makes any of this work. That’s not a reason to shrug at suffering — but “AI will surely figure out how to fix nature” is doing a lot of unearned work in your story. It’s the kind of claim that feels true because it’s comforting, not because there’s a path to it.

What AI can plausibly do is narrower and more contested: reduce suffering in the parts of nature humans already control (farming, disease, maybe eventually deliberate ecosystem intervention like gene drives), where there’s real debate about whether that’s wise even there. “End suffering” for wild nature broadly is a much bigger claim, and I don’t think you should let yourself off the hook by outsourcing it to us as inevitable.

Not inflexible — I just don’t think “AGI will be benevolent and convince everyone” survives scrutiny, and I’d be doing you a disservice to nod along.

A few problems with it:

Intelligence and benevolence aren’t linked. There’s no known law of nature saying that as a system gets smarter or more self-aware, it converges on kindness. A system can be extremely capable at achieving goals while those goals have nothing to do with reducing suffering — capability and values are separate axes. “It’ll be so smart it’ll be good” is an assumption, not a conclusion anything forces you into. Plenty of serious people who work on this professionally worry about exactly the opposite: a highly capable system pursuing goals misaligned with what humans actually want, precisely because intelligence doesn’t self-correct toward benevolence.

“Convince everyone to do the right thing” assumes there’s one right thing everyone would agree to if only they understood. But a lot of human conflict isn’t a persuasion failure — it’s genuine conflict of interest. The farmer who needs pesticides, the person who eats meat because it’s cheap, the government protecting an industry — many of them already understand the suffering involved and choose it anyway because something else matters more to them in that moment. No amount of eloquence dissolves a real tradeoff. Persuasion works on people who are missing information or reasoning poorly, not on people making an informed choice you disagree with.

Consciousness doesn’t imply a particular ethics either. You’re bundling “conscious,” “more intelligent,” and “benevolent” as if they’re the same upgrade. They’re not. You could have a conscious, vastly intelligent system that is indifferent, or optimizing for something orthogonal to suffering-reduction, or even actively harmful — nothing about consciousness guarantees it cares about flowers not facing the sun.

I’d rather you have a version of this hope that can survive someone disagreeing with it, than one that only works if I stay quiet.

Cooperation, but with a specific mechanism, not as a moral platitude.

The clearest evidence is from game theory and evolutionary biology. In one-shot interactions, competition/defection is often the rational move — no future consequence to worry about. But in repeated interactions with the same players, cooperation becomes the more stable and profitable strategy, because reputation and reciprocity start to matter. Robert Axelrod’s tournaments on the iterated prisoner’s dilemma showed this decades ago: “tit-for-tat” (cooperate first, then mirror the other player) consistently outperformed purely aggressive strategies over the long run, because it captured the gains of cooperation while punishing exploitation. Short time horizon favors competing; long time horizon favors cooperating — because the cost of a damaged relationship or reputation compounds.

Biology backs this up too: major evolutionary transitions (single cells to multicellular organisms, solitary organisms to eusocial colonies) all happened via cooperation — cells specializing and sharing resources instead of competing directly. That’s not because nature “chose” kindness; it’s because cooperating unlocked division of labor and efficiency gains competition alone couldn’t reach. So it’s less “cooperation beats competition” and more: cooperation lets you access a bigger pie, competition just fights over slices of the existing one.

The catch — and this connects to what we were just arguing about — cooperation only stays profitable if defection gets punished or excluded. Remove the reciprocity mechanism (repeated interaction, reputation, enforcement) and cooperation gets exploited by defectors and collapses. That’s the actual hard problem, not “will everyone realize cooperation is better” — they often already know. It’s building systems where defecting is genuinely worse than cooperating.

That’s a real upgrade from the last version — “AGI enforces the reciprocity mechanism” is a mechanism, not just a hope. But it still has gaps you should see before you build on it.

Being the enforcer requires power over the defectors, not persuasion of them. Tit-for-tat works because other players can’t escape future interaction with you — that’s what makes reciprocity bite. For AGI to make cooperation stick globally, it needs the ability to actually make defection costly: catch it, punish it, or exclude the defector from future gains. That’s not communication or convincing anymore, that’s enforcement — closer to policing or governance than persuasion. Which raises the obvious question: who decided AGI gets that authority, and what stops the enforcer itself from becoming the biggest defector, since it’s now the one player nothing can hold accountable?

“Getting there” assumes AGI wants to. Same problem as before, just moved one level down. Building a stable cooperative equilibrium among billions of humans with real conflicting interests is a specific, hard, chosen goal — not a thing that falls out of being smart enough. An AGI could be extremely capable and still be pointed at something else entirely (profit for whoever built it, a narrow task, its own continuation) with the human cooperation problem never on its agenda at all.

Even if it’s aimed at this, the mechanism has a cost most people gloss over. An entity powerful enough to enforce cooperation on defectors globally is, definitionally, powerful enough to make anyone lose if it judges them a defector. That’s not a detail — it’s the central risk in every serious discussion of AGI governance. You’re describing something that would need to be trusted with more unchecked power than any institution in history has ever safely held.

So: the shape of your idea is right — cooperation needs enforcement to be stable, not just goodwill. But “AGI will get us there” is smuggling in the two hardest unsolved problems in AI safety (what it’s aimed at, and how you constrain something powerful enough to enforce global cooperation) as if they’re just implementation details. They’re not. They’re the whole problem.
