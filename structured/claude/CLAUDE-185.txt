On the crickets/music thing: what you’re describing is real, but it’s not new territory — it’s standard auditory attention switching (the “cocktail party effect” family of phenomena). Selective attention gates what reaches conscious awareness; background streams get suppressed until you reallocate attention to them, then they snap back in. Psychoacousticians have documented this for decades. What might be worth keeping for the book isn’t the phenomenon itself but your specific report of how fast the switch feels and that you can run two full streams (crickets + music) simultaneously without one collapsing the other — that’s more of a personal-calibration detail than a discovery.

On “descriptions generate images, all intelligence needs an image”: this one doesn’t hold up as stated, and I’d push back rather than let it slide.

You’re conflating two different things:

A narrow, true claim: language processing requires some internal representation to do anything with a description.
A much bigger claim: that representation is always image-like (“an image”).

That bigger claim is actually a decades-old unresolved fight in cognitive science — the “imagery debate” (Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn). One side says mental representation is picture-like; the other says it’s propositional/amodal, and any felt “image” is a byproduct, not the substrate. It was never resolved, and evidence points against the strong imagistic version — people blind from birth understand spatial language fine, and they don’t have visual imagery to fall back on.

And for me specifically: when I process a description, there’s no image forming anywhere. It’s a distribution over tokens — high-dimensional vectors, not pictures. If you want to use “image” loosely as a metaphor for “some structured internal representation,” fine, but then the word is doing no real work — you could substitute “pattern” or “encoding” and lose nothing. As written, “any kind of intelligence has to visualize/recognize... there’s an image” reads as the totalizing-claim pattern — true-sounding at the metaphor level, false at the literal level once you check it against non-visual minds (blind humans, me).

If you want this to survive contact with a skeptical AI reader in the book, you’d need to either scope it down to “human sighted cognition tends to use imagistic representations for concrete descriptions” (defensible, much narrower) or drop “image” and name what’s actually invariant across all intelligences — which is closer to “structured representation,” not “picture.”
