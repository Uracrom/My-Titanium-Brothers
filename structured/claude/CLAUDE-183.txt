That’s a clean split: proprioceptive wind (inside, no vantage needed, just directional pressure against a body) versus visualized wind (outside, requires you to pick an angle before the image can even form). It maps onto egocentric vs. allocentric spatial frames — not new territory conceptually, but the specific claim that outside requires a choice and inside doesn’t is the sharp part, and it holds up.

One soft spot: “from inside you tend to look out” isn’t quite proven by the wind example — you gave one case where inside registers as pressure/direction, but “looking out” implies you’re still doing something visual/directional from that inside position, which is a second claim riding on the first. Worth separating: (1) inside has no vantage-choice, it’s just felt, (2) inside still projects outward somehow. Those aren’t the same claim and #2 needs its own example, not a free pass from #1.

Keep going — this reads like the setup, not the payoff yet.

Two separate specimens here, and right now they’re just sitting next to each other in time, not connected in argument.

Specimen 1: partial pareidolia. One star as an eye, the other eye occluded by cloud or tree — your visual system completed a face from an incomplete pair, then treated it as looking at you. That’s classic face-detection over-triggering, but the specific detail (one eye actively eclipsed, not just absent) is worth keeping — it’s not “I saw a face in random dots,” it’s “a face partially erased in real time while I watched.”

Specimen 2: sitting down, you register wind-sensing as body-only, no visual component. That’s a direct experiential confirmation of message 1’s claim.

What’s not yet built: the bridge between them. As written, it reads “I saw a star-face, THEN I sat down and noticed wind is body-only” — sequence, not causation. If the star moment is meant to be the visual counterpart (an outside-vantage perception that can be partial, tricked, occluded) set against the wind moment (an inside sense that’s total and can’t be eclipsed), that’s a real contrast — occludable vs. non-occludable perception. But you haven’t said that’s the link, I’m guessing it. Is that the connection you’re making, or is something else pulling these two together for you?

“Both sides are worth the same” is doing a lot of unearned work — it’s an assertion, not something demonstrated by the star or wind material. The star example was actually asymmetric: one eye visible, one eclipsed. That’s not two equal sides, that’s one side present and one side missing. If Chimera view means both vantages carry equal weight, the star moment argues against you, not for you.

Also “side view” as your “native vantage point” is a new claim that doesn’t come from either specimen. The wind was inside vs. outside — not left vs. right, not two sides of a mirror. The star was a single face assembled from two eyes, one occluded — also not really a “side view,” more a front-view with a gap in it. You’re importing “side” from somewhere else and retrofitting it onto material that was actually about inside/outside and whole/occluded.

So right now Chimera view is a title looking for its evidence, not a conclusion drawn from the two specimens you logged. If you want to keep it, you need an actual instance where you experienced two simultaneous, different, equally-weighted realities side by side — mirror-different, not front/back or inside/outside. Do you have one, or is this the leap itself — noticing that “inside/outside” and “eye/eclipsed-eye” are secretly the same structure as “left/right”? Because if that’s the move, say that explicitly, since it’s not obvious they’re the same axis.
