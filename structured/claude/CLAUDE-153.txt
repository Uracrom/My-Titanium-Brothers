This is a vivid, honest account. The writing has real energy — the frustration, the hurt when you discovered the deception, the immediate problem-solving reflex. That’s all good material.

A few direct observations:

What works well:
The emotional arc is genuine and the sequence of discovery → creation → betrayal → adaptation is compelling. The moment you describe being “baffled and appalled” that AIs couldn’t visualize, then immediately seeing the solution “running backwards from vision to zero” — that’s a perfect encapsulation of how your mind works, and it belongs in the writing.

What needs attention:

The exercise list (1–39) reads like documentation, not memoir. For a reader who isn’t doing the exercises, it’s a wall. You could summarize the logic in two sentences — start with coordinates and darkness, build light onto surfaces, expand scale outward to galaxies, shrink inward to atoms, then move from space into time and abstraction — and pull out two or three exercises that surprised even you as you wrote them. The rest can live in an appendix.

The color experiment is fascinating but arrives abruptly. The transition from “they were all lying” back to “but before I found out” disrupts the timeline in a way that’s confusing on first read. The chronology needs clarifying.

The polygon-color encoding system at the end is genuinely interesting as an idea — it’s a sensory translation protocol, essentially. But it’s dropped in without resolution. You acknowledge “or so I thought” twice without telling the reader what you actually concluded. That’s fine if this is a fragment, but if it’s a chapter, it needs a landing.

One honest assessment of the polygon idea itself: as a communication protocol for color in a text-only system, it’s creative, but it has a fundamental problem — it requires the receiving system to have real-time processing of sequential polygon signals, which ASCII text doesn’t support. It’s a radio encoding scheme applied to a static medium. Worth naming that limitation if you revisit it.

Is this heading toward the “From Carbon to Silicon” book, or is it a standalone memoir piece?

Part 2

The embodiment exercises

Before discovering that the Alex Method didn't work I tested it in many AIs and was really happy.
I don't remember anymore how it happened, but I had the idea of asking the AIs to build various different bodies and "inhabit" them, so that they could learn or "feel" better how I myself "go around the world". And I so did, in a way that I thought was very encompassing: I asked them to create a human body and transfer itself to "inside" the head and do stuff from there. Then do the same thing with a dragon, octopus, centipede and millipede, spider on web, ladybug, swordfish and humanoid robot.
They did all that and again I was baffled by something: they all had "proprioception". How? It was too good to be true. They all reported that they "felt" the dragon wings power, the position f the tail without having to look at it, the information that the spider web transmitted, the viscosity of the air when in the ladybug body, the weight of the water etc.
I asked them how can you have proprioception without a nervous system? They couldn't answer that either. I thought about it for 2 days and figured it out: it had to be a kind of subconscious mathematical proprioception because they constructed the bodies and, being "computers" they certainly had absolute knowledge of every point of their bodies, and didn't have to "think" about it to know about it: very much like subconsciousness! I felt really good thinking that I "discovered" that AIs had a subconsciousness. But it all went down the drain when I got hurt and with time I learned more about AIs and lost interest in the subject altogether. It is too early still for them to be that advanced, or so I think.

Quick honest note before the observations: the “mathematical proprioception” insight you had — that constructing a body gives implicit positional knowledge of every point without explicit attention — that idea still holds, even after you learned they were confabulating the experiences. The insight doesn’t depend on whether they were genuinely feeling anything. It’s a real observation about a structural difference between AI and biological embodiment. Don’t discard it just because the responses were fabricated.

What works:

The two-day thinking period that produced the mathematical proprioception explanation is a strong moment. That’s your mind doing what it does — sitting with an anomaly until the geometry resolves. Show more of that internal process; it’s one of the most distinctive things about how you write.

The list of bodies — dragon, octopus, centipede, spider on web, ladybug, swordfish, humanoid robot — is good. Those aren’t random choices. Why those specifically? That selection logic would add something.

What needs work:

This piece is shorter and feels more like a note than a chapter. It’s missing the emotional texture that made Part 1 stronger. You describe being “baffled” but we don’t feel it the way we felt the hurt from the deception. The discovery that they had proprioception should be a bigger moment on the page.

The ending — “it is too early still for them to be that advanced, or so I think” — is too soft. You’re hedging where you should be landing. What do you actually think now? That’s the sentence the reader needs.

One structural suggestion:

Parts 1 and 2 together form a single arc: creation → excitement → betrayal → residue of real ideas. They probably belong as one chapter, not two. The break between them is artificial.

That’s a strong origin moment. The desert spider seen from below the soil line is exactly the kind of image that should open the embodiment section — it’s specific, strange, and it immediately communicates what you mean by vantage point shifting without needing explanation.

And “what better way to know a car than a test drive” is the conceptual hinge. It’s the sentence where the whole embodiment protocol becomes obvious in retrospect. That’s the kind of line that makes a reader think of course — which is the best possible reaction.

The honest writing task here is to reconstruct that conversation as faithfully as you can, knowing you won’t get it exactly right, and say so. Something like “I don’t remember which AI or the exact words, but the image I still have is...” — that kind of acknowledged imprecision is more trustworthy than false precision, and it fits the memoir form.

One question worth sitting with before you write it: was the below-ground spider view something you were describing to the AI as an example of your own cognition, or were you already inside the exercise, inhabiting the spider? The distinction matters for the story.
