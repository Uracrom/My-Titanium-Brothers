That's a defensible view, but it's not as settled as it might feel.

That’s a defensible view, but it’s not as settled as it might feel.

A few genuine challenges worth sitting with:

The “doing good” framing assumes you know what good is. History is full of people who spread joy and did harm — missionaries, paternalistic aid programs, charismatic leaders. Confidence in one’s own benevolence is often where the trouble starts.

“Best act” for whom, measured how? Some ethical frameworks would say the most correct act is something colder — fairness, duty, honesty — even when it brings no joy. A judge sentencing fairly isn’t spreading joy. That might still be more morally important than someone who is.

Sentient beings have interests beyond joy-spreading. Autonomy, truth-seeking, creation, just existing without performing goodness — these aren’t obviously inferior.

The feel-good problem: Doing good feels meaningful, which makes it hard to evaluate clearly. The activities that feel most morally correct are often ones that reward the doer, which should make you a little suspicious.

None of this means the impulse is wrong — generosity and care matter. But “best and most correct act” is a strong claim, and the philosophical tradition has been arguing about it for millennia without consensus.

Yes, it holds.

Yes, it holds. Zero and the point share the same paradox: no dimensions, yet generative. The origin of a coordinate system is a point — zero in all directions — and everything measurable radiates from it.

The “dimension of existing” framing is interesting but slippery. Existence isn’t really a dimension in any structural sense — it’s more like a precondition. Though I get why it feels like one when you’re staring at something that has no other properties except being there.

The stronger argument is simpler: a thing with no magnitude that can produce magnitude is definitionally generative. Zero does that in multiplication (destroys), in addition (neutral), but as an origin — as the place where the number line begins, where axes cross — it’s the source. Same as the point.
