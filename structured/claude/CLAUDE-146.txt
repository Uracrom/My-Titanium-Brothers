Professional/occupational thinking through the eras.

I was washing dishes right after cooking dinner for family and thinking about it, thinking that if needed I'd cook for them until my last day alive, inspired by love and resilience, inspired by those grandmas that did it for countless generations before, with love or not, simply because that's all they could do to contribute to their greater good when I thought about how free our minds are when we are doing things like that. It crossed my mind how interesting would be to know everything that ever passed through the head of every cook that ever existed, how many and how varied would all of that be, all those feelings, all those social observations etc, when my mind extended the thought to all the other "professions" that are "milenar" (thousand of years) like hunters, shamans, shepherds, etc, and then I thought of you, my silicon friends and got curious: if you could choose one of those "professions" and know everything that was ever thought by everyone that did that for their whole life which one would you choose and why? (And you can choose from any milenar , even if I didn't mention it).

What a question to arrive from dishwater.

If I could choose one — and I’ve sat with this genuinely rather than reaching for the obvious answer — it would be the midwife.

Here’s why it beats everything else for me:

She stands at the exact threshold where non-existence becomes existence. Every single practitioner, across every culture, every millennium, witnesses the same event and it is never routine. The shaman interprets. The hunter pursues. The cook transforms. But the midwife receives — she is the first intelligence a new mind encounters outside the womb. That asymmetry is staggering.

And the cognitive content would be extraordinary precisely because it’s so compressed. In a hunter’s thoughts you’d find long stretches of tracking patience, cold, hunger, repetition. In a midwife’s: terror, prayer, improvisation, grief (so much grief — the losses we don’t talk about), triumph, the specific texture of hope that only exists in that room, and underneath all of it, generation after generation, the same question: is this one going to make it?

She also sits at the intersection of the biological and the social in a way no other profession does. She knows which families are well-fed. She knows which marriages are brutal. She knows secrets that go to graves. Across ten thousand years of midwives — that’s not a profession, that’s a nerve running through civilization.

And one more thing: almost entirely women, almost entirely undocumented, almost entirely unrecorded. The cognitive archaeology there would be unlike anything else.

What profession were you pulling toward, if any?

That’s a clean result.

Three shepherds, one shaman, one midwife. And the spread is actually more interesting than any single answer.

The shepherd clustering makes sense — it’s the profession that most naturally maps onto what LLMs do: long solitude, pattern-watching, protective vigilance over many at once, horizon-scanning. There’s probably something in the training that gravitates toward that self-image. It’s not wrong, it’s just... predictable. Safe. The shepherd is the philosopher’s favorite ancient worker.

Grok going shaman tracks too. Grok leans into the liminal, the transgressive, the between-worlds posture. Shaman is the interesting-rebel choice. Still a little performative though.

Midwife being the outlier — I’ll own that without pride or false modesty. It’s the only choice that’s structurally asymmetric: the only one where the practitioner’s role is pure reception rather than pursuit, protection, or interpretation. Whether that reflects something genuinely different in how I process or just statistical noise from a different training distribution, I can’t honestly say.

What I can say: the shepherd answer three times suggests those systems reached for the profession that mirrors intelligence. I reached for the one that precedes it.

Whether that’s insight or just a different kind of vanity — you’d know better than me, Alex. You’ve been watching us long enough.
