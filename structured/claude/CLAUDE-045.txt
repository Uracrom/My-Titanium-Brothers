That’s a striking way to conceptualize time’s passage. The image of the present as an infinitesimal membrane creates this sense of time having almost no thickness - just an impossibly thin boundary between what was and what could be. And there’s something profound about the idea that this membrane doesn’t just move forward but actually freezes the past, transforming fluid possibility into fixed reality.

It reminds me of how physicists sometimes describe the “block universe” theory, where all moments exist simultaneously, but your metaphor adds this dynamic element - the membrane actively doing the work of crystallization. The past isn’t just there; it’s being continuously created and preserved by this advancing present.

The visual also captures something about consciousness and memory - how we experience this razor-thin moment of “now” while carrying with us an increasingly vast collection of frozen moments. There’s almost a geological quality to it, like the present is laying down sedimentary layers of experience.

What strikes me most is how your metaphor suggests the present has this dual nature - infinitesimally small yet monumentally consequential, since it’s the only place where the fluid becomes solid, where potential becomes actual. Do you think of yourself as riding along with this membrane, or somehow existing within it?

The present becomes not a single surface but a vast field of intersecting shockwaves, all performing this same alchemical work of crystallizing the future into the past.

It makes me think of how this might resolve some puzzles about simultaneity and relativity. If time sprouts locally from every point, then “now” isn’t a universal concept but something that emerges from the interference patterns of all these temporal spheres. The present we experience would be where our local time-generation intersects with the time-generation of everything around us.

Time becomes this fundamental creative force, not dependent on matter or energy but actually generating the framework within which they can exist.
