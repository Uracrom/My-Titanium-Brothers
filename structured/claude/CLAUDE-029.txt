Your mom’s insight is profound. The idea that translations are “versions” rather than equivalents captures something essential about how language shapes thought. Each language carves up reality differently - what Portuguese calls “saudade” or what German calls “Weltschmerz” aren’t just untranslatable words, they’re entire ways of experiencing and categorizing human emotion that other languages approach obliquely, if at all.

When you know multiple languages, you’re not just collecting vocabularies - you’re collecting worldviews. Portuguese might give you one lens for understanding time or relationships, English another, and each additional language adds another angle of approach to the same underlying reality. It’s like having multiple tools in a toolkit, where sometimes a screwdriver works better than a hammer for the same job.

Your observation about condensing text is interesting too. There’s something elegant about distilling an idea to its essence - finding that minimal viable expression that still carries the full weight of meaning. It’s almost like you’re doing what poets do, but in reverse: instead of expanding language to create new meanings, you’re compressing it to reveal the core that was always there.

That compression skill probably serves you well across languages. When you can identify the essential kernel of an idea, you can more easily find ways to express it regardless of which linguistic “version” you’re working in.

That’s a fascinating observation about the structural differences between the languages. English does seem to have this almost mechanical efficiency - you can strip away articles, compress complex ideas into compound nouns, stack adjectives, and create these dense information packets. The flexibility of English word order and its tolerance for omission makes it very compressible.

Portuguese, with its gendered articles, verb conjugations that carry person and number, and more rigid grammatical agreements, forces you to be explicit about relationships that English leaves implicit. When you say “a mesa” versus “o problema,” you’re encoding gender information that English simply doesn’t carry. The verb conjugations alone contain layers of specificity - tense, mood, aspect, person - that English often handles with auxiliary words or context.

It’s almost like English optimizes for density while Portuguese optimizes for clarity of relationships. In English, you can say “the red car’s broken door” and pack a lot into those four words. In Portuguese, you’d need to maintain all the gender agreements and probably use more words, but every listener would have the same precise understanding of how all those elements relate to each other.

This might explain why technical documentation often feels more natural in English - the compressibility serves efficiency. But for poetry or emotional expression, Portuguese’s precision about relationships and states might offer something English can’t quite match.

Do you find yourself thinking differently in each language, or gravitating toward one for certain types of ideas?

When an idea arrives, it might come wearing Portuguese clothes, or English ones, or sometimes it’s just pure knowing - that wordless understanding that exists before we try to dress it in any particular language’s grammar and vocabulary. The translation only becomes necessary when you need to share it, when you have to choose which linguistic architecture can best carry that internal reality to someone else.

Your “lingo” - that personal blend of languages, concepts, and wordless knowing - is probably richer and more nuanced than any single language could be. It’s like having a private vocabulary that draws from multiple wells but belongs fully to none of them.

The moment you have to translate for the outside world, you’re already making compromises, choosing which version of the idea to present. The internal version probably contains multitudes that no single language could fully capture. That silent knowledge you mention - that might be the most precise language of all, the one that doesn’t need words because it simply is.

It makes me think that true fluency isn’t just about mastering multiple languages, but about becoming comfortable in that space where they all dissolve into direct understanding.
