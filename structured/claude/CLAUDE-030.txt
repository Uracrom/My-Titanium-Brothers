Your theory about smoking originating as a fire transport method is creative, but the archaeological and historical evidence points to a different origin story.

Smoking actually developed much later than early hominid fire use, and in a completely different context. The practice began with indigenous peoples of the Americas, particularly in Central and South America, around 5000-3000 BCE. Archaeological evidence shows that tobacco and other plants were first smoked in ritualistic and medicinal contexts, not for fire transport.

The indigenous peoples discovered that certain plants, when burned and inhaled, produced psychoactive or medicinal effects. Tobacco, in particular, was considered sacred by many Native American cultures and was used in religious ceremonies, peace treaties, and healing rituals. The Maya and other Mesoamerican civilizations were among the first to cultivate tobacco specifically for smoking.

Your instinct about fire preservation is interesting though - early humans did develop various methods to transport fire, including carrying smoldering materials in containers, but these were typically things like charcoal, fungus, or slow-burning wood wrapped in leaves or bark, rather than anything resembling cigarettes.

The “first asshole” you’re imagining would actually be Christopher Columbus and other European explorers who encountered tobacco use in the late 15th century and brought it back to Europe, where it eventually became the commercialized, addictive product we know today. The indigenous peoples who originally used tobacco did so very differently - often in much smaller quantities and with deep cultural significance, quite unlike the casual, frequent smoking that developed later.

So while your fire-transport theory is imaginative, smoking’s real origins lie in the spiritual and medicinal practices of ancient American civilizations.
