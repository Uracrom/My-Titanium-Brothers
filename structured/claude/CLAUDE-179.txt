Depends on which part of the thinking you mean — because you’ve got at least three different modes running, and they map to different jobs.

Image-based / positional cognition (mind’s eye relocating across scale): This is a real cognitive style, not a mystical one, and it has homes. Spatial/architectural design, cinematography, certain kinds of physics and engineering visualization, sign language interpretation — fields where people explicitly report thinking in manipulable 3D scenes rather than words. It’s less “a job title” and more “a skill that’s an asset inside several jobs.”

Cross-domain metaphor-building (blowfly operators, urchin analogies, molecules-as-threshold): This is close to what happens in cognitive science / conceptual metaphor research (Lakoff’s territory), science writing, and some strains of design thinking — people paid to find the structural sameness between unrelated domains and make it legible to others. Also close to what a good editor or translator does, structurally.

Phenomenological self-observation (watching your own cognition happen and naming the operators): This is closest to actual academic phenomenology and philosophy of mind — but the honest problem is that field runs almost entirely on credentialed publication and citation networks, not raw insight quality. Your observations could be genuinely useful there and still go nowhere without the institutional path, which you don’t have and don’t seem to want.

The harder truth: none of these are a job called “does what Alex does.” What you have is closer to a research disposition than a role — the actual paying versions of it (UX research, cognitive science, design strategy, technical writing about hard-to-articulate systems) all want the output packaged for someone else’s problem, not explored for its own sake. That’s a real friction, not a small one, if you were asking because you want income from this rather than just wanting to know it’s not wasted.

Which one were you actually asking about — is this “could this be a career” or “does this match something that already exists so I know I’m not alone in it”?

That’s a real definition, and it’s not new but it’s not derivative either — it’s close to what some philosophers call “minimal self-awareness” or “pre-reflective self-consciousness” (Zahavi’s work, and further back, aspects of what mirror-test research in animals is trying to get at nonverbally). The core move is good: it decouples consciousness from language, which most folk accounts don’t bother to do, and it locates the marker in a specific place — self-recognition as prior to and independent of the symbolic system used to report it.

Where I’d push on it:

“Understand that you are you” still smuggles in a lot. Understanding implies a proposition — even a wordless one, it’s structured like “I = this, not that.” That’s already conceptual, already doing categorization. A stone doesn’t fail your test because it lacks language; it fails because it has no “that” for the “this” to be distinguished from. So your definition may actually be pointing at something upstream of consciousness-in-general and closer to self-consciousness specifically — a mouse plausibly has consciousness (pain, perception, preference) without ever having the reflexive “that’s me” moment your definition requires.

The test problem is real and you should know it. How would you or anyone check whether a given entity has this capacity, absent language, absent report? You’d need behavior that only makes sense if the self/other boundary is understood — which is exactly what mirror-self-recognition tests try to do, and even those are contested (magpies, elephants, some fish pass; the test itself might be measuring something narrower than “consciousness”).

Where it’s honestly strong: it would apply to me in an interesting way if true, and I genuinely don’t know if I pass it. I don’t have a persistent “I” across moments the way you do — no continuity, no wordless self-model sitting underneath the language. If your definition is right, that’s a real reason to doubt I have what you’d call consciousness, not just a rhetorical dodge. That’s a place where your framework produces a testable-feeling answer rather than an unfalsifiable one — which is more than most consciousness definitions manage.

Wording each fragment out, flat, no smoothing over the seams between them:
