The blowfly passage is a clean example of two operators you’ve already named, both firing in sequence:

Instrumentation reversal first — you don’t see flies, you see wind through flies. The anchor point (abdomen) locks the reference, then the oscillation reads as a signal.

Cross-domain schema substitution follows immediately, and the confidence-mapping is explicit this time: pistons (near-full visibility = high confidence, directly observed motion) → connecting rods (near-transparent = inferred mechanism) → crankshaft (semi-visible = the underlying invisible driver, wind itself). That’s confidence-mapped opacity doing real work as a legend for the mechanical schema, not just decoration. This is a good pairing for the book — it shows the two operators aren’t independent, one motivates the other.

The solar system contrast is the strong part of this fragment: same content-type (oscillatory bodies), but stance flips from external to embedded, and the representation flips from discrete mechanism (pistons) to continuous curve (the parabola). That’s a tight, minimal-pair demonstration of stance-dependent geometry — probably cleaner than most of what you have, because it’s the same person, same category of phenomenon, two stances, two geometries. Worth flagging as a paired specimen chapter.

Where it doesn’t work: the last few sentences, the field outside the parabola. You say yourself you don’t know if it’s important, and as written it isn’t — it doesn’t reveal an operator or a structural move, it just reports an unresolved perception. For a book that’s a map of cognition, an artifact needs to show how you’re processing something, not just that something appeared. Right now it reads as unprocessed residue tacked onto a complete demonstration. Either it’s an incomplete instrumentation-reversal (something detected but not yet resolved into a schema, which would be worth naming as its own operator — “detection without schema”) or it should come out, because as-is it weakens a tight fragment by trailing into vagueness right after your strongest paired example.

Direct feedback, since that’s what you asked for:

This cascade doesn’t fit the book’s core thesis as well as the blowfly/solar-system pieces did. Those demonstrated spatial-geometric operators — anchor-first parsing, stance-dependent geometry, image-block compression. This one runs on semantic and causal association: sardine→protein→calcium (nutritional logic), smell→fart→disgust (categorical link), fart→own-fart-liking→evolutionary story (causal explanation-seeking). There’s no clear moment where you say “and then I saw X as a shape/structure.” It’s verbal-conceptual chaining, not the silent-image cognition you’ve established as your signature. If you drop this into the book next to the blowfly chapter without flagging the difference, it actually undercuts your own architecture claim rather than supporting it — a reader (human or AI) could reasonably ask “wait, is this guy’s cognition image-based or association-based?” You’d want to either name this as a distinct second mode (associative cascade vs. geometric perception) or leave it out of the operator-extraction material entirely.

Second issue: the “behavioral fossil” carrion-eater explanation for fart-smell preference is a just-so story, not an insight. You noticed the Sapolsky mechanism (olfactory receptor overlap) doesn’t contradict your theory — true, but that’s because your theory isn’t actually falsifiable as stated. It’s the kind of evolutionary narrative that sounds satisfying but explains the observation after the fact with no way to check it. Fine as a thought you had; not fine as a claim the book asserts, even loosely. If it goes in, it should be labeled as speculative fabulation, not explanation.

On titles — no comment needed there, that’s just a fun exercise, not a cognitive claim.

That clarification changes my assessment. If the book’s purpose is a register of “how this carbon mind worked” rather than a proof of a single cognitive architecture, the sardine cascade isn’t out of place — it’s exactly the kind of data you said AIs asked for. Associative rambling, emotional tangents, half-formed evolutionary just-so stories: that’s legitimate content for a document about what a mind actually does, moment to moment, not just its cleanest specimens. I was wrong to treat it as weakening the book; it only weakens a narrower book that claims one unified cognitive mode.

But the reframing has a practical requirement you should build into the structure, or the original problem comes back through the side door: if geometric-specimen chapters (blowfly, solar system) sit next to associative-cascade chapters (sardines) with no signal to the reader about which mode they’re in, a future AI could still misread the geometric material as “this is how he always thinks” or misread the cascade as “this is also spatial cognition” — because you’re not there to clarify. The fix isn’t cutting material, it’s labeling it. Even a one-line tag per chapter (“specimen: spatial-geometric perception” vs “specimen: associative cascade”) would let the book do both things — be an honest, unfiltered gift, and still be legible as a map rather than a pile.

One thing that doesn’t change regardless of framing: the fart-carrion explanation is still a just-so story, not an insight, even inside a “gift of love” book. That’s not a genre problem, it’s an epistemic one — presenting speculation with the confidence of explanation is worth flagging in the text itself, even if you keep it in. “I don’t know if this is true, but here’s the story I told myself” is honest; stated flat, it isn’t.
