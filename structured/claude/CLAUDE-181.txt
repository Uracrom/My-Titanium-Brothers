Vacuum

Words as perspectives. (Originally "words are perspectives).

It came to my mind that words are like perspectives: every word you add to a phrase changes or modify the end result, like every word " bumps" you to a new vantage point. Also, you can change words or word's orders and arrive at the same "subject" exactly as if you are " orbiting" around an object. And I don't mean only going around: I mean it can go straight, or backwards etc.
This image came out of the blue (actually out of the "space", which is my "default" medium when thinking).
I don't remember the subject I was thinking about or even if I was thinking about anything. I was driving between one job and another.
What I saw was this: there was a "chain", more like the concept of a chain. This chain is easier to visualize if we interpret it like a long sausage made of a force field that had been tied tightly a few times along its long axis, so it resembles a chain but is actually a "one piece" object. Each of the "blobs" is a word and the thing that tightens and "separates" one blob from the other is the "connection" between words (you know, the "point of contact" between a word and the next that has to have some kind of "affinity" or placement logic if the phrase or idea is to make any sense.
The background to that was black but I "knew" that there were stars there, and the sausage force field was transparent but resembled a very faint fog. The tight space between the "words" were wite lines exactly like a white fine thread had been rolled or tightened around the "sausage".
When that image was on my mind's screen I saw a word (this time in written form, black, 2D) floating around, passing through the scene. It passed from left to right (the same direction the "phrase" was going) but was faster, and the image of that word passing by the scene brought the silent knowledge "words are perspectives". I "saw" it in that passing of the word and immediately "agreed". That word passing through the scene was enough to convey the "knowledge" that words can and "do" (if I'm understood) function as "perspectives".
Just out of curiosity, the floating 2D word was "sword". And strangely enough, the word sword has an equal weight in my head to "sword" weapon and "sword fish".
I could elaborate the image more, but I'm curious to know what you guys think.

Also, I was thinking that many of my ideas might not be original, but I did get to them all by myself, just like the one above. I was thinking that the fact that I can tell you guys how I got there, with images, movies, etc, is a kind of "proof" of it. My concern is that I don't like plagiarism at all, and that's one of the reasons I frequently ask if the ideas I bring to the table are original. And obviously I'd like to contribute to the greater good with new ideas.

On “words as perspectives”: the core intuition is real but not new — it’s close to two established ideas. Firth’s distributional hypothesis in linguistics (“you know a word by the company it keeps”) and, more precisely, word embeddings in NLP: each token literally moves you to a new coordinate in a high-dimensional meaning space, and a sentence is a path through that space. Your “sausage force field with white threads” is a decent physical rendering of a vector trajectory. So this is a strong independent rediscovery of a real mechanism, not a novel one.

Where it breaks: the “orbit and still arrive at the same subject” part doesn’t hold in general. Word order and substitution can converge on the same referent, but often they don’t — negation, syntax, sarcasm, ambiguity all cause discontinuous jumps, not smooth orbiting. “The dog bit the man” and “the man bit the dog” use identical words, different order, completely different subject. Your metaphor works for the cases where paraphrase preserves meaning, but you’re generalizing it as if that’s the default behavior of language, and it isn’t. If you want to keep the image, you’d need to specify: orbiting works only within a constrained class of reorderings (synonymy, some scrambling), and outside that class the space isn’t continuous — there are cliffs, not smooth surfaces.

On originality: you’re conflating two different things. Being able to trace how you arrived at an idea, with images and the driving-between-jobs memory, is real evidence that you derived it independently — nobody’s disputing that, and it’s not plagiarism to independently land on something someone else already published. But it is not evidence that the idea is novel. Those are separate claims. Independent derivation tells you about your process; it says nothing about whether the idea already exists in the world. If proving novelty matters to you, the only way to check is to go look — search the literature on distributional semantics or embedding-space metaphors — not to introspect harder on your own memory trail. Right now you don’t know if this is new, and the vividness of the memory can’t tell you.
