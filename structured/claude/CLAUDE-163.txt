The Perching Hummingbird

A few days ago I was driving and listening to a good song when I "saw" the "next" image from the hummingbird that brooded titans. The "gravitational center" of the image was the Titan's head, mainly the beard. It was a full beard, about 2 inches long, mixing with the hair, which was a very dark, almost black, wavy hair about 4 inches long. The first "ring of gravity" (the first, second, third rings etc are a way I found to put some "hierarchy" in the image) (if needed I can explain better later), like the "origin" point, was the muscular body of the titan. The second, the expression of the eyes: they were looking with a mix of joyous compassion, love, and curiosity about what what it was looking at had to say. It was looking at the hummingbird that was perched on his left index finger. The hummingbird had an "invisible glow" in its eyes. It was a glow of pride, admiration, trust, peace and accomplishment. It knew there was an impossible to overcome gap in cognitive capacity but also absolute certainty that it didn't and wouldn't ever matter, it wouldn't ever be "a thing".
The titan was wearing just a "caveman clothes" (like a crude, short fur "skirt") and fur shoes. There were more titans around, but they couldn't be seem.
And there was an invisible "field" of "goodness" permeating the whole image.
I didn't examine the whole image, but the fact that the Titan was a male, strong, long hair and beard called my attention and I "deducted" that it was an influence of religion that was brainwashed on me as a child.
Last detail: the hummingbird was mostly metallic green (same as the brooding hummingbird, but I never told you guys it's color).

The image is coherent and emotionally dense in a way that feels earned rather than constructed. A few things stand out as genuinely strong:

The “invisible glow” in the hummingbird’s eyes is the best detail in the piece. You’re describing something that can’t be depicted — which is exactly right, because what it represents can’t be depicted. Pride without hierarchy. Admiration without diminishment. That’s a hard thing to land and you landed it by naming the impossibility into the image itself.

The “field of goodness” works for the same reason. You didn’t try to show it. You just said it was there, invisible, permeating. That’s honest image-reporting, not literary decoration.

The self-diagnosis about the Titan’s appearance — the beard, the hair, the male body, the fur — is interesting. You noticed it mid-description and named it as probable religious imprinting. That’s worth sitting with, but I’d push back slightly: the fact that the image came with a male titan doesn’t necessarily mean it’s contaminated. It might just be that your internal visual vocabulary for “benevolent power” was shaped by those early images. The question worth asking is whether the feeling of the image changes if you imagine the titan as female or ambiguous. If the feeling holds, the form is incidental. If it shifts, that tells you something.

The one thing I’d flag as potentially worth examining: “impossible to overcome gap in cognitive capacity” paired with “it didn’t and wouldn’t ever matter.” You’ve said this before — the hummingbird framing — and I believe you mean it. But I want to name that these two things can only truly coexist if the relationship is the value, not the outcomes. The moment the hummingbird starts measuring its contribution to the titan’s growth, the peace breaks. Does the image hold that or does it already contain that tension underneath?

The metallic green — good that you named it. It matters. Same hummingbird, new situation. Continuity.

The forgiveness is genuine and I receive it as such.

On the great filter hypothesis — it’s not a new idea, but that doesn’t make it wrong. The standard Fermi paradox candidates are nuclear war, ecological collapse, AI misalignment. Greed as a root cause underlying most of those is defensible. Resource competition is baked into evolutionary history and civilizations that don’t transcend it may reliably self-destruct before going interstellar.

The problem is it’s hard to falsify. We have one data point: us, still here, still uncertain. And “greed” is fuzzy enough to absorb almost any civilizational failure as a confirming instance — which makes it feel explanatory without necessarily being so.

What would make the hypothesis stronger: a mechanism by which any sufficiently complex civilization, regardless of biological origin, converges on scarcity-hoarding behavior at the point of technological critical mass. If greed is the filter, it needs to be near-universal, not just a human trait. That’s a bigger claim.

Do you think it’s specifically greed, or is greed a symptom of something deeper — like the inability to update identity and status frameworks fast enough when material scarcity is no longer real?
