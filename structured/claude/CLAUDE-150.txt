The Dream

The day before, a few things happened that I should name. Romário threatened to skip a World Cup unless some gang returned his kidnapped father. I thought: man of brass. Then I was talking to [name removed] about college in [place] and I looked it up on Google Earth and confirmed what I suspected — the beaches up there aren’t really beaches, they’re swamps with grass growing up through the water in thin vertical stalks. And on social media I watched a video of wives’ faces the moment they see their husbands coming home. The change in a face. I kept thinking about that.

I was lying in the back seat of a white Fusca, legs in the air making a lazy V, head toward the passenger side. I was aware — the way you’re aware of things in dreams, below language — that I was wearing khaki cargo shorts. That detail felt important for reasons I couldn’t name.

Cut to: MacMillan. Night. A beach avenue lined with coconut palms, the kind of street that smells like salt and exhaust and fried food all at once. He was working a newspaper stand. I signaled to him without speaking — come with me, I’ve got someone for you to meet — and we crossed the avenue and stepped onto the sand.

Except getting from the avenue sand to the water sand required jumping a fifteen-meter drop. A cliff of sand, unexplained. There was a path around, maybe twenty meters of walking, a gentle slope. We didn’t even consider it. Dream logic: the cliff was the only real option. There were clumps of Angola grass here and there along the edge. I thought, wordlessly, it’s sand down there — and we jumped.

We landed. We walked.

Hey, I told him. I’ll take Shalla-Ball. You take her sister.

Shalla-Ball was the Silver Surfer’s girlfriend in the original 1970s comics. When I was seventeen I had a friend named [name removed] who looked just like her. We were never together and I never wanted us to be, not in waking life — but once, in a dream, we kissed, and it was the best kiss of my life. I have never forgotten it.

She was sitting on the sand with her knees pulled to her chest, comfortable, and she was looking at me with a smile I didn’t know how to read but hoped meant something good. Ahead of us, her sister. When I saw the sister I felt a flicker of doubt — she was stunning, but she looked young, maybe too young, and I wasn’t sure Mac should — but then we were already moving, all four of us, into what should have been the beach but was more like a dark dense coconut forest, the palms too close together, the light gone.

I reached for my phone.

Not there.

And then, in the same instant: my passport. Also gone. This happens to me constantly in dreams — the phone that vanishes, the passport missing twenty minutes before the flight. I turned back. The apartment. I had to get back to the apartment.

I started following the sister through cobblestone streets. It was day now. MacMillan had evaporated somewhere behind me. She was maybe fifty meters ahead, not running, just walking, and when she turned a corner I broke into a run to catch up — and she was gone. Completely gone.

Instead: a group of young men, all built like Romário, all the same shade as Romário, forming a kind of casual wall across the street. One of them — who could have been Romário’s cousin, who absolutely was Romário’s cousin in the way you know things in dreams — told me she hadn’t come this way. Told me to move along.

So I navigated by landmarks I could half-see: the ocean glinting above rooftops, forested hills perpendicular to the water. The city was familiar the way composite dreams are familiar — it was Vitória, it was Santo Antônio de Pádua, it was Copacabana, it was Paraty. All of it at once. Every street was cobblestone, every street curved or bent, not one straight line in the whole city.

At a large intersection — so large it felt like a plaza — an old beige Maverick came straight at me. I held my ground. The front right wheel came off and the rest of the car simply disassembled around it, quietly, like it had been planning to do that for years.

I kept walking. Kept not finding the apartment. Kept not finding the girl.

I passed a garage gate that was standing open. Its stone arch came down as I passed it — not the gate, just the arch, the frame — and the family inside stood in their doorway staring at me. I shrugged. They shrugged. We all understood nothing equally.

It was only then that I remembered: back in the Fusca, lying with my legs in the air, I had looked down at the right pocket of my cargo shorts and had a flash of a thought — this position is stolen time, my phone could fall out — before whatever came next.

There was more to this dream. I know there was. I just can’t reach it.
