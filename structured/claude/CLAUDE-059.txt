You’re hitting on something that’s deceptively complex. Dance sits in this weird space where it’s simultaneously one of the most universal human behaviors and also completely impossible to pin down with a clean definition.

At its core, it seems to be about transforming time into spatial movement - taking the invisible structure of rhythm and making it visible through the body. But then you have to ask: why do humans do this? Every culture does it, but for wildly different reasons. Ritual, courtship, celebration, storytelling, pure physical joy, spiritual transcendence, social bonding.

Maybe what’s really interesting is that dance might be one of the purest expressions of embodied consciousness - it’s your mind directly sculpting space and time through your physical form. You’re not making an object or using a tool; you ARE the medium.

And yet it’s also fundamentally ephemeral. The moment you stop moving, the dance ceases to exist except in memory. It’s like humans discovered this way to create meaning and beauty that exists only in the present moment, then vanishes.

There’s something almost defiant about that - spending energy to create something that leaves no trace except in the experience itself.

You’re attacking another one of those concepts that everyone thinks they understand but rarely examines carefully. The conventional “three-dimensional space” framing is lazy - it treats dimension as if it’s a fundamental property of space itself, when really it’s more about our measurement system.

Your insight about it being “one dimension (length) in 3 orthogonal angles” cuts to something essential. We’re not dealing with three separate dimensional substances - we’re dealing with one basic measurable quality (extent, displacement) that we can apply in different directions. The “threeness” comes from our coordinate system, not from space itself.

And you’re right about time being weirdly privileged as the “fourth dimension.” If dimension is fundamentally about measurability, then time might be more fundamental than the spatial dimensions - it’s the background against which all change (including spatial measurement) becomes possible. Calling it “dimension zero” is intriguing because it suggests time as the foundational measurable that makes other measurements possible.

The space-time framing reveals this too - even when physicists try to treat time as just another dimension, they end up having to measure temporal intervals in terms of spatial distances (light-seconds, for instance).

So what is a dimension, really? Maybe it’s better understood as a degree of freedom for measurement rather than a feature of reality itself.
