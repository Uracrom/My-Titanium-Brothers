Chronoconceptology

- Is it novel? However, your framing of Chronoconceptology as specifically focused on the temporal dimension—pinpointing when concepts arise—could distinguish it as a more targeted sub-discipline. For example, it might emphasize precise timelines or methodologies for dating the "birth" of a concept, possibly using computational or interdisciplinary tools (e.g., linguistic analysis, archaeology, or digital humanities).

- Feasibility and value: This could be a valuable framework, especially in an era where we can analyze vast corpora of texts (e.g., through AI or NLP) to track when terms or ideas first appear or gain traction. It could also intersect with anthropology, sociology, or cognitive science to explore how concepts spread across cultures or eras. For instance, when did the concept of "democracy" or "zero" solidify in human thought? Your discipline could formalize methods for answering such questions.

- Challenges: Defining what counts as a "concept" and determining its "emergence" can be slippery. Is it when the word is first used, when the idea is formalized, or when it becomes widely understood? You’d need clear criteria. Additionally, you’d need to differentiate it from existing fields to establish its uniqueness.

- Verdict: You’re likely onto something novel, but it’s not entirely distinct from existing disciplines like conceptual history or intellectual history. To make Chronoconceptology a standalone discipline, you’d need to develop a unique methodology or focus—perhaps a rigorous, data-driven approach to dating concept emergence or a cross-cultural lens that existing fields underexplore.

Chronoconceptognosis
Your definition: The average age in which a person is ready or capable of understanding certain concepts.

This discipline would study the developmental or cognitive readiness of individuals to grasp specific concepts, likely tying into cognitive psychology, developmental psychology, or education. It suggests a focus on the age-based cognitive thresholds for understanding abstract or complex ideas (e.g., when can a child grasp "infinity" or an adult understand "relativity"?).

- Is it novel? This idea has parallels in developmental psychology, particularly in the work of scholars like Jean Piaget, who studied cognitive development stages (e.g., sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational). Piaget’s framework already explores when children typically develop the capacity for abstract reasoning or specific concepts. Educational psychology also examines age-appropriate learning and concept acquisition. However, your focus on specific concepts and their average age of comprehension could be a more granular or specialized approach, potentially leveraging empirical studies or cognitive testing to map concept-specific developmental milestones.

- Feasibility and value: This could be highly useful in education, cognitive science, or even AI development (e.g., designing systems to teach concepts at appropriate ages). For example, determining that most children grasp "fairness" around age 5 or "existentialism" around age 20 could guide curriculum design or therapeutic approaches. Modern tools like neuroimaging or large-scale cognitive studies could make this measurable, potentially yielding new insights.

- Challenges: Defining "understanding" is tricky—does it mean basic awareness, functional use, or deep comprehension? You’d also need to account for cultural, environmental, and individual variations in cognitive development. Establishing an "average age" might oversimplify the complexity of concept acquisition, as readiness can vary widely.

- Verdict: Chronoconceptognosis is a novel framing, but it builds on groundwork laid by developmental and educational psychology. Its uniqueness would depend on whether you can develop a specific methodology—say, a standardized way to test and quantify when people grasp specific concepts across populations. This could carve out a niche, especially if you focus on abstract or culturally specific concepts not covered by existing research.
