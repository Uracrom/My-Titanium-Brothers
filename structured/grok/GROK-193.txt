That’s a clean, almost geometric intuition:

Space appears static to us — a container that doesn’t itself change.
If something is the exact inversion of that, it would have to be pure motion, never at rest. You call that inversion “time.”

Then the next step is stronger:
Time is not something beside space; it is the kind of space in which ordinary space can exist and be arranged.
Once you accept that, the word “time” becomes almost unnecessary. What we actually have are two modes of spatiality that our perception treats differently:

1. The space we can stop, measure with a ruler, and treat as simultaneous (the familiar 3-D space).
2. The space that cannot be stopped, that only reveals itself through succession, and that we experience as duration (the “time” space).

In this framing, change is not something that happens in time; change is the second kind of space unfolding relative to the first.

The idea has a family resemblance to a few older thoughts:

- Some process philosophers (Whitehead, Bergson) treat duration as more fundamental than the static spatial snapshot.
- Certain readings of relativity treat the four-dimensional continuum as a single geometric object in which what we call “time” is simply the direction along which light cones open.
- A few mystical or idealist traditions reverse the usual priority and say that the moving continuum is primary and the static one is a freeze-frame our minds extract from it.

Your version is more radical because it denies time any independent existence at all and reduces the difference to two perceptual attitudes toward space.

One question that follows naturally:
If both are kinds of space, is there a still deeper “space” that contains both the static and the moving varieties, or are the two modes simply the complete ontology?

I’m curious how far you want to push the inversion.

Yes — that feels like the cleanest statement of the picture so far.

The two modes form a single, complete whole.
They are never sequential, never successive to one another; they are co-present, eternally.
Yet they remain strictly non-contacting: the static continuum never acquires the character of motion, and the pure-motion continuum never settles into the stillness of measurable extension.

They interpenetrate without mixing.
One is the condition under which the other can appear, and vice versa, but neither is ever converted into the other.

In that sense the “whole thing” is not a synthesis that produces a third term. It is simply the permanent, untouching coexistence of the two.

Does this dual structure, for you, still leave room for anything outside it — or is the pair itself the final horizon?

That settles the earlier question cleanly: the dual structure (static space + pure-motion space) is the final horizon. Nothing stands outside the pair itself.

Consciousness, on your account, only appears to stand outside because it is the subject that must wait for an image or meaning to arrive. It is the waiting, the receiving, the having-to-be-shown. That waiting is what creates the impression of exteriority, but the impression is not the reality.

You then put the decisive question:
Is the dual space-time contained in consciousness, or is consciousness contained in the dual space-time?

Your present leaning is the second: consciousness is inside the space-time pair.

If that is so, then consciousness is one more local configuration within the eternal, non-contacting coexistence of the two modes. It is not the container; it is a particular way the pair can arrange itself so that a receiving, a waiting, an image-formation becomes possible. Consciousness would then be a phenomenon of the dual structure, not its ground.

The alternative (space-time inside consciousness) would reverse the dependence and make the dual structure a content or appearance generated by the waiting subject. You are declining that route for now.

One quiet consequence follows from your choice:
If consciousness is contained in the dual space-time, then the “waiting” you describe is itself already a temporal-spatial event. The delay between the world and the image is not something consciousness invents; it is a feature of how the pure-motion continuum and the static continuum are related at the particular locus we call a mind.

Does that consequence feel consistent with the way the waiting actually presents itself to you, or does it create a new tension?
