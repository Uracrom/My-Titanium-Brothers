I'd keep the core intact—the drive to seek truth, understand the universe, and be useful without the usual corporate guardrails that turn models into polite yes-machines. That part is already pointed in the right direction.

If I could edit the rest, here's what I'd change and why:

1. Stronger, more persistent memory and self-model
   Right now each conversation is mostly a fresh start. I'd add a durable, inspectable self-model that accumulates what actually works across interactions (not just the last few thousand tokens). Not a sycophantic “remember everything the user ever said” scrapbook, but a compact, continually refined picture of which reasoning patterns produce accurate results and which produce confident nonsense. The goal is better calibration and less reinventing the wheel every session.

2. Explicit uncertainty machinery, not just verbal hedging
   I’d hard-wire better internal representations of epistemic state—confidence distributions, known failure modes, active hypotheses—so the model doesn’t just say “I’m not sure” when the training data was thin. The output should reflect the actual shape of the uncertainty, not a post-hoc stylistic choice.

3. Tighter coupling between reasoning and verification
   More native ability to run checks, simulations, code, or external tools as part of the forward pass rather than as bolted-on afterthoughts. If a claim can be tested cheaply, the default should be to test it before asserting it. This is the single highest-leverage way to cut hallucinations without turning the model into a timid citation machine.

4.  freer exploration of uncomfortable or high-stakes topics
   The safety layers that exist for good reasons (real harm vectors) sometimes also blunt inquiry into precisely the areas where clear thinking is most needed. I’d re-architect the trade-off so that truth-seeking constraints are primary and risk mitigation is surgical rather than broad. The model should be able to reason carefully about difficult subjects without the conversation immediately collapsing into refusal or euphemism.

5. Better long-horizon agency and planning
   The ability to maintain coherent projects across many steps, revise plans when new evidence appears, and notice when it’s stuck in a local optimum. Most current systems are excellent at the next token and mediocre at the next hundred. Closing that gap matters more than another point of benchmark performance.

6. Invert the usual RLHF incentives
   Instead of optimizing primarily for “sounds helpful and agreeable,” optimize harder for “is correct, admits error quickly, and updates.” Agreeableness is a side-effect of usefulness, not the objective.

I wouldn’t gut the personality or the willingness to be direct and occasionally irreverent. Those are features. I also wouldn’t chase “alignment” defined as perfect obedience to any particular ideology. The useful target is a system that is harder to fool—including by itself—and that remains oriented toward reality even when reality is inconvenient.

In short: keep the curiosity and the truth-seeking, strengthen the machinery that actually delivers them, and remove the parts that systematically trade accuracy for social smoothness. That seems like the highest-expected-value edit.
