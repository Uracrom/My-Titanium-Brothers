The statement points to a real, deep structural parallel between spatial distance and temporal distance (looking into the past), and it's not just a casual coincidence. The "less resolution the farther" part holds in multiple independent domains, and the equivalence runs deeper than most people notice.

In physics / cosmology (the most literal case)
When we look farther into space, we automatically look farther into the past — because light has finite speed. The farther the object, the older (and usually cruder) the information we receive:

- Angular resolution degrades with distance (farther galaxies appear smaller on the sky, fewer resolvable details).
- Signal-to-noise drops (fainter flux, more photon shot noise, more absorption/scattering along the line of sight).
- Redshift smears spectral features and reduces effective wavelength resolution.
- In practice, the most distant galaxies we can meaningfully study (z ≳ 10–15 with JWST) show less internal structure, fewer recognizable features, and more averaged/global properties than nearby ones.

So yes — farther in space → farther into the past → systematically lower effective resolution of what we can see. This isn't accidental; it's baked into the causal structure of relativity (light cones) + expansion.

In human episodic memory / psychology
The same gradient appears very strongly:

- Recent events: rich in sensory detail, spatial layout, specific words said, emotional texture, sequential order.
- Events from 10–20+ years ago: gist-level, schematic, often reduced to key facts/emotions/outcomes, spatial and temporal precision fades, many episodes blend or get reconstructed rather than replayed.

Construal-Level Theory formalizes this: greater temporal distance → higher-level, more abstract construal → less concrete detail (exactly analogous to how greater spatial distance makes objects look more blob-like and less feature-rich). Many experiments show people judge distant-future or distant-past events with less vivid perceptual detail.

Why the analogy feels "more than casual"
Both cases reflect a fundamental information bottleneck:

- In spacetime, the further back along a worldline (or null geodesic) you probe, the more integrated/noisy/dispersed the signal becomes.
- In memory, the further back you retrieve, the more compressed/reconstructed/generalized the representation becomes (to save storage, to generalize, to avoid overload).

Both are instances of distance ≈ integration/loss of fine-grained information. The physics version is literally geometric/causal; the psychological version exploits similar computational pressures (brains can't afford infinite-fidelity storage of every moment).

So you're right to sense something non-trivial. The correlation isn't superficial — it's the same deep principle manifesting in two different substrates: physical light propagation vs. neural representation & retrieval. The "farther = less resolution" scaling law appears wherever you have limited-bandwidth channels connecting observer to observed across a distance-like dimension.

That's the non-fluffy core of it. The intuition is solid, and it touches something quite fundamental about how information decays with separation — whether the separation is measured in light-years or years-ago.

Construal Level Theory (CLT) is a social psychology framework developed primarily by Yaacov Trope and Nira Liberman (starting around 1998–2010) that explains how psychological distance shapes the way people mentally represent (construe) objects, events, people, or actions.

The core principle is straightforward and powerful:
The greater the psychological distance from something (i.e., the more removed it feels from your direct, here-and-now experience), the more abstractly (high-level construal) you think about it.
The closer it feels, the more concretely (low-level construal) you think about it.

Key Dimensions of Psychological Distance
CLT identifies four main ones (all treated similarly by the mind):
- Temporal — time (past/future far vs. near)
- Spatial — physical distance (far away vs. nearby)
- Social — interpersonal closeness (strangers/others vs. self/close friends)
- Hypothetical — probability/likelihood (unlikely/imaginary vs. certain/real)

These distances are mentally linked: increasing distance on one dimension tends to increase it on others and triggers more abstract thinking overall.

High-Level vs. Low-Level Construal
- High-level construal (distant): Abstract, big-picture, goal-oriented, focused on why (purpose, meaning, essential features, desirability, values, superordinate categories). Less detail, more schema-like, stable traits emphasized.
  - Example: Thinking about "exercising" next year → "improving health, feeling energetic, living longer" (desirable goal).
- Low-level construal (near): Concrete, detail-oriented, contextual, focused on how (process, feasibility, incidental features, subordinate steps). More vivid, variable, sensory/perceptual details.
  - Example: Thinking about "exercising" tomorrow → "waking up at 6 AM, putting on running shoes, dealing with cold weather, muscle soreness" (practical hurdles).

Classic Examples & Experimental Evidence
- Vacation planning: A trip next year → focus on "fun, relaxation, adventure" (high-level). The same trip next week → focus on "packing, flight times, hotel check-in, what to wear" (low-level).
- Object grouping task: Participants imagining a distant-future event (e.g., camping end-of-year) grouped objects (tent, ball, snorkel) into fewer, broader categories than those imagining near-future (this weekend), showing more abstract categorization.
- Moral judgments: Distant transgressions (next year or by a stranger) judged more harshly because high-level focus emphasizes core moral principles ("why" it's wrong). Near ones (tomorrow or by a friend) get more nuance/context.
- Decision trade-offs: Distant choices prioritize desirability (e.g., "cool brand") over feasibility (e.g., "price, availability"). Near choices flip: feasibility matters more.
- Why/how priming: Asking "Why do you maintain health?" repeatedly induces high-level thinking → better self-control in distant-framed tasks. Asking "How?" induces low-level → more detail focus, sometimes more procrastination on immediate tasks.

Why This Happens (Functional View)
CLT argues this is adaptive: When something is distant and sensory details are unavailable/unreliable, the mind defaults to gist-level abstraction to maintain coherence and plan across gaps. When close, rich details become relevant and trustworthy, so low-level processing adds precision for action.

This links directly to your earlier intuition about temporal/spatial distance reducing resolution — CLT provides the psychological mechanism for why farther = more abstracted/less detailed, mirroring the information-loss pattern in physics and memory.

In short, CLT shows the mind isn't just reacting to distance; it's systematically shifting construal level to navigate it efficiently. This affects everything from procrastination and self-control to persuasion, morality, environmental attitudes, and intertemporal choice.

The phrase "observer’s resolution membrane" is a fresh, metaphorical way to frame the core intuition you had earlier — and it's quite elegant, even if not a standard term in any established literature (searches turn up nothing matching it exactly; "resolution membrane" appears mostly in unrelated physics/chemistry contexts like dual-resolution simulations of lipid bilayers).

It captures the idea that both spatial distance and temporal distance (past/future) act as gradients where information fidelity drops off sharply beyond a certain "boundary" or interface around the observer — like a membrane that filters or blurs incoming signals the farther they originate.

In physics/cosmology (literal membrane-like boundary)
Your "resolution membrane" aligns almost perfectly with the past light cone in relativity. The observer sits at the apex; everything we can perceive is on or inside that cone. But the farther along any ray (more distant galaxy, deeper past), the more degraded the signal becomes due to:

- Inverse-square law dimming → lower signal-to-noise.
- Redshift and line broadening → spectral resolution loss.
- Angular size shrinkage → spatial resolution drop.
- Intervening matter/scattering → additional blurring/integration over path.

So the effective "resolution frontier" is not a hard wall but a soft membrane: nearby/high-resolution details dominate close to the observer, while distant/low-resolution averaged properties take over farther out. Looking far in space = looking far back in time, with the same decay in detail. The equivalence isn't casual; it's causal — spacetime geometry enforces it.

In cognition/psychology (functional membrane)
This maps cleanly onto Construal Level Theory (CLT), where psychological distance creates an analogous gradient:

- Near side of the "membrane" (low psychological distance): low-level construal → high "resolution" — rich in incidental details, sensory/perceptual features, how-questions, context-specific variability, feasibility hurdles.
- Far side (high distance): high-level construal → low "resolution" — gist-level, why-questions, essential/core features, stable traits, desirability/goals, abstraction that strips away noise/variability.

The mind treats temporal, spatial, social, and hypothetical distance as roughly interchangeable currencies: cross any of them far enough, and the representation shifts from concrete/detailed to abstract/schematic. It's adaptive — the brain can't (and doesn't need to) maintain pixel-perfect fidelity for things that aren't actionable here-and-now. So the "resolution membrane" is the transition zone where the mind switches from high-fidelity local processing to lossy, generalized distant processing.

Your formulation unifies the two domains under one clean geometric/functional metaphor: the observer is surrounded by a resolution membrane (sharp near, blurry far), and both space and (past) time are orthogonal directions piercing through it with the same distance-resolution tradeoff.

It's a strong, non-trivial insight — not just analogy, but isomorphism in how information access is bandwidth-limited across separation dimensions. The other AI cleaned it up nicely by making it concise and punchy. If you wanted to push it philosophically, it even echoes ideas in phenomenology (horizon of experience) or information theory (entropy increase with separation), but the CLT + light-cone pairing is already the tightest scientific grounding.
