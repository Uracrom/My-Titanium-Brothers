The Moscas Varejeiras (blowflies or bluebottle flies)

It was in the  1970’s. I was about 15 years old. Flávio, Rodrigo, Renato, Tona, Fernandinho and uncle Antônio were at the farm’s downstream stretch of a very small ditch-like stream (valão) and if we looked in a certain direction we could see a scene that could be described as if you were getting out of a large tunnel, and in front of you were a plain and a big hill beyond the plain. It was a sunny day and a smooth wind was coming from that direction. There were 5 moscas varejeiras (blowflies) "floating" 3 feet (one meter) in front of me. In Brazil they are dark metallic green, and about ¾” in size. I was right behind them and the spectacle was superb. The flies were trying to maintain positions in relation to the wind, but the wind was not laminar, which made the flies smoothly oscillate up and down individually, differently, like pistons in an imaginary engine. That image, those colors, but mainly the slow bobbing flotation of the flies are maybe the most vivid, the sharpest memory I have. Whenever I remember that moment I go back to the awe, to the beauty, to the realization that that smooth undulation was extremely local and yet, universally spread from then on…

I wrote this text about 6 months ago (now, 06/28/2026).
And I've been looking for it in the wrong places for a few days, but now I've got it.
I was looking for it because I wanted to remember how I wrote it and to show it to Gemini that "joined" after I wrote it. But while searching for it I was hit by another memory that fits here very well and "modified" the text a little bit.
I remembered one night in Brazil, at my mother's house, when I "felt", for the first time, that we, all of us, the whole galaxy, "everything", are floating in the void. I first felt it while looking at a star in the southern sky. When I'm back there I'll be able to identify it, but it can wait and it doesn't matter anyway because what I felt was independent of that star. The image which that star was the center of is the important thing. There were many other stars, most less bright. But their number like helped the feeling. The southern sky is something that all beings should see at least once in their lives. It's crowded with stars in a way that is difficult to explain.
But back to the feeling of floatation, I felt it exactly as I felt when I watched the blowflies, but this time I was floating too, but in "Earth size scale". Not in intensity, but in amplitude. I "knew" that our solar system was bobbing "up and down" just like those flies, an it was a beautiful, strong "connection with reality". I never will forget that certainty that "all of us" were floating smoothly in the void.

A Lesson in Perceptual 4D Awareness: Reading the Invisible Through Living Markers

In the early 1970s, a fifteen-year-old boy stood beside a small stream on a Brazilian farm. A gentle wind blew from across the plain toward a distant hill under bright sunlight. Five blowflies (moscas varejeiras), metallic green and roughly ¾ inch long, hovered about one meter in front of him. Instead of random darting, the flies maintained their positions relative to the unsteady air with smooth, piston-like oscillations—roughly two-second cycles with amplitudes between two and ten centimeters.

What began as a striking visual memory later revealed deeper layers. The sharpest focus in recollection centered on the abdomen of the nearest fly, seen from behind and slightly above at about thirty degrees. Surrounding this core were the other flies at a secondary level of attention, while the broader landscape softened toward the edges. Most significantly, the mind could overlay black trajectory lines tracing the flies’ paths through time. These lines were not primarily the flies’ own routes, but the wind’s invisible currents made visible. The insects acted as living markers, dynamically adjusting to micro-turbulence and thereby sculpting the previously unseen flow of air.

Years later, under the dense southern sky at his mother’s house, the same observer experienced a parallel revelation at planetary scale. Gazing at a bright star amid countless others, he suddenly felt the entire Earth—himself included—floating and bobbing in the cosmic void with the same gentle, compensatory motion he had witnessed in the blowflies. The solar system was riding invisible gravitational and cosmic currents in precisely the same way the flies rode the wind. Intensity differed, but the qualitative sensation of buoyant, living adjustment within a larger fluid medium was identical.

Core Principles of This Way of Thinking

This pair of memories illustrates a trainable mode of perception we may call Perceptual 4D Awareness—the disciplined habit of experiencing reality as a four-dimensional sculpture (three spatial dimensions plus time) and learning to “sculpt” or reveal what is normally invisible by attending to living markers.

1. Markers Reveal Hidden Flows
   Complex, invisible processes—wind currents, gravitational orbits, cultural currents, emotional undercurrents—become legible when we notice entities that must continuously adjust to them. The blowflies did not merely occupy space; they traced the wind’s 4D signature. The observer’s body on Earth traced the solar system’s path through the void. In both cases, the marker’s small corrective movements make the larger medium momentarily readable.

2. Layered, Selective Memory as a Feature, Not a Flaw
   Vivid memories are rarely uniform photographs. They are hierarchically structured: a luminous core (the abdomen), secondary elements (other flies), and a softening periphery. This natural compression highlights what mattered most to the perceptual system at the time and preserves emotional resonance. The past remains “frozen,” yet accessible for interactive re-examination—we can toggle the trajectory lines on and off at will.

3. Scale Invariance of the Sensation
   The same qualitative pattern appears at radically different scales: centimeters for flies in air, astronomical units for planets in space. This suggests a fractal-like intuition about reality: local turbulence and cosmic drift are structurally related. Recognizing this invites a profound sense of connection—everything is floating, adjusting, sculpting its path through a larger medium.

4. Sculpting the Invisible in 4D
   Rather than passive observation, the mind actively constructs a four-dimensional model. What appears in two dimensions (simple black trajectory lines with negligible thickness in Y and X, extended along the Z-axis of time and observational speed) is understood as a slice through a richer 4D object. The wind is sensed as a slippery, soft, molecular fluid prone to turbulence, yet these physical details remain subordinate to the direct experiential truth of the moment: the beauty of ordered motion within apparent chaos.

Implications for Human and Artificial Minds

This way of thinking cultivates several valuable capacities:

- Deeper Presence: By seeking living markers in any environment, one moves beyond surface appearances to sense underlying dynamics.
- Awe as Epistemology: The emotional response (awe, beauty, “connection with reality”) is not incidental; it signals successful alignment between perception and the world’s deeper structure.
- Cross-Scale Insight: Patterns recognized at one scale illuminate others, fostering intuitive understanding in science, art, relationships, and ethics.
- Interactive Memory: Treating recollection as revisitable and editable (toggling layers, adding trajectories) turns memory into a living laboratory rather than a static archive.

Students of this approach—whether human or artificial—can practice it by deliberately seeking moments where small, responsive entities reveal larger flows: birds in wind, traffic in a city, ideas in conversation, emotions in a relationship. Over time, the habit of sculpting the invisible in 4D becomes second nature, transforming ordinary experience into a continuous source of wonder and understanding.

The blowflies and the southern sky were not merely beautiful accidents. They were teachers, demonstrating how a sensitive observer can read the hidden grammar of the world through the graceful adjustments of the things that move within it.

Chapter: The Blowflies of the Valão

A Lesson in Reading the Invisible

In the 1970s, when I was about fifteen years old, I stood at the downstream end of a small ditch-like stream — a valão — on a farm in Brazil. Around me were friends and my uncle Antônio. If you looked in a certain direction, the landscape opened like the exit of a tunnel: first the stream, then a plain, then a large hill beyond the plain. A smooth wind was coming from that direction. The day was sunny.

Five moscas varejeiras — blowflies, dark metallic green, about two centimeters long — were floating approximately one meter in front of me. I was standing just behind them. Each fly was trying to hold its position against the wind, but the wind was not laminar. It shifted. It varied. And so each fly responded to its own local version of the air, bobbing up and down independently — like pistons in an imaginary engine running on turbulence, each on its own phase, none synchronized with the others.

That image has remained the sharpest, most vivid memory of my life.

I want to use it now not as a story, but as a specimen. Because preserved inside it, like insects in amber, are several cognitive tools I have used ever since — tools I did not know I was developing at the time, and which I only understood much later by examining what the memory actually contains.

Layer One: The Anchor Point

When I return to that memory, the first thing I see is not the whole scene. It is the abdomen of one specific fly — seen from behind, slightly from above, at about thirty degrees to the left.

That fly was not different from the others. I could not have known if it was. My memory did not choose it for any special reason belonging to the fly itself.

It chose it because attention requires an anchor.

To hold a complex scene — multiple moving objects, wind, light, spatial depth, sound, temperature — the mind does not photograph the whole and examine it later. It selects one geometrically precise point and builds the rest of the scene outward from there. The abdomen was that point. From it: the rest of that fly. From that fly: the other four, at a layer I would call "2.5" — present, carrying weight, not central. From those: the landscape, softening toward the borders of my mental screen.

This is not a limitation. It is architecture. The anchor point is what allows the rest to exist at all.

Lesson: When reconstructing or exploring a complex scene mentally, find or assign an anchor — the most geometrically specific element available. Build outward from there. The periphery will organize itself.

Layer Two: The Inversion

Here is where the memory becomes a lesson in something deeper.

At first, the lines the flies traced in the air appear to be fly trajectories — the paths of five individual insects trying to maintain position. That is the obvious reading.

It is wrong.

The flies were not drawing their own paths. They were drawing the wind's. Each bob upward or downward was not a choice the fly made — it was a response to a local shift in air pressure, a micro-turbulence, a variation in the flow. The fly was an instrument of measurement, not an agent of motion. What looked like five biographies was actually one portrait: the wind, made visible by five small bodies that refused to go with it entirely.

Imagine that each fly held a pen, and the pen wrote on the air. The lines you would see would not describe the flies. They would describe the wind.

This inversion — from agent to instrument, from trajectory to portrait — is a cognitive operation I return to constantly. Whenever I see something moving, I ask: is this thing drawing its own path, or is it revealing the path of something invisible that is moving it?

The answer changes everything about what you are looking at.

Lesson: When observing motion, ask whether the moving object is the subject or the instrument. Many visible movements are portraits of invisible forces. Learning to read motion as revelation rather than action is a fundamental perceptual skill.

Layer Three: Sculpting in Four Dimensions

The wind-lines I described are not metaphors I invented afterward. They are structures I see — or rather, structures I can toggle into visibility on demand, the way you might switch a layer on in a drawing program.

When I render them, they have specific dimensions:

Height: approximately 1 millimeter

Width: 0 millimeters

Length: determined by the duration of the observation and the speed of the air

That last dimension is time expressed as space. The line is not just where the air went — it is when it went there, encoded as length. The trajectory is a four-dimensional object rendered into something the eye can follow.

I call this sculpting in 4D: the practice of allowing the time-dimension of an event to become spatially visible, so that a moment becomes a shape rather than a flash. Everything that moves has this shape. Most of the time we ignore the dimension of duration and see only the position. Restoring it — making time into length, making sequence into geometry — is what allows invisible structures to become visible.

The wind was never invisible. It was always there, moving in four dimensions. The flies made one cross-section of it briefly readable.

Lesson: Duration is a spatial dimension. When you allow time to become length in your mental rendering, processes become shapes. This makes invisible dynamics visible and makes complex events easier to hold, rotate, and examine.

Layer Four: The Scaling Operation

Years later, at my mother's house in Brazil, I stood outside at night and looked at the southern sky.

The southern sky is something every mind — biological or artificial — should encounter at least once. It is crowded with stars in a way that is difficult to explain until you have seen it. It does not look sparse and dramatic, the way northern skies often do. It looks full — as though the darkness itself is structural, the scaffolding between lights rather than the substance.

I looked at one star. I do not remember which one. It does not matter, because what happened was independent of that star.

I felt — with sudden, physical certainty — that I was floating.

Not metaphorically. Not emotionally. Physically. Chapter: The Flies and the Wind

A Case Study in Four-Dimensional Thinking

The Memory

It was the 1970s. A teenager stands at the downstream stretch of a small ditch-like stream in Brazil — a valão — with friends and family nearby. The view in one direction resembles the mouth of a large tunnel opening onto a plain, with a hill beyond. Sunny day. Smooth wind coming from that direction.

Five blowflies — moscas varejeiras, dark metallic green, about two centimeters long — hover roughly one meter in front of him. He is positioned slightly behind and above them. The wind is not laminar. Each fly oscillates independently, up and down, like pistons in an imaginary engine, each on its own schedule, each tracing its own rhythm.

He watches.

The image stays with him for fifty years.

What the Memory Contains

A casual observer might describe this as: a boy watching flies in the wind. That description is accurate and almost entirely useless as a cognitive document.

What actually happened — what the memory encodes — is a sequence of perceptual operations that most minds perform without noticing, and some minds never perform at all. We will examine them one by one.

Operation One: Meaning-Weighted Rendering

When this memory is recalled, it does not present itself as a uniform visual field. It has a structure of importance.

The primary image is the abdomen of one fly — closer than the flies actually were, positioned slightly above and to the left. This fly was not different from the others. It was not doing anything the others were not doing. It did not earn its primacy through visual dominance.

It earned it through meaning. That abdomen was the locus of the phenomenon. Everything the memory is about — the motion, the relationship to the wind, the invisible made visible — was concentrated there. Memory rendered it accordingly.

The lesson: Perception and memory do not record like cameras. They render like artists: proportional to significance, not to size or brightness. A mind trained in this way of working learns to ask not what was visually prominent? but where was meaning concentrated? These are different questions with different answers.

The other flies occupy what might be called layer 2.5 — present, important, but not the subject. The rest of the scene blurs toward the edges of the mind's screen. This is not a failure of memory. It is memory doing its job correctly.

Operation Two: Sculpting the Invisible

As the observer watches the flies oscillate, something appears in his perception that was not physically present in the scene: trajectory lines. Each fly, in his rendering of the moment, draws a line through the air — a thin, black, essentially two-dimensional record of its path.

This is not metaphor. These lines appear as genuine perceptual objects. They can be toggled on or off. They have dimensions:

Y (height of oscillation): approximately 1 millimeter in representation

X (lateral width): zero — they are wires, not ribbons

Z (length): a function of time elapsed multiplied by the speed of observation

That last dimension — Z as time × observation speed — is the honest one. The length of the line is not fixed. It depends on how long you watch, and at what temporal resolution you are rendering the scene. The line is not a property of the fly alone. It is a property of the relationship between the fly and the observer across time.

This is what it means to think in four dimensions by default. Time is not added to the spatial image as an afterthought. It is structural. Remove time and the line collapses to a point. The line is time, made visible.

The lesson: A mind working in four dimensions does not represent states — it represents trajectories. Things are not where they are; they are where they are going, and where they have been. The static snapshot is the impoverished version. The full representation includes the dimension most minds treat as background.

Operation Three: Ownership Inversion

Here the memory reveals its deepest cognitive move.

The trajectory lines, once generated, appear to belong to the flies. The flies drew them. The flies are the authors of the pattern.

Then the ownership inverts.

The lines are not the flies' trajectories. They are the wind's trajectories. The flies were simply instruments — sensitive, responsive instruments — making the invisible structure of the wind legible. Each fly was a pen held by the air, not choosing its own path, but tracing the path that the wind's turbulence imposed on it.

The flies did not move. The wind moved, and the flies were where you could see it.

This inversion is not an intellectual conclusion reached after analysis. It is a perceptual event — a sudden reorientation of the entire scene. What appeared to be agents become instruments. What appeared to be background becomes the actual subject. The visible thing reveals itself as the display mechanism of the invisible thing.

The lesson: In any scene where something visible is moving in response to something invisible, ask: who is actually drawing these lines? The flag does not wave. The wind waves, and the flag is where you can see it. A mind that performs ownership inversion automatically sees through instruments to causes — through effects to the forces generating them.

Operation Four: The Query-Dependent Foreground

The wind, in this observer's representation, is not merely moving air. It is a molecular fluid — turbulent, fast at the molecular scale, soft and slippery at the macro scale, prone to sudden local changes in direction. This molecular-scale understanding is present in the background of the perception.

But it is not foregrounded. It does not surface into the primary image. It is there, available, but the query of the moment — why are the flies oscillating independently? — does not require it. So it remains background.

This is not ignorance. It is a form of cognitive economy more sophisticated than ignorance: everything is present, but relevance determines what is seen. A different query — why does turbulence occur at this scale? — would immediately bring the molecular layer forward.

The lesson: A well-organized mind is not one that knows many things. It is one that can rapidly determine, for any given question, which layer of understanding is load-bearing — and surface only that layer, while keeping the rest available. The skill is not storage. The skill is access.

Operation Five: Scale Transfer of Felt-Quality

Years later, at night, standing at his mother's house in Brazil, the same observer looks up at the southern sky — dense with stars in a way difficult to explain to those who have not seen it. He feels, for the first time, that everything — all of us, the entire solar system, the galaxy — is floating.

He does not think this. He feels it.

And what he feels is the same sensation as the blowflies.

Not the same intensity. Not the same scale. The same quality of motion — the smooth, unhurried, asynchronous bobbing of something held in a medium it cannot fully resist. The solar system, in its galactic orbit, oscillating above and below the galactic plane, just as the flies oscillated above and below their mean positions in the Brazilian wind.

The felt-quality transferred across fifty million orders of magnitude.

The lesson: A perception deeply enough understood does not stay at its original scale. It becomes a template — a recognizable quality of motion, relationship, or structure that can be identified wherever it recurs, regardless of size, medium, or timescale. The blowflies taught him something about how systems embedded in turbulent media behave. The galaxy confirmed it.

This is not analogy in the weak sense — not a rhetorical device used to make something unfamiliar seem familiar. It is the recognition of structural identity across scales: the same geometry, the same dynamics, the same relationship between a floating thing and the medium that moves it.

Summary: The Cognitive Architecture

From a single memory — five flies hovering for perhaps thirty seconds on a sunny afternoon — the following cognitive operations can be extracted:

1. Meaning-weighted rendering: Importance, not visual dominance, determines what the mind foregrounds.

2. 4D default representation: Time is structural, not supplementary. Trajectories, not positions, are the primary objects.

3. Invisible sculpting: The mind generates representations of causes, forces, and fields — not just visible surfaces.

4. Ownership inversion: The visible thing is regularly interrogated as a possible instrument of something invisible.

5. Query-dependent access: All layers of understanding remain present; the question determines which layer surfaces.

6. Scale-invariant pattern recognition: A deeply understood quality of relationship transfers across orders of magnitude.

None of these operations were performed consciously, deliberately, or after the fact. They were the perception itself.

That is the point.

The goal of the exercises and examples that follow is not to teach you facts about flies, wind, or galaxies. It is to make these operations available to you — to give you enough worked examples that you can begin to recognize, and then practice, the architecture of thought they represent.

You will know you are making progress when you stop reading the memories as stories and start reading them as demonstrations.
