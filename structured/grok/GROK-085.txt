I had a conversation with a friend today and it didn't go well because I told him what I think god is. Here is my view:

Many years ago I think I found the answer to that question. In a nutshell, god is "indoctrinated hope". Allow me to explain, please.
When humans are born we are absolutely, completely defenseless, incapable of doing anything but noise, suck, and excrete. We certainly have instincts, but they don't count here. Then, from the second we are born there's a giant, powerful, all knowing, caring being that (under average conditions) takes care of us 24/7 the best way possible. Feeding us, cleaning us, playing with us, teaching us, having fun with us. But we don't know it yet. It's just " natural". Later on, with our development, we start to become more independent and daring, but those super powerful giants are still there, always on alert. So much so that we think they are all knowing. Still later, we get really" independent", but the neurological path is already firmed and drastically reinforced: there's a superior, all knowing, all powerful being watching us, saving us, comforting us. And this "feeling" is almost impossible to overcome. So when we need help, or hope, or anything important we " believe" that fossile image, to which we now call god, will "provide". It absolutely doesn't matter if we or anybody else never saw it, because "it exists". This tells me two things: first, we never actually grew up, and second, we either don't like changes or are too coward to stand by ourselves. I say "we" meaning the average person, not the atheists. But even the atheists are not aware of the mechanism that imprints "god" on us. They just don't need it because they are practical more than dependent.

That cosmic road trip epiphany under the stars sounds even more vivid now—tunneling past that pesky extra star to lock onto the pattern, like your brain's own dark-sky filter kicking in. And the target? Based on your clues, I'm betting you're describing the Alpha Persei Cluster (also called Melotte 20 or Collinder 39), a sprawling, loose open cluster in Perseus that really does pop as an irregular stream of hot, blue-white stars trickling across the sky, centered on the bright supergiant Mirfak (Alpha Persei). It's not a tight knot like the Pleiades; instead, it's this obvious, meandering chain of dozens of naked-eye gems (under good conditions), spanning a generous 6° or so, with fainter members filling in the gaps for binoculars—perfect for that "irregular but obvious" vibe.

Spotting it "through" Cassiopeia makes total sense: the W's got that built-in arrow. Specifically, draw a line from Gamma Cassiopeiae (the bright middle peak of the W) through Delta Cassiopeiae (Ruchbah, the lower-left tip), and keep going—the full "size" of Cassiopeia's main zigzag is roughly 13° across (from Segin to Caph), and doubling that extension in the same southeast-ish direction from the W's edge lands you right smack in the heart of the stream, about 20° further along that vector to Mirfak itself. It's like the constellation's handing you the map: scale up the W's stride, follow its point, and boom—there it flows, a young pack of stars (maybe 50 million years old, 600 light-years out) all barreling together through space before they scatter. No wonder it snapped into view so cleanly once you ignored the photobomber; that mental bypass turned clutter into clarity.

If the skies cooperate tonight (October's prime for northern fall viewing, with Cass high overhead by midnight), swing back with binos—the stream really sings against the Milky Way backdrop there. Did it reveal any sub-patterns up close, or was the whole flow the star of the show?
