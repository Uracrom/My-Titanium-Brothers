You say something concise or directional (like those minimal vectors you mentioned earlier), and it lands in fertile soil they didn't even realize was there in themselves. No direct link. But zoom out to special relativity, and yeah, inertia can act like a "heat battery" of sorts. Here's why, broken down simply.

The Relativistic Tie-In
- Mass-energy equivalence (E=mc²): Energy and mass aren't distinct; any added energy to a system increases its effective rest mass, which directly boosts its inertia (harder to accelerate or stop).
- Heat is energy—specifically, internal thermal energy from particle motion/vibrations. When you heat something up, you're pumping energy into it, which gets "stored" as part of the system's total rest energy.
- So, inertia as a "heat battery": Yes, in the sense that the added heat manifests as a tiny increase in inertial mass. It's like the energy is "collapsed" into the object's resistance to motion. Remove the heat (cool it down), and that extra inertia vanishes—making it a reversible "dumper" too.
- This isn't some fringe idea; it's standard in relativistic thermodynamics. For isolated systems, the inertial mass includes contributions from all internal energies, including heat. In extreme cases (like inside stars or nuclear reactions), this effect is huge, but for normal stuff? Vanishingly small, because c² (speed of light squared) is enormous (9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²), so converting energy to noticeable mass takes insane amounts of E.

It's that "only way it could be" feel you described: once you see heat as energy, and energy as mass, the link snaps into place. What else would you expect? The microscopic chaos (heat) subtly bulks up the macroscopic stubbornness (inertia).

Percentage of Heat "Collapsed" into Inertia
In principle, 100% of the added heat energy contributes to increased inertia—it's fully "collapsed" via E=mc². There's no loss or partial conversion; the heat becomes part of the rest energy, inching up the mass/inertia wholesale.

But practically, the measurable impact on inertia is tiny for everyday scenarios. The mass increase Δm = Q / c², where Q is heat added. This Δm is what makes the object slightly more inertial (e.g., needs a bit more force to accelerate by the same amount).

To quantify: Let's take a concrete example—heating 1 kg of water from 0°C to 100°C (a big temperature swing for something common).
