No, a black hole doesn't "swallow time" or "swallow space" in the way it swallows matter—even when it's completely quiet and not accreting anything.

The phrase "swallowing time" (or similar poetic ideas like black holes "eating spacetime") pops up in popular descriptions, but it's metaphorical rather than literal physics. Let's break down what actually happens with time and space around a black hole, whether it's feeding or not.

The spacetime curvature is always there
A black hole's gravity comes from its mass (plus angular momentum and charge in more general cases). That mass creates extreme curvature of spacetime everywhere around it, including at and beyond the event horizon. This curvature:

- Doesn't require ongoing matter infall to exist
- Is static (unchanging over time) for an isolated, non-accreting black hole (described by the Schwarzschild or Kerr metric)

The geometry is fixed by the total mass enclosed. Once matter crosses the horizon, it adds to that total mass almost immediately from the black hole's perspective → the horizon grows outward to encompass the new total mass. But when nothing is falling in, the curvature and horizon size just sit there, unchanging.

What about time?
Gravity causes gravitational time dilation → clocks run slower in stronger gravitational fields.

- Far away: time runs at "normal" rate
- Closer to the horizon: time dilation becomes more extreme
- At the event horizon (from a distant observer's view): time dilation → infinite. Any clock sitting exactly at the horizon would appear infinitely slowed (never ticking forward). Light emitted from there would be infinitely redshifted.

This extreme slowing happens whether or not matter is currently falling in. It's purely a feature of the spacetime geometry created by the black hole's mass.

From the perspective of someone falling in, time passes normally for them → they cross the horizon in finite proper time and continue toward the singularity without noticing anything special at the horizon itself.

So the black hole isn't actively "swallowing" or consuming time like it consumes infalling gas or stars. Instead, it creates a region where time is stretched/dilated dramatically due to curvature. Time isn't a substance that gets pulled in—it's how events are ordered and how durations are measured, and that ordering gets extremely warped.

What about space?
Similar logic applies. Space isn't being "sucked in" continuously when the black hole is quiet.

- In some coordinate systems (like Gullstrand–Painlevé), you can think of space as "flowing" inward toward the horizon at the speed of light exactly at the horizon → nothing can swim outward against that flow.
- But this is again a feature of the static geometry. It doesn't require active accretion to maintain the inward "flow."
- When the black hole is isolated, the overall spacetime structure remains stationary—no net shrinking or growing of the horizon.

Only when matter/energy crosses the horizon does the horizon expand (very slightly for each bit of mass added).

In short:
- Matter/energy → yes, the black hole can swallow it (and grow) only when it's actually falling in.
- Time and space → no active swallowing. The black hole warps them permanently via its gravity, creating the extreme dilation and inescapable region. That warping persists forever even in complete isolation (ignoring the ultra-slow Hawking radiation evaporation over insane timescales).

The black hole doesn't need to keep "eating" to keep being a black hole—once formed, its gravitational influence on spacetime is self-sustaining.

Yes, exactly — in general relativity, mass and energy bend (or curve) spacetime. That's the core idea: gravity isn't a force pulling things together like in Newton's view. Instead, massive objects like stars, planets, or black holes warp the geometry of space and time around them. Objects (including light) simply follow the straightest possible paths — called geodesics — in this curved spacetime, and we experience that as gravitational attraction.

The rubber-sheet analogy (a heavy ball denting a stretched sheet) is popular because it captures this bending visually, even though it's a 2D simplification of a 4D reality.

When a black hole (or any massive object) is wandering/moving through space, it carries its curvature along with it. As it passes through a region:

- Spacetime in front of it gets deformed as the black hole approaches (the "dent" moves forward).
- Right around it, the curvature is extreme (especially near the event horizon).
- Behind it, as the black hole moves away, spacetime relaxes back toward flatness in that region.

So yes — space "goes back to normal" (or very close to flat) after the black hole passes. There is no permanent deformation or "scar" left behind in spacetime itself, like a gouged track or a fixed wrinkle. The curvature is directly tied to the presence and location of mass/energy. Once the mass moves on, the geometry adjusts accordingly.

A few precise points from general relativity:

- Changes in curvature (like the "unbending" as the black hole leaves) propagate at the speed of light. The relaxation isn't instantaneous everywhere; distant regions "learn" about the movement only after a light-travel-time delay.
- Gravitational fields have infinite range (they fall off as 1/r²), so technically there's always a tiny residual effect from any mass anywhere in the universe — spacetime is never perfectly flat if there's any matter at all. But in practice, once the black hole is far enough away, the curvature becomes negligible and effectively zero for any measurement.
- There are tiny, lingering effects in special cases — for example, gravitational waves (ripples in spacetime) can be emitted if the motion involves acceleration (like orbiting or sudden changes), and those waves carry away energy and propagate outward forever. But for a black hole just cruising along in a straight line at constant speed, no significant waves are produced, and the spacetime returns to its prior (nearly flat) state behind it.
- No accumulated "drag" or permanent twisting happens from mere passage (though a spinning black hole does cause frame-dragging, a local twisting of spacetime around itself, which moves with it).

In short: spacetime is dynamic and responsive — it bends where mass is, unbends where mass leaves, and the whole thing adjusts at the speed of light. A wandering black hole deforms spacetime wherever it currently is, but leaves no lasting mark once it's gone.
