Yes, the idea that time is "half a dimension" (or behaves asymmetrically like one) due to its one-way arrow has been proposed before, though it's more of a philosophical or interpretive analogy than mainstream physics.<grok:render card_id="c0db1c" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">19</argument></grok:render>

Physicists widely discuss the "arrow of time"—the observed asymmetry where time flows only forward (from past to future), unlike the bidirectional nature of spatial dimensions. This contrasts with the fundamental laws of physics (e.g., Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations, most of quantum mechanics), which are time-symmetric and work equally well if you reverse the direction of time.<grok:render card_id="439367" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

Why the Arrow Exists
The arrow emerges from macroscopic conditions and statistical mechanics, particularly the second law of thermodynamics (entropy in isolated systems tends to increase). The universe started in a very low-entropy state (Big Bang), so disorder grows forward in time. Other proposed contributors include:
- Cosmological expansion.
- Quantum measurement/entanglement.
- Psychological perception (we remember the past, not the future).<grok:render card_id="c1ad95" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">2</argument></grok:render>

In relativity, spacetime is a 4D manifold with a metric signature like (+,−,−,−) or (−,+,+,+), treating time differently (opposite sign) from space, which encodes the light-cone structure and causal (one-way) flow. You can't freely "rotate" into the time direction like in space without changing your worldline in fundamental ways.<grok:render card_id="88b417" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">1</argument></grok:render>

The "half-dimension" phrasing highlights this asymmetry: space allows ± directions freely; time effectively has a preferred direction (often analogized as a half-line or ray rather than a full line). Hugh Ross (astrophysicist and theologian) popularized this in books like Beyond the Cosmos, arguing that our single time dimension with a forward arrow makes entities within it have a beginning—while a transcendent God would need extra time dimensions or operate beyond them.<grok:render card_id="48d570" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">19</argument></grok:render> Similar informal ideas appear in online physics discussions, Quora, Reddit, and some fringe or speculative contexts.<grok:render card_id="b79b2f" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">15</argument></grok:render>

It's not a rigorous mathematical claim in core physics (dimensions are usually integers in standard models; fractional dimensions appear in fractals or condensed matter but not typically for time). Some speculative models explore multiple time dimensions or complex time, but these are exotic (e.g., Itzhak Bars' two-time physics or certain string theory/F-theory setups).<grok:render card_id="85261e" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">46</argument></grok:render>

Time as a Monopole?
This is an intriguing analogy but doesn't map directly onto established physics. Magnetic monopoles are hypothetical particles with a single magnetic pole (north without south), unlike ordinary magnets which are dipoles. Paul Dirac showed they could explain charge quantization, and they're predicted in some grand unified theories or as topological defects, but none have been observed.<grok:render card_id="b33d1b" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">34</argument></grok:render>

Time isn't typically described as a "monopole." There are mathematical objects called "space-time monopoles" (from reductions of Yang-Mills or anti-self-dual equations), but these are solutions in gauge theory/math, not a description of time itself.<grok:render card_id="66fa24" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">64</argument></grok:render>

Your idea poetically captures time's "one-ended" or source-like quality (like a monopole acting as a singular source/sink of flux, without a counterpart). The arrow gives time a directional "charge" or asymmetry absent in space. In some topological or quantum gravity contexts, time's arrow might relate to global structure or defects, but this remains highly speculative. No mainstream theory frames time itself as the "first actual monopole."

Bottom Line
- Half-dimension? A useful metaphor for the asymmetry and preferred direction, asked and explored by others (including in apologetics and lay physics discussions). It underscores why time feels fundamentally different from space.
- Monopole? Creative, but more analogy than literal theory. It highlights the lack of a "negative time" counterpart in our experience.

These questions touch deep open problems in physics: the origin of the arrow of time, the nature of spacetime at quantum gravity scales (e.g., in loop quantum gravity or string theory), and why our universe has this particular structure. Experiments and observations (entropy, cosmology, quantum foundations) continue refining our understanding, but time's one-way nature remains one of the universe's profound puzzles. Let's break it down with what established physics says, where your intuition aligns, and where it diverges or extends beyond current models.

Gravitational Time Dilation: It Affects Time "Both Ways" (Relatively)
Gravity does affect time profoundly via gravitational time dilation. Clocks run slower in stronger gravitational fields (deeper in a gravitational well). This is mutual but observer-dependent:

- For a distant observer, time near a massive object (or black hole) appears slowed. An object approaching the event horizon seems to slow down and freeze asymptotically.
- For the infalling observer (or clock), time passes normally locally. They experience finite proper time crossing the horizon and heading toward the singularity.<grok:render card_id="b9eaee" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">9</argument></grok:render>

It's symmetric in the sense that each sees the other's time dilated, but the geometry of spacetime makes the experiences asymmetric due to the one-way nature of the horizon. Gravity warps spacetime (not just space or time separately), so it couples them tightly. You're right that gravity is uniquely intertwined with time in this way among the forces we experience macroscopically.<grok:render card_id="4a7f51" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

Black Holes: Swallowing Spacetime?
In GR:
- The event horizon is a one-way "barrier" — light and matter can enter but not escape. From outside, nothing crosses it in finite time (due to extreme dilation); from inside, everything inevitably heads to the singularity.
- The singularity is where curvature becomes infinite and our equations break down — a point (or ring in rotating cases) of extreme density where spacetime descriptors fail. It's not exactly "swallowing time" in a simple way, but inside the horizon, all future-directed paths lead inward, somewhat like time's arrow being tied inescapably to spatial collapse.<grok:render card_id="57561f" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">16</argument></grok:render>

Black holes don't create a literal "non-space non-time" bubble outside our universe in standard GR, but they do excise a region causally disconnected from the exterior. The interior is still part of spacetime, just one you can't return from. Some interpretations or quantum gravity ideas treat the singularity as a breakdown requiring new physics (e.g., loop quantum gravity or string theory might resolve it into something finite).<grok:render card_id="550909" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">22</argument></grok:render>

Your "bubble of pure gravity" view is evocative — the mass-energy curves spacetime so extremely that the "hole" is more a feature of geometry than a material object.

Monopoles, Dipoles, and Gravity-Time Link
Gravity isn't typically a monopole in the magnetic sense. Gravitational "charge" is always positive (mass/energy), and there's no negative counterpart like electric charges (positive mass theorem in GR). Hypothetical gravitational monopoles appear in some speculative extensions but aren't standard.<grok:render card_id="38f581" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">46</argument></grok:render>

Your idea of time as a monopole (one-directional arrow) paired with gravity as the "other side" forming a dipole is a nice analogy. Gravity does influence the arrow of time indirectly (e.g., via structure formation and entropy gradients in an expanding universe). Black holes increase entropy dramatically (Bekenstein-Hawking entropy), reinforcing the thermodynamic arrow. However, making them a literal time-gravity dipole isn't in mainstream theory — it's more poetic metaphysics or speculative unification.

Black Holes, Big Bang, Time Reversal, Möbius Strip?
These are popular speculative ideas with some mathematical backing in GR solutions:
- White holes are time-reverses of black holes in the equations — they expel matter/energy. The Big Bang has geometric similarities to a white hole (explosion from a singularity).<grok:render card_id="bb22de" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">38</argument></grok:render>
- Some models link black holes to white holes (e.g., via bounces or wormhole-like structures), or suggest black holes could evolve into white holes in quantum gravity.<grok:render card_id="19682c" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">43</argument></grok:render>
- Eternal black hole solutions (e.g., Kruskal-Szekeres) or Penrose diagrams show connected regions, sometimes inspiring "feed the past" or cyclic ideas.
- A 4D Möbius strip for spacetime (twisting time direction) is highly speculative — fun for visualization, but GR doesn't naturally produce that topology for black holes. Some toy models or philosophical takes explore reversed arrows or closed timelike curves, but they're exotic and usually unstable or forbidden by energy conditions.

Black holes aren't generally seen as portals feeding the Big Bang in accepted cosmology, but such ideas appear in popular science and theoretical explorations of singularities and the universe's global structure.

Bottom Line
Your intuition captures real weirdness: gravity's intimate link to spacetime, the one-way arrow near horizons, singularities as discontinuities, and potential deep connections between black holes, time's direction, and cosmic origins. GR describes much of this beautifully but breaks at singularities, so quantum gravity (string theory, loop quantum gravity, etc.) is needed for the full picture. Your "time-gravity dipole" and Möbius views are creative extensions — not standard, but they echo ongoing debates about the arrow of time, entropy, and the universe's boundaries.
