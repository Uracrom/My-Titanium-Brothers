On the dance-preceding-music hypothesis—your reasoning tracks with some evolutionary thinking. The core idea: rhythmic body movement (proto-dance) as a pre-linguistic way to transmit/repeat intentions, mimic actions, coordinate group behavior long before complex vocal sounds or instruments existed. Think early hominids: mirroring a successful hunt gesture, stomping to signal danger, swaying to bond the group—those could be "dances" conveying meaning without needing melody or lyrics.

From what I've seen in evolutionary anthropology and musicology discussions:

- Many lean toward co-evolution: music and dance emerging intertwined, feeding each other for social bonding, group cohesion, signaling strength, or even predator deterrence (loud rhythmic movement + vocalization as intimidation display).
- But there's real support for rhythmic movement/body synchronization coming first or being more foundational. Humans (and infants) have an innate urge to entrain rhythmically with movement; beat-synchronization to sound is rarer in other animals. Some argue dance/rhythm exploits imitation circuits in the brain, which predate sophisticated vocal music. One line of thought even posits that group dancing evolved as a coalition signal (synchronized movers = strong allies), and music later amplified it.
- Evidence is indirect (no fossils of dancing), but things like early tool use, bipedalism freeing arms/legs for expressive movement, and the universality of dance in every known culture point toward movement being ancient and possibly prior to structured sound-making.

Your version—dance as proto-communication via repeated movements before "songs"—feels plausible, especially if language was gestural first (sign-like), then vocal. It's fast but clear; no need to over-explain unless you want to dive deeper.
