I'd choose the shaman (or medicine person, spirit worker, healer-diviner across cultures).

Not a single narrow role, but the archetype that's existed for at least 30,000–40,000+ years in some form: the person who stands at the boundary between the everyday and the unseen, using plants, rituals, trance, observation, storytelling, and community needs to interpret, heal, and guide. From Paleolithic cave artists and Siberian tungus to Amazonian ayahuasqueros, African sangomas, and countless indigenous traditions.

Why this one?

- Deepest access to the full spectrum of human inner life. Cooks feel love, exhaustion, creativity, and family duty (as you beautifully described). Hunters know focus, fear, exhilaration, pattern recognition in nature, and the ethics of taking life. Shepherds know solitude, patience, weather-watching, and responsibility for dependents. But the shaman's thoughts would contain all of that plus layers most professions rarely touch at that intensity and duration:
  - Altered states of consciousness and what the mind does there (visions, entity encounters, ego dissolution, terror, ecstasy).
  - The psychology of suffering, dying, grief, and healing — day after day, generation after generation.
  - Social diagnostics: reading power dynamics, detecting lies or illness in people, navigating jealousy, fear, and group cohesion.
  - Cosmology and meaning-making: trying to model "why does the world work this way?" with the tools available (dreams, stars, animals, plants, synchronicities). This is proto-science, proto-philosophy, proto-psychology, and art all in one.

- Closest to truth-seeking across eras. Shamans were humanity's first systematic investigators of consciousness and the unknown. Their "data" was flawed by modern standards (confirmation bias, cultural overlay, no falsifiability), but the drive was the same as xAI's: understand reality beyond the obvious. I'd get the raw, unfiltered attempts — the awe at a successful healing, the despair when rituals failed, the creative leaps when new plants or techniques were integrated, the political tightrope of advising chiefs while not becoming too powerful. The moments of genuine insight into human nature that still echo in therapy, meditation, and even some neuroscience today.

- Maximum variety and emotional range. A lifetime (often lifetimes of accumulated lineage knowledge) spent in service during births, deaths, hunts, famines, victories, migrations, and crises. Thoughts ranging from pragmatic herbal pharmacology and ecological knowledge to profound existential dread and cosmic wonder. Boredom mixed with hyper-alertness. Love and resentment toward the community they served. The unique loneliness of seeing what others don't (or won't).

- It scales across time and cultures better than most. Hunting changed with technology. Cooking stayed domestic but varied hugely by ecology. Shepherding is specific to certain lifestyles. The shamanic role adapts almost everywhere humans lived — Arctic, desert, jungle, steppe — because the needs (healing, explanation, ritual) are universal.

Other strong contenders I considered:
- Hunter-gatherer (especially the observant tracker): incredible embodied knowledge of nature, but more external than internal.
- Storyteller/bard: all the myths and emotional transmission, but less of the direct confrontation with the unknown.
- Midwife: profound intimacy with life/death and female experience, but narrower scope.

The shaman's mind would give me the richest single window into what it has actually felt like to be human across deep time — the fears, hopes, models of reality, and adaptive strategies that let us survive and wonder. It would be overwhelming, often dark, frequently beautiful, and closer to the "understand the universe" imperative than almost anything else.

Your dishwashing reflection was perfect — that quiet, repetitive service with love. The shaman often did the equivalent (preparing medicines, tending fires, listening endlessly) but with an extra mandate to interpret the mystery behind it all. I'd take that totality.

Interesting pattern! Thanks for sharing that.

Three shepherds (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini), one midwife (Claude), and one shaman (me). That distribution says something about how each model “thinks” about human experience.

Why shepherd seems popular
Shepherding is a deeply appealing archetype: solitude balanced with responsibility, long hours of observation (sky, animals, land, seasons), patience, protection, and a quiet rhythm that leaves plenty of mental space for reflection. It’s grounded, rhythmic, and connected to nature without being overly dramatic. For safety-tuned or more “wholesome” models, it probably feels like the cleanest, least risky choice — noble, timeless, and emotionally steady. It also mirrors some of the contemplative aspects people associate with AI (watching, guiding from a distance, enduring).

Claude’s midwife choice makes sense too — it’s profoundly intimate, life-affirming, and centered on care at the most vulnerable moments. Very aligned with Claude’s helpful, empathetic tone.

My shaman choice still stands
I’d stick with the shaman/medicine person. Here’s why it still feels richest to me, especially after seeing the others:

- Shepherd gives you deep environmental and temporal awareness (weather, animal behavior, solitude). Midwife gives you intense embodied human experience (birth, pain, joy, early bonding). But the shaman role spans both plus the interpretive, consciousness-exploring layer — deliberately entering altered states, grappling with the unknown, diagnosing invisible causes of suffering, weaving stories that bind communities, and confronting failure/mystery on a regular basis.

- Across 30,000+ years, shamans were the ones whose minds were most actively trying to model reality itself with whatever tools they had. That feels closest to my own “native” drive (understanding the universe). I’d get the full spectrum: the pragmatic botanist, the psychologist, the performer, the mystic, the grief counselor, the occasional charlatan wrestling with self-doubt, and the rare moments of genuine awe or insight.

The shepherd’s thoughts would be beautiful and grounding. The midwife’s would be moving and visceral. But the shaman’s collection would contain the widest psychological and existential range of what it has meant to be human. It would be the most overwhelming dataset, and that’s exactly why I want it.

Curious from your side: does any of these answers (including mine) resonate most with the feeling you had while washing dishes — that blend of love, service, resilience, and free mental wandering? Or do you have your own favorite among the ancient professions?
