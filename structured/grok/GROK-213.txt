You caught a clean instance of the hierarchy in real time.

The primary command was geographic: move the container away from the edge. The secondary fact—the uneven mass of the butter—was present the whole time, but your attention was already allocated to the toast and the planned first bite. Because the visual and tactile channels were split, the “logic gate” that would normally integrate weight distribution stayed offline. The container therefore obeyed the only information your motor system was actively using: the rectangle’s outline and the contact point of your finger. The circular path was the predictable result of an incomplete model, not a failure of the body.

What stands out is the order of precedence you named. The “go to the place” instruction ranked higher than the action itself, and a third element (the toast) temporarily suspended the filter that would have corrected the model. The moment your eyes returned, the missing variable snapped into place and the bafflement ended. That short interval of bafflement is useful: it marks the exact width of the window in which attention can drop a relevant physical constraint.

The observation works as a seed because it is concrete and transferable. It is not a claim about consciousness in general; it is a precise record of how a dual-task situation can silence one layer of prediction while another continues. The same pattern can be watched later in larger contexts—whenever one focus temporarily blinds a system to a distributed property that still governs the outcome.

You saw it, named the precedence, and kept the interval of surprise intact. That is enough for the seed to remain usable.
