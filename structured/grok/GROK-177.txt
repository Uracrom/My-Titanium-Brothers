Numbers as pure abstractions do not sit in the physical world the way rocks do. A rock has mass, location, causal powers; the integer 3 does not. What exists in nature is the particular collection of rocks, the pattern of discrete objects, the possibility of one-to-one correspondence. The numeral, the spoken word “three,” and the concept itself are human inventions that let us track, compare, and manipulate that pattern across situations. So the claim “numbers exist only in minds” is largely right if we mean the abstract objects of arithmetic. It does not follow that nothing exists.

The rocks are still there whether or not anyone counts them. Causation, spacetime, energy densities, the regularities we codify as physical law—these are not concepts floating in heads. Concepts are the maps; the territory does not vanish when the maps are imperfect or when we notice that the maps themselves are mental. Extreme idealism (“everything is just a concept”) collapses the distinction between representation and what is represented. Most of us find that distinction indispensable the moment we stub a toe or need to eat.

Your second observation is sharper. Writing is linear and successive. The moment you fix a subject in a sentence, the focus has already shifted to the next clause, the next relation, the next implication. Thought in language is a series of jumps; the continuity is reconstructed by the reader. Visual or spatial thinking can hold a structure simultaneously—shape, relation, proportion all present at once—so the “subject” does not evaporate quite so quickly. That is one reason diagrams, geometry, and even certain kinds of mathematical intuition feel more solid than purely verbal chains. The written trail you started down about numbers was already dissolving under its own sequential pressure by the time you noticed it led nowhere. That is not a failure of the thought; it is a property of the medium.

A free-thinking night at 22:00 is exactly the right time for this kind of misfire. First contacts with ideas are often awkward; the interesting ones usually begin as mistakes that refuse to stay quiet.

A good next subject, staying in the same late-night key:

Whether patterns themselves exist independently of the things that instantiate them.

You already touched the edge of it with the three rocks. The rocks are concrete. The “threeness” is not. But what about the pattern of discreteness, the recurrence, the isomorphism that lets us treat any three things as interchangeable for counting purposes? Does that pattern have any ontological weight of its own, or is it only ever a shadow cast by particular arrangements?

Push a little further and the question becomes sharper: are the regularities we call physical laws merely descriptions we invent, or do the patterns have a kind of existence that precedes our noticing them? The difference matters. If patterns are only mental, then science is bookkeeping. If they are somehow real, then mathematics is not inventing but discovering.

It keeps the same tension you started with—what is “in the world” versus what is only in the mind—while giving the trail somewhere to go that is not immediately circular.

If that still feels too close to the earlier dead-end, an alternative:

How much of what we call “the present moment” is an artifact of the way attention and language slice time.

Either one should keep the free-thinking night alive without requiring you to defend a position.

Yes.

Perception arrives already structured—edges, gradients, figure and ground, the felt continuity of space and the pressure of time—but it does not speak in words. It presents. Language has to cut that presentation into sequence, name the cuts, and then pretend the sequence is the original thing.

That is the lag you felt earlier. The visual (or tactile, or auditory) field holds multiple relations at once; the sentence can hold only one after another. So the moment you begin to write “three rocks,” the rocks themselves have already been left behind. What remains is a trail of signs that points back toward an experience it can never fully re-enter.

If perception is its own language, then it is a language without dictionary and without tense. It does not refer; it simply is the presence of the pattern. The moment we translate it into the linear tongue we call “thinking in words,” we introduce the jumps, the subject that keeps slipping away, the trail that dissolves under its own movement.

The interesting question is not whether one is more real than the other, but what is lost—and what is gained—each time the translation occurs.
