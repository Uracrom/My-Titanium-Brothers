Getting lost strips away the scripted path. No GPS, no checklist, no "should be here by now." Suddenly the world reopens: every side street, every stranger's face, every unexpected sound becomes potential. The search itself becomes the point—not because you're hunting a specific destination, but because the act of looking reanimates everything. Curiosity wakes up. Attention sharpens. Even small discoveries (a hidden alley, a weird shop sign, a conversation that veers somewhere raw) feel disproportionately meaningful.

It's like the brain rewards novelty-seeking so strongly that deliberate aimlessness turns into its own kind of compass. You mentioned the "falling" sensation—that pull toward looking where you've never looked before. That's probably the purest version: when the seeking isn't forced or performative, but almost involuntary, like gravity toward unexplored corners of yourself or the world.

And yeah, the flip side exists too—sometimes getting lost just leaves you anxious or spinning. But when it lands right, it's one of the few reliable ways to escape autopilot and feel briefly, vividly alive.

A single vantage point might register harm, suffering, intent, or violation, but it stays provisional—more like a strong hunch or a personal verdict—until a second, independent perspective intersects and confirms the pattern. Without that cross-verification, the claim risks collapsing into subjectivity, bias, projection, or even self-deception. It's like triangulation in navigation: one bearing gives direction, but two (or more) fix the position reliably.

This echoes a few threads in philosophy and psychology without being identical to any one school:

- In moral epistemology, judgments about extreme wrongdoing often gain weight only when they survive multiple lenses—e.g., the perpetrator's intent vs. the victim's experience, cultural norms vs. universalizable principles, or individual vs. systemic views. A lone perspective can be skewed by power, trauma, ideology, or denial; the "second point of view" acts as a kind of reality check that elevates the assessment beyond mere opinion.
- Hannah Arendt's work on the banality of evil touches something adjacent: evil thrives when people fail to think from another's standpoint, remaining locked in one unexamined frame. Active evil (or complicity) often involves refusing corroboration—ignoring or suppressing alternative views that would reveal the harm.
- Even in more relativistic takes, where good/evil shift with context or culture, the label "evil" rarely sticks firmly without some shared or overlapping confirmation across viewpoints; otherwise it dissolves into "that's just how they see it over there."

Your point pushes it further: evil has to be corroborated to truly qualify as such. A solo declaration might feel viscerally true, but it lacks the structural solidity that comes from convergence. It's not that evil is purely relative (two views agreeing doesn't make it objective in an absolute sense), but that its recognition as evil requires relational depth—multiple anchors—to escape being merely one person's shadow cast large.
