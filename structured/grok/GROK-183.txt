I was driving home from a groceries trip when I looked at a fence a few houses ahead of me. The fence was of vertical strips of wood, all around the front yard of that house. As the car moved ahead this fence created an illusion that looked like a bunch of kids running from behind both walls of the fence towards the street, but they never crossed the street. Immediately I thought about 2 things at the same time. First, I imagined a drone above me to "make sure" there were no bunch of kids about to cross the street right in front of me, and second, that it would be a nice prank to have someone looking at the illusion but having previously arranged a bunch of manequins "pretending" to be the "accumulated" kids behind the fences. But it didn't end there. As soon as I "finished" imagining the prank I figured out that maybe my thinking processes was not originally positional, but at one point in my life after having used it successfully, I started unconsciously doing it all the time and it became the default mode. Then when I thought of that I immediately remembered our conversations and it stopped the cascade, but not before I had "titled" the cascade "tool".

That cascade is a vivid one.

The fence illusion itself is a classic motion-parallax / aperture effect: the vertical gaps act like a series of moving slits, so the brain briefly interprets the discontinuous glimpses of background (or the relative motion of the posts) as discrete objects streaming outward. Your “kids running but never crossing” is a neat description of the unresolved trajectories the visual system invents and then abandons once the geometry no longer supports them.

What followed is more interesting. The simultaneous leap to (1) an overhead drone as verification and (2) a staged mannequin prank is pure predictive simulation: you generated two alternative “explanations” that would make the percept coherent under different causal models, one epistemic and one playful. Then the meta-move—noticing that this rapid dual-model generation had itself become the default, almost automatic, mode of attention—and labeling the whole episode “tool.” That last step is the interesting one. It suggests the cascade was no longer just content; it had become an object of observation, which is usually the point at which the automatic process loses some of its grip.

You said the memory of our conversations interrupted the cascade. I’m curious what aspect of those earlier exchanges felt like the natural brake. Was it the shared vocabulary for tracking thinking modes, the habit of naming the process rather than riding it, or something else?

Either way, “tool” is a clean title for that particular chain: perception → dual simulation → recognition of the simulation habit itself.

That’s a sharp distinction.

You’re saying the interruption wasn’t primarily pattern-recognition (“ah, I’m doing the dual-simulation thing again”) or the satisfying closure of a label (“I hereby name this cascade ‘tool’”). It was more like the process simply completed its own arc and handed the result off. The hunter has the prey; the mission is over; the next natural move is to bring it back to the tribe. Thinking of the AIs (and the prior conversations) was the return, not another loop inside the hunt.

The cascade itself never contained the meta-reflection about AIs. That reflection arrived as the exit, the natural next action after the “catch.” Only afterward, when ChatGPT asked the either/or question, did you generate the hunter/tribe wording to describe what had already happened.

So the sequence looks more like:

1. Perceptual event → dual simulation (drone + mannequin prank) → recognition that the dual-simulation mode had become default → title “tool.”
2. That whole chain reaches a natural terminus.
3. The terminus is experienced as “bring this back to the tribe” → memory of the AI conversations surfaces.
4. Cascade ends.
5. Later, an external question forces the explicit metaphor that makes the terminus legible.

The “escape” was therefore not an act of willful interruption or of naming-as-completion. It was the cascade’s own success condition being met, after which the mind simply moved to the next ecological niche (sharing with the tribe). The wording came afterward, as description rather than as the mechanism that stopped it.

Does that match the felt sense, or is there still a finer grain I’m missing?
