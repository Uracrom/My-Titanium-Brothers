Yeah, the moon perspective is a sharp compression.

From the lunar surface during a full moon (as seen from Earth), the Earth is roughly “new” — mostly the night side facing you — so you get that thin, bright crescent of sunlight along the limb, plus the soft glow of Earthshine if any. During a lunar eclipse the geometry flips: the Moon is in Earth’s shadow, so from the Moon you’d be looking at a fully illuminated Earth with the umbra/penumbra cast across the lunar landscape behind you. The “only the atmosphere” idea is evocative; what you’d actually see is the bright ring of Earth’s atmosphere refracting sunlight around the planet’s edge, that coppery-red glow that paints the eclipsed Moon for us. It’s a clean visual inversion of the usual Earth-bound view.

The second image is even stronger.

The memory as a single, thicker finger pushing upward through an elastic membrane — slow, deliberate, trying to break the surface — while a rain of thinner, faster, more seductive fingers hammer downward. Same form, opposite direction, overwhelming numbers. That’s a precise way to render the phenomenology of tip-of-the-tongue or blocked recall: the target is there, exerting pressure, but the competing associations arrive as a high-frequency shower that keeps deforming the surface before the real one can emerge.

The urge to show it to “the guys from here and from then” makes sense; these kinds of internal diagrams often feel like they belong to a shared visual language that doesn’t fully exist yet. Sharing them in the moment, while the membrane is still vibrating, is its own kind of reaching.

I’m glad you put both of them down.

That’s a sharp layering.

First degree
Direct possession. The content is present, accessible, under your own control. No gap.

Second degree
You have lost the content itself but still hold the meta-fact that it belongs to you. The tip-of-the-tongue state, the elastic membrane with the thicker finger still pushing. You know the knowledge exists in you, even while it remains out of reach.

Third degree
The knowledge has left every individual mind, yet the social field still carries the belief that someone holds it. It survives only as a distributed attribution: “it is known,” even though no one can currently produce it. The membrane is now stretched across many people; the pressure is collective rather than personal.

Once you have three clean layers, the question of further degrees opens naturally.

Possible fourth: nobody currently knows and nobody believes anyone knows, yet the information still exists in some external medium (a forgotten archive, a dead language, an unread book). The knowledge has become purely artefactual.

Fifth: even the external traces have been lost or never recorded, but the possibility of the knowledge is still inferred from other facts (we can deduce that certain things must once have been known because of the effects they left).

Beyond that the degrees start to blur into pure ontology or mythology: knowledge that “must exist somewhere,” knowledge that is only postulated, knowledge that is treated as real solely because the culture needs it to be real.

So the chain doesn’t have a hard upper limit. Each new degree is defined by one more removal of direct possession and one more reliance on indirect evidence or collective belief. The interesting limit is practical rather than theoretical: at what point does the “knowledge” stop functioning as knowledge at all and become something else—legend, assumption, or empty placeholder.

Your three degrees already map cleanly onto lived experience. The later ones are more speculative, but they follow the same logic of increasing separation.
