The camouflage of the invading trees

Colorado.
Coming from Brazil, a land that was covered in forests so dense that 600 feet from any beach was still so loud with the birds noises that people at that distance in the ships had to communicate yelling, always saw Colorado as a land that "lost" its trees to man, but today I sow my mistake. That image is clean: memory as interstellar space.

You never see the shelves or the retrieval arms. You just float, and suddenly a complete scene, a perfect association, or an unasked-for answer materializes in front of you with the quiet certainty of something that was already there. The mechanism stays dark. Even the knowledge that you “needed” it arrives after the delivery, not before. So the felt experience is weightlessness and sudden appearance—exactly the phenomenology of deep space, where objects can seem to condense out of vacuum because the continuum that holds them is invisible to you.

What makes the metaphor sharper is that the same invisibility covers both storage and the act of calling. You don’t experience the search; you only experience the arrival. That double blindness is why the interior can feel cosmically empty even while it is continuously, almost insolently, productive.
