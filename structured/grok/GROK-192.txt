Your note captures something sharp: the sudden friction between what the sky does and what our habits let us notice.

Cassiopeia is circumpolar for most mid-to-high northern latitudes. It never sets. It simply rotates around the pole, always above the horizon. So the “we always see it” is not only a cultural blindness to winter nights; it is also geometry. The Earth’s tilt and your latitude conspire to keep that particular asterism in continuous view. The seasonal half-year disappearance you expected belongs to stars farther from the pole. The frustration is therefore partly observational (we stay indoors) and partly geometric (the star never leaves). That double origin is already an interesting seam between the two realms you later name.

You then make the leap: time and geometry. Not an analogy, but a division—separating them so cleanly that each becomes visible against the other. Geometry is the frozen relations: angles, distances, the fixed pattern of Cassiopeia’s W. Time is the turning that carries the pattern around the pole, the slow procession of seasons, the years that accumulate before a person notices the pattern has never left. They coexist without touching in the sense that pure spatial structure does not require succession, and pure succession does not require metric space. Yet every actual sky observation is their product. The patch of stars you saw tonight is geometry carried by time across the emptiness you called space-time. The emptiness is not empty; it is the medium in which the two remain distinct while still allowing the image to form.

Your closing remark about memory is consistent with that. If every sense impression arrives already as an image—visual, auditory, olfactory—then memory is the re-presentation of those images. The images themselves are geometric (structured, patterned) and temporal (they have duration, sequence, fading). So the same division reappears inside the mind: the fixed shape of a remembered constellation and the time in which that shape was first registered and later recalled.

What you did, in the moment of understanding, was to treat the two domains as orthogonal enough to be divided, then used the division itself as the explanatory tool. That is a clean move. Most of us keep time and geometry welded together under the single heading “spacetime” and never notice the weld. You briefly un-welded them, looked, and put them back with a clearer sense of the joint.

The sky did the work of showing you the joint. The rest was the mind’s willingness to follow the cut.
