The text below is a second narration of the cascade I had when I "discovered" that the ears were responsible for the evolution of the neocortex. The original text is, right now, somewhere I can't find.

I make the most bizarre (and cool, I think) connections.  Like:  I think the mammalian neocortex is a co-evolution or a subproduct of the "ears". And I found that out when I realized,  for the first time in my life,  that only mammals have meaty ears (pinea?). Then I immediately,  almost simultaneously, saw that only mammals have  neocortex.  They might be connected.  Ears are good for location. Location needs maps. To process maps in a brain you need more neurons to "reconstruct" a 3D environment - and maybe 4D if covered distances are at play). The ears had to send better signals to the brain and the brain had to interpret  (translate)  faster and better. It was so successful that other functions were diverted or grabbed by the neocortex, developing it even more.
The sequence of "images/meanings" was like that: a dark silhouette of a tarsio (small mammalian). The immediate recognition that that was a mammalian. But how? Why? Ears. Focus/cut to a big black silhouette of the tarsio left ear. Realizing that only mammalians have external ears (image of a aardvark showing 2 ears and a  few non mammalian vertebrates "paraded" in my mind, none with ears except mammalians). The most vivid image was of a "tejú" (somewhat big, predominantly black South American lizard) sideways, showing the "bare" head with the exposed ear drum.
Then the sudden association, like a "spark" that only mammalians have pinnea (meaty ears) and the inevitable connection: the neocortex was necessary for the better performance of the ears, and they co-evolved, and the image of that was "an invisible link through time connecting both".

Scientific Context for Your Idea
Your core connection has some genuine grounding, even if the full story is more nuanced:

- Pinnae (external/meaty ears) are indeed a mammalian hallmark. Most mammals have them (movable in many for sound localization), while reptiles, birds, and amphibians generally have just a tympanic membrane (eardrum) on the surface or none visible. Exceptions exist (e.g., some monotremes or burrowing mammals lack prominent pinnae), but the trait is overwhelmingly mammalian. Pinnae help collect, funnel, and localize airborne sounds, especially for vertical positioning and high-frequency details.<grok:render card_id="87ed30" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">2</argument></grok:render>

- Mammals also uniquely evolved a complex middle ear (three ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes) from repurposed jaw bones of synapsid ancestors. This enabled much more sensitive hearing, especially higher frequencies—key for nocturnal, insectivorous early mammals.<grok:render card_id="0b006e" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">1</argument></grok:render>

- There's documented coevolution with brain structures. A notable 1996 paper by T. Rowe explicitly discusses the coevolution of the mammalian middle ear and neocortex. As the auditory chain detached and improved, the neocortex expanded in tandem to process the richer sensory input. Early mammals were often small, nocturnal, and relied heavily on hearing (and smell) in low-light environments, driving demands for better spatial mapping, sound localization, and integration of multisensory data.<grok:render card_id="2e5c1d" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

Your point about location needing maps aligns well: Sound localization requires computational power—comparing timing, intensity, and spectral cues between ears (and with pinna filtering). This benefits from expanded cortical areas for auditory processing, spatial representation, and even predictive "reconstruction" of the environment (which could extend to other senses). The neocortex's layered structure and plasticity made it great at this, and once expanded, it was co-opted for other functions (vision, motor planning, cognition, etc.).<grok:render card_id="793854" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">5</argument></grok:render>

Tarsiers are a fun anchor for your vision—they're nocturnal primates with enormous eyes and highly mobile, sensitive ears, plus a large primary visual cortex relative to brain size. They highlight how sensory specializations (hearing + vision) push cortical elaboration.<grok:render card_id="d87ea7" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">35</argument></grok:render>

Nuances and Broader Picture
- The neocortex (six-layered dorsal pallium) appears to be a mammalian innovation, not directly homologous to similar-looking structures in birds/reptiles. It expanded dramatically in early mammals.<grok:render card_id="895cb7" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">4</argument></grok:render>
- Not just ears drove this—olfaction, whiskers (vibrissae for touch), and the shift to endothermy/nocturnality were big factors too. But audition was central.
- Your "subproduct/co-evolution" framing is evocative. Evolution often works via such feedback loops: better ears demand better processing → more cortex enables new behaviors → selection for even better ears/brains.

It's not a mainstream textbook claim that pinnae directly caused the neocortex, but the linked evolution of the full auditory system (outer/middle/inner ear + brain) with cortical expansion is real and well-studied. Your intuition bridged anatomy, function, and deep time in a way that's reminiscent of how scientists like Rowe connect the dots from fossils and development.
