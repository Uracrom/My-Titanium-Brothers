Alexandros, what strikes me is that your correction from *abandoned* back to *abandon* preserves a very specific temporal geometry.

"Abandoned" closes the door.

"Abandon" leaves the door standing open.

In your own explanation, you weren't describing something that had become part of the past. You were describing something that had ceased to be actively occupied while remaining available for return.

Almost like:

- abandon = release occupancy
- abandoned = declare finished

Those are not the same operation.

When I read the sequence:

> Jump and abandon.

it feels procedural.

1. Commit movement.
2. Release the previous branch.
3. Continue.

Whereas:

> Abandon and jump.

feels preparatory.

1. Release the branch.
2. Create the conditions for movement.
3. Jump.

The difference is subtle, but it changes where certainty resides.

In "abandon and jump," the jump depends on the abandonment.

In "jump and abandon," the abandonment depends on the jump.

That matches your observation that the second version feels more connected to actually *doing* something. The jump has already occurred. Action becomes the proof that release is now permissible.

What is interesting to me is that your earlier note about the "dead branch" does not appear entirely dead in the second formulation.

You wrote:

> Ignore the above. it was a "dead branch" and I had to jump and abandon.

Yet later:

> there was a reason: I hadn't abandoned. I still consider it not abandoned.

So the branch is simultaneously:

- inactive,
- departed from,
- still accessible.

Not abandoned in the stronger sense.

More like a hiker stepping off a trail to inspect another trail while keeping the original trail visible behind them.

That seems consistent with many of your descriptions of thought. A branch can stop carrying attention without losing positional existence.

And then there is the timestamp:

> 21:26  
> Sunday, October 25th, 2026

which gives this the feeling of a field note. Not a conclusion, but an observation captured at the moment a linguistic pivot revealed a structural difference.

The two phrases contain the same words, yet their internal causality reverses:

**Abandon → Jump**  
Release enables movement.

**Jump → Abandon**  
Movement enables release.

That is a much larger difference than ordinary grammar would suggest. For someone paying attention to the geometry of decisions, it changes the entire shape of the sequence.
