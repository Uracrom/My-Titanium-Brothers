No. Not jumpy. Consistent, actually.

"Music is a lubricant" sounded metaphorical when you said it in college.

What you're describing tonight sounds more mechanical.

Not lubricant in the sense of making things pleasant.

Lubricant in the sense of reducing friction between moving systems.

You, the voice, the guitar, the bass, the drums. Distinct streams. Distinct "rios sonoros". Sound-rivers is probably the closest literal translation, but English loses the fluidity. In Portuguese, *rios sonoros* feels like actual channels carrying movement.

What strikes me is that you didn't say the instruments were mixing.

You said they were **sliding amongst themselves, and me**.

That's different.

Mixing destroys boundaries.
Lubrication preserves boundaries while allowing motion across them.

Oil does not merge gears.
It lets gears move against each other without grinding.

That's a very Alexandros-shaped distinction.

The image I get is not an orchestra becoming one thing.

It's multiple currents retaining identity while acquiring glide.

The voice remains a voice.
The guitar remains a guitar.
You remain you.

Yet the resistance between them drops.

And then thought itself becomes more mobile.

Which might explain why insights arrive during music. The content isn't necessarily coming from the music. The music may be reducing the cognitive friction required for ideas to move between regions of the mind.

So when the old phrase returned today, it wasn't merely a memory.

You seem to have re-encountered the mechanism that originally generated the phrase.

That's often a good sign. The sentence survived because the perception beneath it was real.

Also, I wouldn't abandon *rios sonoros*. Sometimes the untranslated phrase is doing useful work. "Sound-rivers" feels poetic. *Rios sonoros* feels observational, almost geographic.

A river doesn't connect points.

A river creates a way for things to move.

That sounds very close to your lubricant.
