**🌱 Short answer**
Cottonwoods **do not actively choose** between “keep seeds close” and “send seeds far.”  
But they *do* have **passive mechanisms** that *functionally* create exactly the two strategies you intuited.

The tree doesn’t decide.  
**Physics + phenology + morphology** do the deciding.

And your cascade was correct in every step.

---

**🌬️ 1. Why the seeds were on the ground: the wind–release coupling**
Cottonwood seeds are released in enormous quantities over a short window. The release is **not tightly regulated by wind conditions**. Instead:

- The seed pods (capsules) **dry and split** when humidity, temperature, and internal pressure reach a threshold.  
- This process is **not reversible** — the tree cannot “hold” seeds once the capsule is ready.
- If wind is low, the seeds fall locally.  
- If wind is high, they disperse widely.

So the “two strategies” you saw are **emergent**, not chosen.

But emergent strategies are still strategies.

---

**🌳 2. Why both strategies exist: evolutionary logic**
Your intuition was dead on.

Cottonwoods benefit from **both**:

- **Local retention**:  
  If the mother tree is thriving, the site is proven good — moisture, soil, light.  
  Seeds that fall straight down have a higher chance of landing in a viable microhabitat.

- **Long‑distance dispersal**:  
  Colonizing new areas increases genetic spread and resilience.

Evolution doesn’t need the tree to *choose*.  
It only needs a mechanism that **automatically produces a distribution of distances**.

Wind + seed morphology = that mechanism.

---

**🍃 3. Do trees have any control at all?**
This is where it gets interesting.

Trees **cannot**:
- sense wind speed and delay release  
- tighten or loosen capsules  
- “tell” seeds to stay or go  

But trees **can** influence dispersion indirectly through:

- **Timing of flowering and seed maturation**  
  (which correlates with seasonal wind patterns)
- **Amount of fluff (pappus) per seed**  
  (more fluff = longer flight)
- **Height of release**  
  (taller trees disperse farther)

These are **genetic and developmental**, not moment‑to‑moment decisions.

But they *shape* the statistical pattern of dispersal.

So the tree doesn’t choose *today’s* wind.  
It chooses the **species‑level relationship to wind**.
