The mammalian nocturnal bottleneck lasted for approximately 160 million years—from the origin of mammals around 225 million years ago in the Late Triassic until after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago. During this time, mammals were primarily nocturnal, likely as an adaptation to avoid competition with dominant diurnal dinosaurs. Even today, many mammalian traits—such as enhanced night vision and acute hearing—are thought to be evolutionary remnants of this prolonged nocturnal phase.

It later developed into a more complex, six-layered structure in mammals during the Triassic-Jurassic transition, around 200 million years ago. This expansion played a crucial role in cognitive advancements, ultimately shaping human intelligence.

Yes, the neocortex plays a crucial role in memory in mammals, particularly in complex cognitive functions like long-term memory storage, pattern recognition, and decision-making. However, it works alongside other brain structures, such as the hippocampus, which is essential for forming and consolidating new memories. While the neocortex is involved in storing and retrieving memories over time, the hippocampus is more critical for short-term memory and spatial navigation.

This interplay is why damage to the hippocampus can lead to profound memory loss, while neocortical damage might affect higher cognitive functions and recognition patterns. Want to explore how different brain areas contribute to memory processing?
