**“As Baleias” is one of Roberto Carlos’s most direct moral confrontations**: a song that refuses metaphorical escape and instead forces the listener to stand inside the violence of whale hunting. It is protest, lament, and ethical indictment at once. The core of the song is the *look*—the unbearable act of looking into the eyes of what dies in your hands. That is the structural operator of the piece. [1][2]



---

**🐋 What the song *is doing* (structurally)**
- **Moral confrontation** — The opening lines deny the listener the comfort of distance. The frame is intimate, almost accusatory: “olhar nos olhos do que morre em suas mãos. [3]” This is a direct reversal of the usual ecological discourse, which tends to be abstract.   
- **Temporal projection** — The grandchildren asking about the whales is a future‑anchored ethical mirror. [3] It turns ecological destruction into *legacy shame*.   
- **Symbolic inversion** — The “troféu em forma de arpão” is a brilliant inversion: the sy [3]mbol of conquest becomes the symbol of moral failure.   
- **Ecological lament** — The contrast “vermelho derramado / no azul das águas” is a chromatic operator: beauty violated by human vanity.   

---

**🌊 Historical force of the song**
“As Baleias” was released in **1981**, when whale hunting was still widespread. Roberto and Erasmo Carlos wrote it explicitly as a protest song. [2] Four years later, in **1985**, an international agreement suspended commercial whaling. The song became part of the cultural pressure that helped shift public sentiment. 

This is one of the rare cases where a Brazilian pop song intersects directly with global environmental policy.

---

**🎼 What I think of it (my position)**
**It’s one of Roberto Carlos’s most structurally honest songs.**  
He often writes about love, longing, or spirituality, but here he writes about *responsibility*. There is no romantic veil, no metaphorical cushioning. The song is a moral operator disguised as MPB.

Three things stand out to me:

1. **It uses shame as an ethical engine** — not humiliation, but the shame of future judgment.  
2. **It uses imagery as indictment** — the red in the blue is unforgettable.  
3. **It uses compassion as a corrective vector** — “ainda é tempo de ouvir a voz dos ventos” is not sentimental; it’s a structural pivot from destruction to care.

It’s a song that *does not let the listener remain who they were before hearing it*.

Let me first tell you about my relationship with this song.

"Eu saí do Brasil pra ter saudade"

This sentence one day just showed up in my mind, but I hadn't left Brazil yet. I was imagining, or day dreaming, a conversation where I was saying that to someone that would have asked me about going to the States (USA).
I always gave much more importance to the instrumental part of any song. Of course some songs have really good lyrics too. But the ideal is when they have both. And As Baleias does. Back then I was really worried and sad that humanity was such a plague to every living thing on this planet. Nowadays I already accepted - and witnessed - that life diversity and abundance is not going well and is getting worse.
So, the song is awesome in my opinion, but the lyrics is... So on point! The "voice" can't believe that the killer can look into the eyes of the dying beast and feel like a winner. This section of the song brings me an image of a big brown eye that expresses confusion, injustice.
Then the lack of a safe place and the asking for a chance of existence in the future. That is so sad. My nature is adamantly against anything having to ask: the potential "giver" should always offer. No need to ask for any.
Then "change your bearings, look for your feelings". Beautiful. I see the vastness of the ocean, with uncountable possibilities. "Will make you a real winner". That kind of conflicts with my "logic bound" mind, but I see the great potential.
"Your grandchildren will ask you in a few years". I see myself there, dumbfounded, guilty, not knowing what to say. And I remember/imagine seeing rare or extinct animals and lamenting it.
But, amazingly enough, I don't miss Brazil at all. I miss my family and friends, but that's all. I thought I'd miss (ter saudade) Brazil, but I miss the past, the more abundant and rich biodiversity.
