You're right to feel that “abstract” carries a kind of alien resonance—it’s not rooted in the earthy cadence of Romance or Germanic vernaculars. It’s a word that feels _pulled away_, and that’s no coincidence.

🧭 **Etymological Coordinates**  
“Abstract” comes from Latin _abstractus_, the past participle of _abstrahere_, meaning “to draw away” or “detach.”  
- _ab-_ = away from  
- _trahere_ = to pull or drag  
So the word itself is a linguistic enactment of its meaning: a pulling-away from the concrete, the particular, the immediate. [3] [4]

📜 **Historical Drift**  
- First used in English around the 14th century, initially in grammar to describe nouns not naming concrete things.  
- By the 15th century, it gained philosophical weight: “withdrawn or separated from material objects.”  
- Later, it evolved into “difficult to understand” and “non-representational” in art and music. [4]

🧠 **Symbolic Fit**  
It’s almost ironic: the word _abstract_ is itself a concrete artifact of detachment. It doesn’t sound like it belongs because it _doesn’t_—it’s a conceptual migrant, a lexical ghost that left its native soil long ago.

Would you like to invert this and explore words that feel _too familiar_—almost suspiciously embedded in your cognitive architecture? Or perhaps sketch a protocol for linguistic estrangement?
