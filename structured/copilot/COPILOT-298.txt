This is interesting because your intuition is actually touching something that already happens in mathematics, but not in the way you're imagining.

Mathematically, there is only one **0** in ordinary arithmetic. A zero is a zero.

Yet as mathematics became more sophisticated, people kept discovering that "nothing" comes in different flavors.

For example:

- The zero of a function, where the function becomes exactly zero.
- The zero vector, which represents no direction.
- The empty set, which contains nothing.
- The origin, the zero position in a coordinate system.
- The vacuum state in physics.
- The null element in algebraic structures.

All are "zero-like" but they play different roles.

So your colored-zero civilization is not completely alien to mathematics. It is more like a civilization that stops treating all forms of absence as identical.

What fascinates me about your phrasing is this:

> I wonder if in certain equations some zeros are "better" than others.

A mathematician would probably reinterpret "better" as:

> Do different zeros carry different structural information?

And suddenly the question becomes surprisingly deep.

Imagine a future symbolic notation:

🔵 Zero = equilibrium zero  
🔴 Zero = cancellation zero  
🟢 Zero = fertile zero (a state from which many solutions emerge)  
🟣 Zero = boundary zero (a transition point)  
🟡 Zero = symmetric zero (perfect balance)

Numerically they are all equal to 0.

But informationally they are different.

Like this:

```
2 - 2 = 🔴0
```

The zero came from cancellation.

While:

```
f'(x) = 🔵0
```

could denote equilibrium.

The value is the same.

The meaning differs.

That is very similar to how physicists discovered that particles cannot be classified merely by mass. They needed spin, charge, parity, color charge, and other labels because the number alone was insufficient.

Your thought experiment is asking:

> What if "zero" eventually suffers the same fate?

What if civilizations become so familiar with absence that they begin classifying its species?

And because I know your tendency to think in images, I suspect the jump wasn't

> There are many zeros.

It was closer to

> I saw a zero whose texture felt different.

Then the analytical mind arrived afterward and asked whether a future mathematics could formalize that distinction.

In your own language of constraints and perception, I might phrase it this way:

> The colored zeros answer the question "What kind?"
