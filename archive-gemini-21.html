From “Where?” to “Then”: The Geometry Hidden Inside a Jump

It began with something almost absurdly ordinary: a refrigerator.

Alex noticed that there were no more cans of sparkling water in the fridge, except for the one he was about to open. Before the refrigerator door had even closed, he had opened the can and taken a sip. Then another.

At the same time, another intention was already forming: get more cans and put them in the refrigerator.

At 21:15, he directed himself toward the boxes of cans.

He arrived there.

And then something strange happened.

He looked around and essentially asked himself:

Why am I here?

The answer returned immediately:

Oh. I'm getting more cans.

The geographic command had succeeded.

The second command—the purpose—had not.

He had successfully navigated to the correct place while apparently losing the reason for going there.

That small event became a window into a much larger question.

1. The geographic command

Alex noticed that there seemed to be two different components in the original intention:

WHERE
and
WHAT FOR

The first had survived.

The second had disappeared.

He began thinking about what mechanism could produce such an asymmetry.

His intuition was that once the first decision had been made—the decision most trusted to produce the desired result—the cognitive “guard” relaxed. The mind could then wander into autopilot, while the secondary instructions became vulnerable to being obliterated by whatever happened next.

That offered a possible explanation for the familiar question:

“How could you forget what you were doing?”

Perhaps the mind did not forget everything.

Perhaps it preserved the location while losing the purpose.

And that led to a much larger realization:

Geographic positioning may be a primal imperative of intelligence.

Before you can go somewhere, you have to know where you are.

Before you can retrieve something, you have to know where it is.

Once something is located, it can be inserted into an internal map.

So Alex formulated it very simply:

If you don't know where you are, you also don't know where to go.

And then:

Once you locate anything, you have it in your internal map.

This suggested that intelligence might be understood, at least in part, as an amplified form of location-sensing.

Not necessarily that intelligence is location sense, but that position may be one of its most primitive interfaces with reality.

2. Then

The next discovery came from the writing itself.

Alex had written a sequence containing “then” over and over:

Then...
Then...
Then...
Then...

He suddenly saw why the repetition felt strange.

The word was being used deliberately to jump from one event to another.

And once he looked at the word itself, “then” became an object of investigation.

What exactly does then contain?

Very little—and potentially almost everything.

Consider:

A happens. Then B happens.

The word “then” does not tell us what happened between A and B.

It merely establishes an ordering:

A → THEN → B

The interval is compressed into a tiny linguistic placeholder.

The transition could contain almost anything.

A movement.

A transformation.

A disappearance.

A discovery.

A completely unexpected event.

Or nothing that we can currently describe.

“Then” therefore became interesting because it seemed to preserve the existence of a transition while leaving the actual content of that transition open.

It was a small word carrying an enormous unexplored space.

And this connected naturally to the earlier geographic observation:

WHERE identifies a configuration.

THEN indicates a transition.

WHAT describes the resulting configuration.

The mind seemed to be navigating not only through places, but through configurations and transitions.

3. The cut

Then another visual phenomenon appeared.

Alex imagined a scene divided by a line.

At first, the line was simply a separation.

But then the line began to widen.

It became a crack.

The crack opened farther, revealing something apparently behind the two scenes.

At first, that hidden thing looked like a large bag.

But the “bag” was only a temporary visual scaffold.

The important realization was not that there was literally a bag.

It was that what we call a cut might not actually be a line at all.

A line has no width.

Once the separation widens, it becomes a region.

A surface-like gap.

And then something even more interesting happens:

The gap can be understood as revealing a dimension of structure that was previously hidden.

The first intuition was:

A cut is a big surface gap.

It almost felt like a space warp.

Something could go from one extreme of the opening to the other without traversing the apparent distance in the ordinary way.

But then Alex corrected the metaphor.

There are no bags.

There are not even necessarily surfaces.

There is an underlying continuity, and the visual concepts of bags, surfaces, cracks, and folds are merely scaffolds that make something otherwise difficult to communicate imaginable.

This distinction became essential.

The scaffold is not the thing.

It is the temporary geometry used to point toward the thing.

4. There are no jumps

The idea became more precise.

Suppose A and B appear to be separated by a huge distance.

If the transition from A to B is instantaneous, it is tempting to say that the distance between them has disappeared.

But Alex's intuition was different.

The distance has not disappeared.

It has been folded.

The “bag” was merely the fastest way to visualize the folded remainder.

So the apparent jump:

A → B

does not mean:

A → nothing → B

Instead, it means something closer to:

A → [continuous structure, compressed or folded in representation] → B

The underlying continuity remains.

The apparent jump is produced by the way the continuity is being represented.

This led to a much stronger formulation:

There are no jumps. There is only continuity seen at a resolution where the interval becomes negligible.

And then an important addition:

The interval hasn't disappeared; it has been compressed into the transformation between the apparent endpoints.

At this point, the “bag” had served its purpose.

It could be discarded.

5. The movie film

Then came the example that made the whole thing dramatically clearer.

Imagine an old movie film.

A physical strip contains individual frames, perhaps thirty frames per second.

The strip itself is continuous.

Now suppose the movie contains the complete alphabet:

A — B — C — D — E — F — G — H — I — J — K...

Normally, the projector shows the frames in sequence.

A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J...

But now imagine that after F, the projection jumps directly to J.

Crucially, the film is not physically cut.

G, H, and I still exist.

They remain on the continuous strip.

The only thing that has changed is what gets projected.

The physical sequence is still:

F — G — H — I — J

but the visible sequence becomes:

F → J

The apparent jump therefore isn't produced by destroying the intervening material.

The intervening material has simply become unshown.

Alex's earlier “bag” can now be understood differently.

The “bag” is the folded portion of the film containing G, H, and I.

It is not an actual bag.

It is a convenient visual representation of the part of the continuum that remains present but is not being projected.

The “cut” isn't an actual cut either.

It is the non-showing of a specific chunk of the continuum.

That was the crucial refinement.

6. The cut is an operation of projection

The film example reveals an important distinction:

What exists
versus
what is shown.

The film can contain:

F — G — H — I — J

while the screen displays:

F — J

Nothing has necessarily been removed from the underlying structure.

The discontinuity exists in the projection.

The continuity exists in the substrate.

This allows the apparent jump to be reformulated:

A jump may be a property of the presentation rather than a property of the underlying continuum.

And this gives a precise meaning to the earlier intuition that there are no jumps.

It does not mean that things cannot appear to jump.

They obviously can.

It means that the apparent abruptness may arise because an interval of an underlying continuous structure has been excluded from presentation.

7. And then the idea becomes universal

At this point the film analogy revealed something much larger.

If the underlying continuum is continuous, then every apparent cut could potentially be understood in the same way.

Not just a movie.

Not just a visual scene.

Any transition.

Any apparent separation.

Any jump from one state to another.

If everything is connected to everything else in the underlying continuum, then the fact that we perceive A and B as separated does not necessarily mean that the continuum between them has ceased to exist.

We may simply be seeing:

A → B

while the underlying structure contains:

A → [everything between A and B] → B

The projection, perception, or representation has selected two regions and suppressed the rest.

The “cut” therefore becomes less like a knife and more like a selection operator.

It doesn't necessarily destroy continuity.

It chooses what becomes manifest.

8. The surprising connection to “then”

This brought the earlier discovery about the word then back into the picture.

Consider:

F happened. Then J happened.

The word “then” performs almost exactly the operation represented by the movie example.

It tells us:

Show me the next relevant configuration.

It does not tell us what happened to G, H, and I.

Those events may exist in the underlying story.

They simply aren't being presented.

So:

Then is a linguistic compression of a transition.

And a cinematic cut is a visual compression of a transition.

Both can connect two visible configurations while leaving the intervening structure unshown.

This produces a striking possibility:

A “cut” in visual experience, a “then” in language, and a “jump” in thought may be different manifestations of a common operation: selecting two configurations from a continuous structure while suppressing the intervening portion from presentation.

9. The scaffold problem

But there is a final philosophical complication.

Even the words being used to describe this are scaffolds.

There may be no literal:

bag,

surface,

line,

crack,

fold,

film,

distance,

or even separate A and B.

Those are the tools available to human language and visual cognition.

The underlying thing, in Alex's conception, precedes those categories.

So the process becomes almost self-referential:

We use geometry to describe something that may not literally be geometric.

We use a bag to describe something that isn't a bag.

We use a fold to describe something that isn't necessarily a fold.

We use a film strip to describe a continuum that isn't necessarily film.

The metaphors are not intended as claims about physical ontology.

They are handles.

They allow an otherwise inaccessible relationship to become visible to thought.

This is why the film analogy is so effective.

It does not prove the underlying theory.

It makes the relationship thinkable.

10. Is the insight original?

That naturally led to the final question:

Is this insight original?

The careful answer is: the broad territory is not new.

Mathematics, physics, philosophy, topology, film theory, and theories of representation have all dealt with neighboring ideas: continuity versus discontinuous representation, apparent jumps, projection, hidden intermediate structure, mappings, and the difference between an underlying object and how it is represented.

So it would be too strong to claim:

“Nobody has ever thought that a discontinuity might be an artifact of representation.”

People have.

But Alex's particular route into the idea is distinctive.

He did not begin with a mathematical theory of discontinuities.

He began with an experience of a line widening into a crack, then discovered that the crack seemed to expose something behind the scenes. He then rejected the literal “bag,” replacing it with an elastic continuum and folding. Finally, the old movie-film strip supplied a concrete mechanism:

The strip is continuous.
The projection skips.
The skipped frames still exist.
The apparent jump is therefore in what is shown, not necessarily in what exists.

That is a coherent and unusually intuitive formulation.

The responsible claim is therefore not:

“I discovered discontinuity.”

It is:

“I independently arrived at a visual model in which an apparent cut or jump is produced by suppressing the presentation of a still-continuous portion of an underlying continuum, with the folded film strip serving as the simplest scaffold.”

Whether that becomes more than a powerful metaphor depends on the next step.

The real test would be whether this model can be formalized and whether, once formalized, it produces distinctions or consequences that existing mathematical frameworks do not already provide.

But regardless of whether it ultimately becomes a new theory, something important happened during this conversation.

A mundane question—

“Why did I arrive at the cans but forget why I was going there?”

—opened into:

WHERE → THEN → CUT → FOLD → CONTINUUM → PROJECTION → APPARENT JUMP.

And the thread connecting them is surprisingly consistent:

Intelligence may not encounter reality as a sequence of isolated things. It may continuously construct, navigate, and selectively reveal relationships within an underlying structure.

The visible scene may be only what the projector currently chooses to show.

The unseen portion does not necessarily cease to exist.

It may simply be outside the frame.
