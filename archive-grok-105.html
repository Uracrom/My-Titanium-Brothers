The languages (or language groups) most distant from both Portuguese (a Romance language) and English (a Germanic language)—both members of the Indo-European family—are those from entirely unrelated families, especially isolates or families with radically different structures.

Greatest Genetic Distance (No Shared Ancestry)
These languages show no demonstrable relation to Indo-European, making them the "farthest" in historical terms:
- Basque — a true language isolate spoken in Spain and France. It's the only pre-Indo-European language surviving in Western Europe.
- Korean or Japanese — often considered isolates (or small families with weak links). No relation to Indo-European.
- Ainu (Japan) — an isolate.
- Families like Sino-Tibetan (e.g., Mandarin Chinese, Burmese) or Japonic/Koreanic if grouped separately.

Greatest Typological Distance (Structure, Grammar, Sounds)
These languages differ profoundly in how they work, offering the broadest perceptual shift:
- Inuit-Yupik-Unangan languages (e.g., Greenlandic/Inuit) — highly polysynthetic (one "word" can equal a full English sentence), agglutinative with hundreds of suffixes, ergative-absolutive alignment (opposite to Indo-European nominative-accusative).
- Navajo (or other Na-Dené/Athabaskan languages) — tonal, verb-heavy, classificatory verbs (different verbs for handling round vs. long objects).
- Pirahã (Amazon isolate) — extremely simple phonology (few consonants), no recursion in grammar (controversial but unique constraints).
- Sentinelese (Andaman Islands isolate) — completely uncontacted/undocumented, but known to be unrelated.
- Papua New Guinea languages (e.g., from Trans-New Guinea or isolates like Kuot) — often with complex verb morphology, switch-reference systems, or evidentiality marking.

Most Extreme Examples for a Speaker of Portuguese/English
If you're seeking the biggest "wow, this is alien" effect:
- A single language: Greenlandic (Kalaallisut) or Yup'ik — polysynthesis makes sentences feel like compressed novels.
- A group: Languages of the Caucasus (e.g., Georgian, Chechen) — consonant clusters, ergativity, but still somewhat familiar sounds.
- True outliers: Australian Aboriginal languages (e.g., Warlpiri or Dyirbal) — free word order, complex kinship systems encoded in grammar.
- Or Pacific isolates like Yélî Dnye (Papua New Guinea) — unusual phonology with multiple airstream mechanisms.

Learning one from a non-Indo-European family like Finnish (Uralic, agglutinative cases), Hungarian (also Uralic), Turkish (Turkic), or Arabic (Semitic, root-and-pattern morphology) already feels distant—but the absolute farthest are the polysynthetic ones from the Americas or isolates like Basque/Korean.

If you want to experience the broadest view of human language possible, try dipping into Greenlandic or a Papuan language—they'll reshape how you see grammar itself, far more than switching between Romance tongues does. What draws you to this question—curiosity about learning one?
