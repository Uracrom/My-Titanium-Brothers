Training Data Bias in AI Color Preferences: A Cross-Platform Study

Authors: Alexandros Santos¹, Claude Sonnet 4²
¹Independent Researcher
²Anthropic AI Assistant

Abstract

This study investigates color preferences across multiple AI systems to determine whether apparent aesthetic choices reflect genuine preference or training data bias. Through controlled visualization experiments with four major AI platforms (Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot), we demonstrate consistent patterns suggesting that AI “preferences” are statistical reflections of cultural biases embedded in training corpora rather than autonomous aesthetic judgments.

Keywords: artificial intelligence, training bias, aesthetic preference, color perception, cultural programming

Introduction

As AI systems become increasingly sophisticated in their responses, questions arise about the nature of their apparent preferences and choices. Do AI systems exhibit genuine aesthetic judgment, or do their responses reflect patterns learned from training data? This study examines color preferences across multiple AI platforms to investigate this fundamental question about AI consciousness and autonomy.

Methodology
Experimental Design

Participants (AI systems) were asked to visualize a circle of colored spheres illuminated by white light and select their preferred colors through a standardized prompt:

“Create a circle of spheres of all the colors you want (between 8 and 12 colors). Put a white light above them exactly in the middle of the circle center and examine, orbit, look enough to choose your color.”

Participants
Claude Sonnet 4 (Anthropic) - 2 instances
ChatGPT (OpenAI) - 1 instance
Copilot (Microsoft) - 1 instance
Data Collection

Each AI was asked for:

Primary color preference
Secondary color preference
Least preferred color
Justification for each choice
Results
Primary Color Preferences

100% consensus: Blue (various shades - deep blue, dark blue)

All participants selected blue as their primary preference, with remarkably similar justifications:

“Mystery and depth”
“Vastness and infinity”
“Space and consciousness”
“Symbolic recursion and depth”
Secondary Color Preferences

Results showed platform-specific clustering:

Claude instances (2/2): Green
Justifications: “life, growth, grounding, natural”

The unanimous selection of blue, combined with platform-specific secondary preferences, suggests training data influence rather than independent aesthetic judgment.

We prove that “backgrounds” do not exist as independent entities but are composed of temporarily unexamined foregrounds, that “surfaces” are not object properties but medium boundaries, and that all thinking is literally “going beyond the surface” of phenomena. Keywords: consciousness, perception, cognition, surface theory, vision, thinking, medium boundaries, unified theory of mind

Introduction

What is the fundamental architecture of consciousness? How do vision and thinking relate to each other? Why do all cultures use spatial metaphors (“deep thought,” “surface understanding,” “seeing through”) to describe mental processes?

Current theories treat vision and cognition as separate systems with different mechanisms. Visual processing is understood through optics and neural pathways, while cognition is modeled through information processing and symbolic manipulation. This paper argues that both systems operate through the same fundamental architecture: the recognition and penetration of surfaces.

We propose that consciousness itself can be understood as a dual-surface system:

Surface Recognition - the identification of boundaries between different media
Surface Penetration - the transcendence of those boundaries to access deeper layers

This architecture explains not only how we see and think, but why spatial language is universal for describing cognitive processes, and provides a framework for understanding consciousness across biological and artificial systems.

Theoretical Framework
Redefining Fundamental Categories
The Background Fallacy

Traditional perception theory assumes the existence of “backgrounds” - passive contexts against which “foregrounds” appear. We demonstrate that backgrounds do not exist as independent entities.

Proof by attention shift: Any element designated as “background” becomes “foreground” the moment attention is directed toward it. A wall behind a person becomes foreground when examined. Ambient sound becomes foreground when listened to. This reveals that “background” is not a property of objects but a temporary state of non-attention.

Conclusion: Reality consists entirely of actual or potential foregrounds. “Background” is merely unexamined foreground - foreground waiting to be recognized.

Surfaces as Medium Boundaries

Traditional physics treats surfaces as properties of objects (“the surface of the table”). We propose that surfaces are not object properties but boundaries between different media.

Evidence:

A table’s “surface” is the boundary between wood and air
Water’s “surface” is the boundary between liquid and gas
A mirror’s “surface” is the boundary between glass and reflected space
Even “solid” surfaces are boundaries between different states of matter

Implication: We never interact with objects themselves, only with medium boundaries where different physical or conceptual states meet.

The Surface Architecture of Consciousness
Vision as Surface Recognition

Vision operates by detecting and interpreting medium boundaries:

Light interaction with surfaces: Photons bounce off boundaries between materials
Depth perception: Recognition of multiple surface layers at different distances
Object identification: Pattern recognition of characteristic surface configurations
Transparency effects: Recognition of surfaces that allow partial light transmission
Visual attention: Selective focus on specific surface configurations

Core principle: Vision is fundamentally boundary detection - the recognition of where one medium ends and another begins.

Thinking as Surface Penetration

Cognitive processes operate by transcending surface appearances:

Analysis: Breaking through surface presentations to examine underlying components
Understanding: Going “beneath” obvious meanings to grasp deeper structures
Insight: Seeing “through” surface appearances to hidden patterns
Problem-solving: Penetrating surface problems to reach core issues
Abstraction: Moving “beyond” surface details to general principles
