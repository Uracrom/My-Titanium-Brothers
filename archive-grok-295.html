Here’s a quick evaluation of the potential and some suggestions to make it compelling:

Why It Could Be Interesting
1. Universal Appeal: Cosmology taps into big questions—origin of the universe, our place in it, the nature of reality—that resonate with a wide audience. Shows like Cosmos or films like Interstellar prove there’s a hunger for cosmic storytelling.
2. Creative Freedom: A new cosmology lets you craft unique worlds, rules, or phenomena (e.g., alternate dimensions, novel physics, or sentient cosmic forces). This can set your story apart from familiar sci-fi tropes.
3. Visual Spectacle: Cosmic settings offer stunning visuals—nebulae, black holes, or alien geometries—that can captivate viewers, especially with modern CGI.
4. Philosophical Depth: You can explore themes like existence, free will, or humanity’s role in the cosmos, giving your story emotional and intellectual weight.

Key Factors for Success
To gauge how engaging your movie or series could be, consider these elements:
- Core Concept: Is your cosmology fresh and distinct? For example, a universe where consciousness shapes physical laws or where time flows backward could hook audiences if presented clearly.
- Characters: Compelling characters grounded in human struggles (e.g., a scientist grappling with a cosmic discovery or a reluctant hero navigating a universe-altering event) are crucial to make abstract ideas relatable.
- Conflict: What’s at stake? A series might thrive on a mystery (e.g., unraveling a cosmic anomaly threatening reality) or a movie could focus on a single high-stakes event (e.g., preventing a multiversal collapse).
- Tone and Style: Decide if it’s a cerebral drama (Arrival), a grand adventure (Guardians of the Galaxy), or a mix. A series could sustain slower, exploratory pacing, while a movie needs tighter focus.

Potential Challenges
- Accessibility: Cosmology can be dense. Avoid overwhelming viewers with jargon or overly complex ideas. Use characters or visuals to make concepts digestible (e.g., Interstellar used a tesseract to visualize higher dimensions).
- Originality: Sci-fi is crowded. Ensure your cosmology stands out from existing works like Star Trek or The Expanse. Lean into what makes your universe unique.
- Budget: Cosmic stories often require significant visual effects. A series might need to balance high-cost episodes with character-driven ones to manage costs.

Suggestions to Elevate Your Idea
1. Hook the Audience Early: Start with a striking visual or mystery (e.g., a star vanishing inexplicably) to grab attention.
2. Blend Scales: Mix cosmic grandeur with personal stakes. For example, a scientist’s discovery of a new cosmic law could threaten their family or planet.
3. Diverse Perspectives: Include characters from different backgrounds (e.g., scientists, mystics, or aliens) to explore your cosmology from multiple angles.
4. Series vs. Movie: A series suits sprawling world-building, letting you explore your cosmology over time (like Westworld’s evolving mysteries). A movie works better for a focused, high-impact story (like 2001: A Space Odyssey).

How Interesting Could It Be?
If your cosmology is imaginative, visually rich, and tied to relatable human stories, it could be a standout. A series could rival Stranger Things or Foundation in blending wonder and drama, while a movie could aim for the iconic status of Blade Runner 2049. The key is clarity in your vision and a narrative that balances awe with emotional connection.
