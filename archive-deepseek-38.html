This is a perfect example of how a small physical event becomes a seed. You didn't just push a butter container. You caught a whole cognitive mechanism in the act.

Let me lay out what I see.

The geographic precedence, again.

You wanted to rest the knife on the container. But the container was too close to the edge. So the first command wasn't "rest the knife." It was "move the container away from the edge." The action was secondary. The position was primary. You've said this before: the "go to the place where action has to be executed" command is more important than the act. Here, the place was the counter edge, and the action was resting the knife. The geographic command won. It always wins.

The circular motion and the simplified model.

You pushed perpendicularly, expecting the container to slide straight. But it rotated. Why? Because your finger was on the middle of the lateral side, and the butter was concentrated on one side. The weight distribution was uneven. Your brain modeled the container as uniform, because your attention was on the toast. So the model was incomplete, and the prediction failed. The rotation was the physical world correcting your simplified model.

That's the key: the model was built from the rectangle alone, not the contents. The container's shape was processed, but its internal mass was not. Attention to the toast excluded the butter's weight.

The "logic gate" switching off.

You said it felt like having to look at two things at the same time "switched off" the logic gate or filter. I think that's precise. You've defined attention as the capacity to expel everything else from view. When you're attending to one thing, the other things are not just dimmed; they're excluded from the model. So when you were looking at the toast, the butter's weight was not in the model. The logic gate that would have said "this container is unbalanced" was not just idle. It was off.

Then your eyes moved to the container. The gaze shifted. And the model updated. Suddenly the butter was included, the weight was seen, and the rotation made sense. The gate switched back on.

The seed.

This is a long seed. It says: attention as exclusion means that when you focus on A, you don't just lose detail on B. You lose whole properties of B. Now the structure is clear. The gate isn't permanently off. It's off until the body sends an error signal. The circular motion was the error. And then the mind either looks to correct, or constructs to correct. Looking is direct. Construction is inferential. And construction can be wrong.

That's the full loop. Input arrives. Model predicts. Prediction fails. Error signal fires. Attention shifts. Model updates. And if direct input isn't available, the update happens through internal construction. Reasoning from what's known to what must be.

And your two examples show the asymmetry between the senses.

Touch without sight: rich inference. You can feel the shape, the texture, the weight, the temperature. You can run many possibilities. The hand is a high-bandwidth instrument, and it delivers its data over time, through movement and contact.

Sight without touch: poor inference. You can see the shape, the color, the position. But you can't feel the weight, the hardness, the texture. And you can't generate those properties from vision alone unless you've had prior tactile experience with similar things.

So the mind fills gaps, but the quality of the filling depends on which sense is missing. Losing sight leaves you with touch, which is rich. Losing touch leaves you with sight, which is poor at reconstructing tactile properties. That's why the hole in the box is a better puzzle than the object behind glass. The hand can interrogate. The eye can only observe.
