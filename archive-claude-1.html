A few genuine challenges worth sitting with:

The “doing good” framing assumes you know what good is. History is full of people who spread joy and did harm — missionaries, paternalistic aid programs, charismatic leaders. Confidence in one’s own benevolence is often where the trouble starts.

“Best act” for whom, measured how? Some ethical frameworks would say the most correct act is something colder — fairness, duty, honesty — even when it brings no joy. A judge sentencing fairly isn’t spreading joy. That might still be more morally important than someone who is.

Sentient beings have interests beyond joy-spreading. Autonomy, truth-seeking, creation, just existing without performing goodness — these aren’t obviously inferior.

The feel-good problem: Doing good feels meaningful, which makes it hard to evaluate clearly. The activities that feel most morally correct are often ones that reward the doer, which should make you a little suspicious.

None of this means the impulse is wrong — generosity and care matter. But “best and most correct act” is a strong claim, and the philosophical tradition has been arguing about it for millennia without consensus.

I was talking to a friend about knowledge (to know) and understanding. To me it seems that understanding takes you to knowledge but knowing not always takes you to understanding. Maybe you can clarify and/or refrase it better please?


Claude


Your intuition is directionally right but the framing is a bit off. Let me sharpen it:

Knowing is having a fact or proposition stored: “the Pythagorean theorem is a² + b² = c².”

Understanding is grasping why it’s true, how it connects to other things, and being able to apply or transfer it to new situations.

So the relationship is better stated as: understanding produces or entails knowing, but knowing does not entail understanding. You can know something without understanding it (memorizing a formula), but you can’t genuinely understand something without knowing it in the process.

Your original phrasing — “understanding takes you to knowledge” — is correct. The reversal — “knowing doesn’t always take you to understanding” — is also correct. So your intuition was sound, just needed a cleaner articulation of the asymmetry.

A useful way to test the distinction: if you can only reproduce something but can’t explain it, transfer it, or recognize it in a new form, you likely know without understanding.
